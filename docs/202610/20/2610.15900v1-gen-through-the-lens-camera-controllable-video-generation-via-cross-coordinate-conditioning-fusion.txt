## Abstract 

We present Gen Through the Lens, a camera-controllable image-to-video generation framework that synthesizes videos from a given reference image while enabling realistic and physically consistent camera operations. Unlike previous models that treat camera mo-tion as abstract conditioning or rely on a single coordinate system, our approach explicitly decomposes and fuses control signals across three coordinate spaces: the camera coordinate (modeled by intrin-sic parameters), the world coordinate (represented by spatial geom-etry hints), and the object coordinate (described by 3D bounding boxes of target subjects). Through cross-coordinate conditioning fusion, these heterogeneous cues are unified to guide the genera-tive process, enabling precise control of camera motion, focus, and framing while preserving temporal and spatial coherence. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method produces controllable and visually consistent results across diverse camera operations, outperforming existing approaches in both realism and editability. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in video generation have achieved impressive visual realism, yet providing precise and physically meaningful control over camera behavior remains a fundamental challenge.

Most existing generative models either treat camera motion as an implicit byproduct of appearance statistics or encode it using low-level signals such as optical flow or relative pose embeddings, making it difficult to specify complex camera operations such as metric-scale motion, depth-of-field variation, or coupled changes in camera intrinsics and extrinsics. A key difficulty lies in the heteroge-neous nature of camera-related information, which simultaneously involves intrinsic parameters (e.g., focal length and distortion), ex-trinsic motion in 3D space, and interactions with scene geometry and object motion. Collapsing these factors into a single coordinate system or abstract latent codes obscures their physical meaning and limits controllability, particularly for advanced cinematic effects such as depth-of-field control or the Hitchcock zoom [10]. In this work, we present Gen Through the Lens (GTL), an image-to-video generation framework that enables explicit and physically grounded control over camera motion, object motion, and depth-of-field by decomposing and fusing camera-related sig-nals across multiple coordinate spaces. Our approach introduces a cross-coordinate conditioning formulation that jointly models (i) the camera coordinate , capturing intrinsic parameters and lens ef-fects, (ii) the world coordinate , encoding metric-scale camera motion and spatial geometry, and (iii) the object coordinate , representing object motion through 6D bounding boxes parameterized by 3D arXiv:2610.15900v1 [cs.CV] 11 Oct 2026 Li et al. 

translation and 3D rotation. By preserving the physical meaning of each coordinate space and explicitly fusing them during genera-tion, our framework enables precise and interpretable control over camera motion, focus, and framing. Building upon this formulation, we design three complementary conditioning signals corresponding to the three coordinate spaces: (1) a camera motion condition , which visualizes metric-scale camera extrinsics and intrinsics in the world coordinate space and enables explicit control over camera translation, rotation, and zoom; (2) an 

object motion condition , which represents foreground dynamics us-ing 3D bounding boxes with 6D pose, thereby enabling disentangled control over object translation and rotation; and (3) a depth-of-field condition , which encodes focus depth and defocus behavior using layered depth cues, allowing explicit and time-varying control of bokeh effects. These conditions are geometrically consistent and mutually complementary, jointly enabling controllable video syn-thesis with coherent camera motion, stable object dynamics, and physically meaningful depth-of-field behavior. However, effectively leveraging physically grounded conditions requires accurate and metrically consistent camera supervision, which is difficult to obtain from unconstrained videos due to scale ambiguity, temporal instability, and intrinsic–extrinsic inconsisten-cies. To address this challenge, we introduce a metric-scale dataset labeling pipeline that combines relative camera pose estimation with monocular metric depth and robust scale recovery, construct-ing a unified metric camera space for reliable camera-controllable supervision. Extensive quantitative evaluations demonstrate that our method significantly improves camera controllability, geometric consis-tency, and motion stability compared to state-of-the-art approaches. In addition, qualitative results showcase compelling cinematic ap-plications, including controllable depth-of-field effects and stable synthesis of the Hitchcock zoom. Together, these results highlight the importance of explicit, cross-coordinate camera modeling for controllable and physically consistent video generation. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Camera-Controllable Video Generation 

Camera motion control in generative video has evolved from coarse motion cues to explicit trajectory conditioning. Early text-based ap-proaches such as AnimateDiff [7] and MotionDirector [42] typically fine-tune LoRA modules for a small set of motion patterns, pro-viding convenient but imprecise control. Recent trajectory-guided methods instead condition video diffusion on camera parameters. MotionCtrl [36] conditions generation on raw camera parameters repeated over the spatial grid, while CameraCtrl [8] and CamCo [38] encode camera parameters as pixel-wise Plücker ray embeddings to obtain more discriminative viewpoint control. Several works lift the reference image to 3D and render proxy views for conditioning, such as ViewCrafter [40] and I2VControl-Camera [5], improving spatial coherence under viewpoint changes. To better handle long-range motion and cross-frame geometry, CamI2V [44] introduces epipolar attention mechanisms, and RealCam-I2V [15] further tar-gets real-world usability by aligning trajectories to metric scale. Beyond pure conditioning, GEN3C [23] strengthens controllability by maintaining a 3D cache rendered along user-specified trajecto-ries, which also improves temporal 3D consistency. Despite these advances, most trajectory-based controllers still rely on approxi-mate geometry or relative-scale supervision and treat the camera as a simplified projection model, which can lead to scale drift, limited robustness under large rotations or occlusions, and weak support for optical phenomena beyond pose changes. 

## 2.2 Object-Controllable Video Generation 

Object control in generative video has progressed from coarse re-gion constraints to explicit trajectory-driven motion specification. Early approaches primarily rely on spatial conditions to anchor ob-ject placement but provide limited control over object dynamics. Re-cent works instead adopt drag-style interaction, where sparse point trajectories convey user intent and are injected into diffusion mod-els through explicit motion representations. DragNUWA [39] in-troduces joint text-image-trajectory conditioning for open-domain object manipulation, while ImageConductor [16] decouples camera and object motions via separate LoRA branches for more precise interactive control. Many methods use optical flow as an interme-diate representation: sparse trajectories are converted to sparse or dense flow to guide temporal attention or feature warping, as in Motion-I2V [26] and MOFA-Video [20]. Beyond flow, Tora [41] encodes trajectories into a trajectory map and integrates them into a scalable diffusion-transformer pipeline via a trajectory ex-tractor and fusion module, and DragAnything [37] uses an entity representation with mask-based feature reorganization to improve object-level coherence under dragging. Despite improved control-lability, these methods remain sensitive to trajectory placement and depth or occlusion ambiguities, can introduce local distortions under large shape variations, and often suffer from control con-flicts when object motion and global camera motion are specified simultaneously. 

## 2.3 Collaborative Motion Control 

Collaborative motion control targets video generation with simul-taneous camera motion and local object dynamics, where naive 2D motion controls often lead to local conflicts under complex camera movements. Early approaches unify camera and object motion in a single 2D representation. AnimateAnything [14] converts both mo-tions into a unified optical-flow field, but entangles global camera-induced displacement with object-centric motion, limiting geomet-ric interpretability. Subsequent works adopt point-based motion control derived from 2D perception signals. MotionPrompting [6] and I2VControl-Camera [5] leverage dense point embeddings to guide motion generation, but collaborative control remains rooted in 2D cues and is typically specified at region or segmentation-mask granularity, restricting fine-grained camera–object interac-tion modeling. Perception-as-Control [2] introduces a 3D-aware motion representation with separate encoders for camera and object control to reduce local conflicts. Despite improved motion harmony, collaborative approaches still depend on upstream perception qual-ity, simplified 3D surrogates may miss motion factors, and overall controllability remains constrained by the underlying generator. ## 3 Method 

In this section, we present a metric-scale supervision and condi-tioning pipeline that first recovers semantic, camera, object, and depth-related supervision from real-world videos, and then con-structs geometry-aware conditioning signals for controllable video generation. Together, these components enable disentangled yet coherent control over camera motion, object motion, depth-of-field, and zoom. The overall data processing workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2. Detailed implementation of each component is provided in Appendix Sec. A. 

## 3.1 Label Prompt 

We use InternVL2.5 [3] to generate dense textual descriptions for each video, which serve as semantic supervision during training. To avoid information leakage and ensure disentanglement between camera control and content description, we explicitly constrain the prompt to exclude camera-related information, such as camera motion, viewpoint, and shooting angles. The prompt is designed to encourage temporally coherent, detailed, and factually accurate descriptions that focus on scene content and object dynamics. 

## 3.2 Camera Modeling 

Metric-scale camera extrinsics and accurate camera intrinsics are essential for injecting reliable camera motion condition into camera-controllable generative models. Wan2.1 [29] is trained on a RealEstate-style dataset [45] that effectively normalizes camera trajectories under a fixed foreground depth assumption; when this assumption is violated at inference, the generated motion exhibits noticeable scale inconsistencies. To address this issue, we build a labeling pipeline that recovers metric-scale camera extrinsics and intrinsics for each video, forming a unified metric camera space .Accurate camera-controllable video generation requires camera extrinsics and intrinsics that are both geometrically reliable and con-sistent under metric scale. However, existing methods typically spe-cialize in only part of this problem, such as temporally stable camera motion, per-frame metric depth, or accurate intrinsic estimation. We therefore combine three complementary approaches—Pi3 [35], Moge2 [33], and MapAnything [13]—to recover metric-consistent camera parameters. Specifically, Pi3 provides temporally stable camera extrinsics up to an unknown global scale, Moge2 predicts per-frame metric depth, and MapAnything provides reliable camera intrinsics. We first estimate a robust global scale factor from Pi3 and Moge2 using pairwise 3D distance ratios across the video, which resolves the scale ambiguity in camera extrinsics. We then align the camera intrinsics predicted by MapAnything to the same metric space us-ing a consistent scaling strategy, ensuring geometrically accurate projection across frames. The resulting metric-scale camera param-eters are used as supervision for training. Quantitative evaluation of the recovered camera parameters is reported in Table 1. Based on the recovered parameters, we construct a camera mo-tion condition using an intermediate world-coordinate representa-tion rather than raw Plücker embeddings [21] or per-frame point clouds. We normalize camera poses to the first frame and represent camera trajectories using a structured grid in world coordinates, which is rendered with the recovered intrinsics to ensure geomet-ric consistency across frames. This representation encodes camera motion in a structured and controllable form. 

## 3.3 3D Bounding Box Modeling 

Controlling object motion solely from 2D image coordinates in-evitably discards spatial information, including depth ordering un-der occlusion and object orientation. This ambiguity motivates the need for an explicit object motion condition .Following Cinemaster [32], we represent object motion using 3D bounding boxes in full 6D, including 3D translation and 3D rotation. We focus on human subjects, as their geometry and motion can be reliably reconstructed from monocular videos using off-the-shelf Human Mesh Recovery (HMR) methods, enabling scalable auto-matic annotation. We employ TokenHMR [4] for identity-consistent tracking, and fit bounding boxes only to the rigid torso vertices of the recovered SMPL mesh [17]. This design mitigates large shape variations caused by articulated limbs or head motion (e.g., bending or sitting), resulting in more stable bounding boxes and temporally consistent 6D object motion supervision. We then convert the recovered 3D boxes into an object motion condition that provides explicit local spatial cues. Complementing the camera motion condition, which defines the global metric space, the 3D bounding box condition indicates precise screen-space oc-cupancy, relative depth ordering, and orientation. The condition is rendered as a projected 3D bounding box with depth-aware filling and consistent edge coloring, enabling the model to infer object rotation and placement without explicit angle representations. As shown in Fig. 5, although the model is trained only on humans, the learned object motion condition generalizes well to generic non-human objects. 

## 3.4 Depth-of-Field Modeling 

To model depth-of-field effects, we construct depth-of-field condition 

signals using recovered metric depth and a physically motivated bokeh operator following AkiRa [34], where blur is determined by the deviation between pixel depth and the focal plane. We use the same operator for both bokeh-augmented data generation and condition construction, ensuring consistency between training sig-nals and supervision. ## 3.5 Zoom Video Augmentation 

In practical cinematography, zoom is an essential component of camera behavior, yet recovering time-varying focal lengths directly from unconstrained videos is highly unreliable, and such sequences are difficult to curate at scale. We therefore synthesize zoom effects through data augmentation. Specifically, we model focal-length changes by applying a temporal center crop with randomly sampled crop factors and zoom intervals, and resizing the result back to the native resolution to simulate push-in and pull-out shots while keeping the final image resolution fixed. Once the zoom parameters are sampled for a video, the same transformation is consistently applied to all conditioning streams—including the camera motion condition video, the object motion condition video, and the depth-of-field condition video—to preserve pixel-wise alignment. 

## 4 Experiments 4.1 Training Paradigm 

We curate a dataset with significant camera and object dynamics from DynPose-100K [24], MultiCamVideo [1], BEDLAM2.0 [27], and internal data, totaling 110k samples with camera trajectories and 3D bounding box annotations. We further construct a zoom dataset (8k) and a bokeh dataset (15k). All training videos are unified to 81 frames at 480p resolution. We build GTL 14B on Wan2.1-14B [29], freezing the encoder, decoder, and DiT, and training only the Context Adapter. Training follows a three-stage curriculum from camera to object and finally to bokeh control. We initialize from partial VACE DiT weights [11]. Stage 1 trains camera control using trajectory annotations; Stage 2 adds 3D bounding boxes and zoom data for object motion and zoom; Stage 3 introduces bokeh data for joint control. The three stages run for 11k, 13k, and 7k iterations with batch size 8 and learning rate 5 × 10 −5, totaling approximately 288 GPU hours on 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs. An overview of the model and training pipeline is shown in Fig. 3.

Trajectory-based motion accuracy. We assess motion fidelity using a trajectory consistency error computed from point tracks extracted by CoTracker [12]. This metric measures the alignment between induced 2D motion and the reference video by comparing point trajectories across frames, and reflects the combined effects of camera and object motion. When evaluated over longer trajectories, it becomes sensitive to accumulated drift, serving as an indicator of long-term motion stability. 

Visual quality and semantic alignment. We report Fréchet In-ception Distance (FID) [9] and Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) [28] to evaluate perceptual realism and temporal coherence. To assess semantic alignment with text prompts, we additionally report the Qwen VQA Score [31], which measures the consistency between generated videos and input prompts using a vision–language model. 

## 4.3 Evaluation Datasets 

We evaluate our method on a held-out test set consisting of 1k video clips drawn from DynPose-100K, the MultiCamVideo Dataset, BED-LAM2.0, and our proprietary internal data. Together, these datasets cover a broad spectrum of foreground occupancy ratios, camera motion complexity, and data sources, including real-world daily-life recordings, cinematic videos, and synthesized scene videos. All evaluation videos are disjoint from the training set. Videos are pro-cessed using the same metric-scale annotation pipeline described in Sec. 3, producing camera parameters, foreground masks, and 3D bounding box poses. This unified annotation enables consistent and fair comparison across different methods under identical evalu-ation conditions. Unless otherwise specified, all quantitative results reported later are computed on this evaluation set.

Although the 1.3B model exhibits slightly lower visual fidelity than the 14B model, it provides a reliable and efficient proxy for comparing relative performance across different conditioning designs. The ablation studies focus on relative metric differences rather than absolute generation quality, which are largely unaffected by the backbone scale. All variants use identical camera extrinsics and intrinsics as input, differing only in the representation of the camera condition. We evaluate control-lability using camera translation and rotation errors, as reported in Table 3. The results show that our geometry-aware cubic grid representation provides more stable and accurate camera control than ray-based representations.

## 5.1 Controllable Depth-of-Field and Bokeh 

Our framework enables explicit and physically meaningful control over depth-of-field effects in generated videos. By leveraging metric depth information recovered in the metric camera space, we can synthesize a wide range of bokeh behaviors with a time-varying fo-cus depth. In particular, the focal plane can be dynamically adjusted over time, allowing smooth focus transitions as well as focus-follow effects that track a moving subject. Figure 7 shows that bokeh in our model is driven by the explicit depth-of-field condition, where both the relative depth to the focal plane and the bokeh strength are integrated into the conditioning video to drive the resulting blur. Because the focus depth is spec-ified as a continuous, time-varying signal, our method naturally supports zoom-dependent and temporally varying focus behaviors commonly observed in real cinematography, as illustrated by the second example.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present Gen Through the Lens, a camera-controllable image-to-video generation framework that enables explicit and physically grounded control over camera motion, object motion, and depth-of-field effects from a single reference image. By de-composing camera-related signals into camera, world, and object coordinate spaces and unifying them through cross-coordinate conditioning fusion, our approach preserves the physical meaning of each control signal while enabling coherent, disentangled, and cinematographically meaningful video generation. ## A.1 Label Prompt Details 

To ensure disentaglement between camera control and content description, we explicitly constrain the captions to exclude any camera related information. We use the following prompt for dense video caption generation with InternVL2.5:                                                                          

> You are aprofessional video description master, your task is to carefully observe the input video, and give a detailed and dense description of the video, please follow the instructions below: 1. It is required to pay attention to the timing of the video, reflecting the development process of the events in the video over time and the details. 2. The output content should not include camera-related information, such as camera shooting angles and camera movements. 3. Require the output to capture as much video content as possible, including details, considering both completeness and specificity. It should provide comprehensive information that is tied to specific elements of the video. 4. The output description is required to be accurate and coherent, without misinformation and misunderstanding. The output must be factually accurate, coherent in content description, and consistent with the video content. 5. Do not give the info about camera such as camera shooting Angle and camera Movement. ## A.3 3D Bounding Box Modeling and Object Motion Condition 

We use TokenHMR [4] to recover identity-consistent human meshes across frames. For each tracked individual, we extract torso vertices from the recovered SMPL mesh [17] and fit a tight 3D bounding box. The object motion condition is rendered as a projected 3D bound-ing box on a black background. Pixels inside the projected box are assigned grayscale values in the range [25 , 255 ] using per-video relative depth normalization, while background pixels remain at 0. During training, if no object motion is present, we use an all-black video as the object motion condition. During inference, we use the same per-video depth normalization rule as in training. The near bound is set to the minimum of 1 meter and the closest distance from the camera to any point on the object bounding box. The far bound is set to the maximum of 10 meters and the farthest distance from the camera to the bounding box. Depth values inside the box are normalized to this range and mapped to grayscale intensities. To encode orientation, the 12 edges of the 3D bounding box are rendered using distinct colors sampled uniformly from the hue channel of HSV space. The color assignment is fixed across frames and the dataset. 

## A.4 Depth-of-Field Modeling and Depth-of-Field Condition 

We use the same physically motivated bokeh operator for both bokeh video construction and depth-of-field condition generation. Given a per-frame focus depth, the operator computes blur radius from the depth deviation to the focal plane. To stabilize blur near large depth discontinuities, we identify regions with abrupt depth changes and apply spatially adaptive smoothing to the blur-radius field. Li et al. 

The depth-of-field condition adopts a three-channel layered rep-resentation based on a synthetic checkerboard image. All channels share the same initial black-and-white checkerboard pattern, where the image width is divided into 30 vertical segments and the height is adjusted to maintain approximately square cells. Each channel corresponds to a canonical background depth in world coordinates, fixed at 5 meters, 15 meters, and 25 meters. For each frame, foreground pixels inside the projected 3D bounding box are assigned the object’s metric depth, while background pixels retain the channel-specific depth. The bokeh operator is applied independently to each depth map, producing three grayscale videos that are stacked into RGB chan-nels to form the final depth-of-field condition video. Because blur depends jointly on depth and bokeh strength, the resulting condi-tion exhibits color variation across channels. During training, if no bokeh effect is present, we use the original unblurred checkerboard as the condition. During inference, the same procedure can be applied with manually specified bounding boxes to enable user-controlled depth-of-field effects. 

## A.5 Zoom Augmentation 

We simulate zoom using temporal center cropping followed by resizing to the original resolution. Crop factors and zoom intervals are sampled randomly. The same zoom transformation is applied consistently to the camera motion condition video, object motion condition video, and depth-of-field condition video to preserve pixel-wise alignment.
