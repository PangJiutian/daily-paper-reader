Abstract
Video generators serve as interactive worlds for exploration and simulation, yet real-
istic and visually appealing frames do not necessarily depict a consistent 3D world.
Existing benchmarks assess selected geometric properties or use vision-language models
to judge memory and consistency, leaving systematic 3D evaluation underexplored.
We introduce Orbia, the first benchmark dedicated to a systematic and comprehen-
sive evaluation of 3D consistency in interactive world models. Through controlled
exploration and revisit, we organize the evaluation into three dimensions: input preser-
vation, generated-content persistence, and 3D self-consistency. For input preservation,
registered RGB-D references and annotated anchors support appearance, geometry,
and spatial stability measurements. For generated-content persistence, we compare
revisits to regions absent from the input image with their first generated observations
to test whether models preserve what they create. For 3D self-consistency, cross-view
depth and feature correspondence, regional scale agreement, and held-out Gaussian
rendering test whether generated views describe the same 3D scene. Minute-long
exploration and revisits further test long-term consistency. Our construction and anno-
tation pipeline brings together public datasets, self-captured scenes, and simulation,
yielding 720 instances across indoor and outdoor environments and six exploration-and-
revisit protocols. Evaluation spans 27 text-, action-, and camera-conditioned models
through native-interface adapters. Our Orbia benchmark provides a systematic basis
for diagnosing 3D inconsistency and developing more persistent video world models.

Introduction
Video generation is expanding from visual synthesis toward world simulation (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan
Team et al., 2025; Google DeepMind, 2025; Gao et al., 2025), supporting interactive games (Bruce et al.,
2024; Valevski et al., 2025), open-domain exploration (Parker-Holder and Fruchter, 2025; Sun et al., 2025),
generative rendering (Yu et al., 2025a; Shen et al., 2026), and embodied simulation (Yang et al., 2024;
NVIDIA et al., 2025). This shift is contextualized by (Kong et al., 2025b), which organizes world modeling
around video, occupancy, and LiDAR representations. These applications require observations across time
and viewpoints to remain compatible with a persistent 3D world. A player should return to the same
environment, a renderer should reveal mutually consistent surfaces, and an embodied agent should reuse
previously observed spatial relationships. Visually convincing frames alone cannot establish this structural
reliability across viewpoints and repeated visits. As generated videos become environments for exploration
and interaction, a fundamental question arises: Do generated videos form a consistent 3D world?
Existing benchmarks address different aspects of this question. These protocols assess perceptual qual-
ity (Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), local or scene-level geometric self-coherence (Duan et al., 2025;
Ying et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026c; Zhao et al., 2026), or memory and state retention upon revisit using
same-view appearance comparisons, point-cloud comparisons, and vision-language judgments (Ye et al.,
2026; Lu et al., 2026c; Xu et al., 2026a). WorldLens (Liang et al., 2026b) broadens driving-world evaluation
to reconstruction, action following, downstream tasks, and human preference. Yet systematic, quantitative
evaluation of 3D consistency remains underexplored. Geometric agreement does not guarantee content
preservation, nor does a successful return establish consistency throughout exploration. A dedicated
benchmark must jointly evaluate 3D structure and the preservation of input and generated content across
viewpoints and over time during exploration and repeated revisits.
We introduce Orbia, the first benchmark dedicated to a systematic and comprehensive evaluation of 3D
consistency in interactive world models. We organize evaluation into three dimensions (Figure 1).
(1) Input preservation assesses whether the model preserves the environment shown in the input as
the viewpoint changes. Registered RGB-D references and annotated anchors measure the appearance,
geometry, and spatial relationships of observed content. Exploration also reveals regions outside the input,

(2) Generated-content persistence compares newly revealed content across visits, using its first gener-
ated appearance as the reference. The model is free to create unseen content, but should preserve what it
has created. Beyond content retention, generated views must agree in their underlying 3D structure.
(3) 3D self-consistency tests whether generated views describe the same 3D scene through cross-view
depth and feature correspondence, regional scale agreement, and held-out Gaussian rendering. These
measurements examine both local surface compatibility and scene-level coherence. To assess resistance to
temporal drift under longer exploration and more frequent revisits, we extend sequences to one minute
and repeatedly return to previously visited regions. This tests whether models maintain scene geometry
and content as the exploration history grows. We also examine how control accuracy and visual quality
relate to the three consistency dimensions.
Joint evaluation requires cross-view references and controlled revisits. Our construction pipeline prepares
registered RGB-D pairs, verifies anchor correspondence and surface support, and synthesizes trajectories
connecting the reference poses. We select revisits to newly revealed regions and manually review anchors,
reference views, and trajectories. To cover diverse scene distributions, we draw on 1real-world datasets,
2self-captured scenes, and 3simulated environments, spanning indoor and outdoor settings in both real
and virtual worlds. Processing over 110k source samples and investing over 300 hours in annotation and
curation yields 720 evaluation instances, comprising 480 short cases and 240 paired minute-long extensions.
Six exploration-and-revisit protocols cover trajectories with distinct spatial demands. Model-specific
adapters translate the trajectories into text instructions, action sequences, or camera conditions for
evaluation across control interfaces for the same exploration and revisit tasks.
To summarize, our contributions are fourfold:
• We introduce Orbia, the first benchmark dedicated to systematic, quantitative evaluation of 3D
consistency in interactive world models across viewpoints and exploration horizons.
• We decompose 3D consistency into input preservation, generated-content persistence, and 3D
self-consistency to systematically assess content and geometry.
• We build a unified annotation pipeline yielding 720 instances with registered evidence and controlled
revisits across real and simulated environments.
• We evaluate 27 text-, action-, and camera-conditioned models, examining how 3D consistency relates
to control execution, visual quality, and exploration duration.

Related Work
2.1
Interactive Video World Models
Large-scale video generators (Brooks et al., 2024; Polyak et al., 2024; Google DeepMind, 2025; Kong
et al., 2024; Wan Team et al., 2025) establish visual and temporal priors for generative simulation by
synthesizing scene appearance and motion from text or visual inputs. Interactive video world models build
on these capabilities by conditioning future observations on user-controlled inputs. Action-conditioned
systems (Bruce et al., 2024; Valevski et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; He et al., 2025b; Jiang et al., 2026)
support movement and interaction, while language-directed methods (Mao et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025)
provide semantic control over unfolding events. Explicit camera-control approaches (Wang et al., 2024;
He et al., 2025a) further expose viewpoint trajectories as a generation interface. Together, these interfaces
support exploring a generated environment through successive choices, introducing dependencies between
current outputs, earlier observations, and the user’s interaction history.
Streaming and open-domain systems (Robbyant Team et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026; DreamX Team et al.,
2026; Zhu et al., 2026b,a; Duan et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026) pursue sustained, responsive exploration
across diverse environments. Beyond generating locally coherent transitions, maintaining a world through
exploration requires relating new observations to previously visited regions. History-conditioned generation
and context memory (Li et al., 2025b; Sun et al., 2025) seek to preserve information from past observations
when extending a rollout. These developments also connect to the view of world models as interactive
proxies providing feedback for agents (Yang et al., 2026a). They motivate evaluating the stability of a
generated environment alongside visual quality and control adherence, particularly when users leave a
region and later return.
Agentic world modeling connects predictive simulation to decision-making across environments (Chu
et al., 2026). Visual navigation approaches couple language-conditioned future observation prediction
with policy learning (Dong et al., 2026b), or integrate visual foresight, planning, and memory within a
unified model (Dong et al., 2026a). For autonomous driving, vision-language-action systems bring together
perception, linguistic reasoning, and actionable outputs (Hu et al., 2025a); OneVL uses future visual
prediction and language explanations to supervise compact latent reasoning for planning (Lu et al., 2026b).
These applications make the persistence of spatial evidence relevant to both generation and downstream
decisions.
A complementary line examines how generative capabilities are learned and aligned. DanceOPD composes
generative capability fields on student-induced states (Zhou et al., 2026b), while on-policy diffusion self-
distillation converts reward guidance into intermediate prediction targets (Zhou et al., 2026a). These image-
generation studies provide a training perspective on capability alignment that complements evaluation of
consistency during interactive rollouts.
2.2
3D Interactive World Models
Explicit scene representations enable navigable world generation and editing (Yu et al., 2025a; Liang et al.,
2025; Szymanowicz et al., 2025; World Labs, 2025, 2026). These systems associate generated content with
a scene that can be queried from different viewpoints. Complementary methods couple generative priors
with reconstructed geometry or spatial conditioning (Yu et al., 2025b; Ren et al., 2025; Huang et al.,
2025b; Lu et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026b; InSpatio Team et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a). Point clouds,
Gaussian representations, and warped observations provide camera-aligned evidence for synthesizing
unseen regions, while allowing learned priors to complete missing appearance. Geometry may directly
determine a rendered output or instead guide a generator that retains freedom to revise appearance and
complete occluded regions. This combination connects the flexibility of image and video generation with
structured constraints on viewpoint changes. Geometry-grounded weather video synthesis offers a related
editing perspective, combining semantic appearance conditioning, simulated particle dynamics, and scene
geometry to control weather effects (Qian et al., 2026a).
The representation taxonomy of Kong et al. (2025b) places video, occupancy, and LiDAR generation
within a common 3D/4D world-modeling landscape. Sensor-based world modeling provides complementary
representations of dynamic scenes. DynamicCity models semantic 4D occupancy (Bian et al., 2025), while
LiDARCrafter generates and edits temporally coherent LiDAR sequences (Liang et al., 2026a) and U4D
introduces uncertainty-conditioned generation (Xu et al., 2026b). Cross-sensor pretraining (Kong et al.,
2026) and multimodal scene-consistency learning (Kong et al., 2025a) address representation learning in
4


these sensor-rich settings. Together, these works motivate assessing scene structure and dynamics through
evidence appropriate to each representation.
Spatial memory extends these mechanisms beyond short temporal contexts. Surfel-indexed view re-
trieval (Li et al., 2025c), geometry-guided correspondences (Shen et al., 2026), and geometry-aligned or
external memory (AlayaWorld Team et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2026b) reconnect current
viewpoints with previously observed content. These advances address spatial forgetting and drift, and
existing studies include reconstruction, revisit, or geometric-consistency diagnostics. Nevertheless, using
3D information does not by itself establish whether original input content is faithfully retained, newly
generated regions persist after leaving view, and repeated observations agree as one 3D world. Orbia
evaluates these complementary requirements jointly under controlled exploration, enabling comparisons
across explicit 3D, geometry-guided, and memory-augmented approaches.
2.3
World Model Evaluation
Video benchmarks characterize visual quality, temporal consistency, and prompt alignment (Huang
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), while faithfulness-oriented evaluations probe physical plausibility and
commonsense (Zheng et al., 2025; Bansal et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Embodied
benchmarks connect generated dynamics to action-level behavior and functional utility (Qin et al., 2024;
Hu et al., 2025b; Shang et al., 2026). WorldLens (Liang et al., 2026b) evaluates driving world models across
generation, reconstruction, action following, downstream tasks, and human preference. Complementing this
benchmark, 3D and 4D World Modeling: A Survey (Kong et al., 2025b) organizes evaluation perspectives
spanning generation, forecasting, planning, reconstruction, and downstream utility. Together, perceptual
and functional criteria answer related but distinct questions about whether generated videos are visually
convincing and useful for interaction.
Evaluation reliability also depends on evidence grounding. DriveBench shows that plausible VLM answers
can reflect linguistic priors rather than visual input (Xie et al., 2025), while Open-o3-Video makes video
reasoning traceable through timestamps, objects, and bounding boxes (Meng et al., 2026). These findings
support pairing language-based assessments with explicit visual and geometric evidence.
Interactive suites examine control responsiveness, trajectory adherence, and geometric consistency (Duan
et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026c; Fang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), while multi-turn protocols assess
interaction adherence and resulting state changes (Ying et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026a).
Complementary diagnostics isolate deformation and occlusion inconsistency (Gu et al., 2025), static-
scene reconstructability (Fan and Favaro, 2026), and reconstruction-grounded manipulation (Wang et al.,
2026b). Geometric evaluation therefore already addresses several failure modes, with different protocols
emphasizing different aspects of spatial coherence.
Memory-oriented tests examine returns to previously observed scenes and recovery of persistent or
dynamically updated states (Ye et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026c; Chen et al., 2026b,d), revisit geometry (Xu
et al., 2026a), and calibrated revisit similarity (Gu et al., 2026b). Long-horizon tests track drift and
state-returning behavior (Wu et al., 2026b; Gu et al., 2026a), while agent-based evaluators provide
transition and persistence judgments (Chen et al., 2026c). Building on these complementary perspectives,
Orbia jointly measures preservation of the input scene, persistence of newly generated regions, and 3D
self-consistency across repeated exploration. This organization separates fidelity to observed evidence from
retention of generated content and compatibility of generated views. Table 1 summarizes the coverage of
representative benchmarks.

The Orbia Benchmark
Orbia systematically evaluates the 3D consistency of interactive video world models. In this section, we
introduce the evaluation principles and protocols (Section 3.1), the data construction and annotation
pipeline (Section 3.2), and the dataset scale and coverage across scene sources and exploration horizons
(Section 3.3).
3.1
Evaluation Principles and Protocols
Interactive World Generation. An interactive video world model generates observations from an initial
image I0, a scene description s, and user inputs U, yielding ˆV = M(I0, s, U). Depending on the model’s
interface, U comprises requested text instructions T, discrete actions A, or camera poses P.
5


Consistency Decomposition. Existing works examine aspects of consistency, including local geometric
agreement or content recovery upon return (Duan et al., 2025; Ying et al., 2026). A systematic evaluation
should account for what the inputs constrain, what the model is free to generate, and whether the
generated content forms a coherent 3D scene. We evaluate three dimensions covering input content, newly
generated regions, and 3D structure.
Input preservation
We test whether the model preserves the scene shown in the initial image. Distinctive static objects and
structures are annotated as anchors, providing landmarks that should remain in place across viewpoint
changes and reappear when the camera returns. Evaluating anchors individually prevents a missing
landmark from being overlooked in a scene-wide average dominated by other visible objects and scene
content.
Generated-content persistence
We evaluate regions absent from the input image. Their appearance is not prescribed, but the controls
specify when the model should revisit them. We compare these revisits with the first generated
observations to test whether the same content remains at corresponding locations when the camera
revisits the region.
3D self-consistency
We examine the generated video itself, using recovered cameras to test cross-view agreement and
whether a shared 3D reconstruction explains observations beyond those used to build it. These tests
assess internal coherence independently of the requested camera trajectory used to produce the video.
Exploration-and-Revisit Protocols. Exploration-and-revisit trajectories test input preservation, generated-
content persistence, and 3D self-consistency within one rollout. Six templates challenge preservation
under changes in parallax and viewing direction: 1forward and backward, 2lateral return, 3yaw return, 4arc
return, 5orbit around an anchor, and 6peek and return. In peek-and-return, the camera moves past an
anchor into an unseen region, returns, and revisits that region on a second excursion. Each trajectory
starts at P0 and returns to the same camera pose at Pr. For input preservation, we compare the return
view against the initial RGB image and reference depth. A cross-view query at Pq uses registered RGB-D
and annotated anchors to evaluate input-visible surfaces from another viewpoint. For generated-content
persistence, we pair a first visit at Pfirst to a region outside the input view with a revisit at Previsit after
exploring elsewhere. The first generated observation provides the reference for comparing appearance and
geometry across visits. For 3D self-consistency, views sampled along the trajectory support comparisons
of corresponding surfaces across views and reconstruction tests using held-out generated views.

Reference-Guided Data Construction
We combine 1public datasets, 2self-captured scenes, and 3UE simulation to cover diverse indoor and
outdoor environments. Each case pairs registered RGB-D observations from different viewpoints with a
synthesized revisit trajectory that follows one of six templates and passes through both reference poses.
This enables controlled exploration from existing observations without frame-by-frame RGB-D references.

to reviewing the completed cases.

ViPE (Huang et al., 2025a). We select input–query pairs (P0, Pq) from registered poses using each
template’s rotation and translation requirements.

3.3
Dataset Scale and Coverage
We curate 240 cases from approximately 110,000 source samples across four public datasets, Scan-
Net++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023), SpatialVID (Wang et al., 2026a), Sekai (Li et al., 2025d), and Mira-
Data (Ju et al., 2024). For simulation, we collect 48 UE environments and render 365 candidate videos,
retaining 180 cases after curation. We also record 85 videos ourselves and retain 60. Together, data
annotation and manual curation required over 300 hours.
Orbia contains 720 cases and 859 anchor evaluation targets, with 480 short cases and 240 paired
minute-long extensions, standardized at 720p and 16 FPS. The short cases cover 120 indoor and 360
outdoor settings, including 300 cases from real-world observations and 180 from simulation.
Short
trajectories last approximately 22 seconds with one revisit; their minute-long extensions include three
revisits to test preservation through longer exploration and repeated returns. Source-specific construction
procedures and dataset composition are detailed in Appendix B.

Evaluating 3D Consistency
Given a generated video, we estimate depth and poses with DA3 (Lin et al., 2026) and evaluate the
designated observations against registered RGB-D references or earlier generated views. Cross-view
agreement and held-out rendering assess internal 3D consistency. The three dimensions differ in their
reference: the input scene for input preservation, the model’s own first observation for generated-content
persistence, and the generated video itself for 3D self-consistency. The survey by Kong et al. (2025b)
distinguishes generation, reconstruction, and task-oriented evaluation; here, we focus on complementary
content and geometry tests during interactive exploration. We describe the core consistency metrics and
long-horizon evaluation below. Detailed metric definitions and computation procedures are provided in
Appendix C.
Input Preservation. To assess input preservation at two checkpoints, we compare the return at Pr
with the input at P0 and the query at Pq with registered RGB-D references on input-visible surfaces.
Surfaces revealed for the first time at Pq have no reference and are excluded from the query comparison.
1Scene Appearance Fidelity quantifies appearance fidelity at return and query views, while 2Anchor
Appearance Fidelity assesses the preservation of distinctive landmarks across viewpoints and on return.
Both use DINO similarity (Oquab et al., 2024), PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS on input-visible scene or anchor
regions, with equal weight per anchor. A missing or altered landmark is one of the most visible failures
of a generated world, yet it often occupies a small part of the frame, so a scene-wide average can hide
it. Scoring each anchor separately makes such failures directly measurable. 3Scene Geometry Fidelity
quantifies geometric fidelity to the observed scene, while 4Anchor Geometry Fidelity assesses anchor
geometry relative to the surrounding scene. Both measure depth AbsRel against the registered reference
using a shared scene-level scale.
5Anchor Identity Consistency tests anchor identity retention during
exploration. SAM3.1 (Carion et al., 2026) tracks input masks toward Pq. We average masked DINO
cosine similarity to the input over four times and equally across anchors.
Generated-Content Persistence. For each annotated pair (Pfirst, Previsit), the first generated obser-
vation serves as the reference for a revisit at the same pose or an overlapping viewpoint. Regions
outside the input view have no ground truth, and many different contents would be plausible. What must
hold is that the model shows the same content when it returns, so the model’s own earlier output serves
as the reference. 1Revisit Appearance Consistency measures appearance retention across revisits, while
2Revisit Depth Consistency measures geometric consistency across visits. Both use surfaces visible in
both observations, matched by projecting first-visit depth into the revisit view with estimated cameras;
we compare DINO features for appearance and compute AbsRel for depth. Matching through 3D aligns
the same surfaces across visits, so the test also holds when the revisit viewpoint differs. At identical
requested poses, we also compare images directly with DINO, PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, and depth maps
at the shared estimator scale. 3Revisit Surface Agreement measures whether both visits reconstruct the
same 3D surfaces. We compute a bidirectional nearest-point F-score between aligned point clouds, with
matching distance relative to scene depth.
3D Self-Consistency. We assess whether generated views form a coherent 3D scene using cross-view
correspondences from recovered cameras. These tests do not use the requested trajectory. A model can
follow the requested camera motion and still produce views that disagree in 3D, or produce a coherent
scene along the wrong trajectory; control alignment and 3D self-consistency are therefore scored separately.
1Cross-View Depth Consistency tests whether views agree on the depth of shared surfaces, while 2Cross-
View Feature Consistency tests whether matched surfaces retain consistent visual features across views.
Both use valid, unoccluded correspondences established by bidirectional depth reprojection for frame
pairs at short and medium temporal intervals, measuring depth AbsRel and DINO cosine similarity,
respectively. 3Regional Scale Agreement tests relative scale consistency across scene regions. We measure
dispersion in observed-to-reprojected depth ratios across a 4×4 grid. 4Reconstruction tests reconstruction
accuracy on unseen views. DA3 predicts a Gaussian scene (Kerbl et al., 2023) from 64 context frames;
we render 16 held-out views and compare them with the corresponding generated frames using PSNR,
SSIM, and LPIPS for pixel-level and perceptual agreement. WorldLens (Liang et al., 2026b) likewise
uses reconstructed scenes and re-rendering to assess driving-world fidelity. Pairwise tests check nearby
frames, whereas held-out rendering tests whether one scene explains views outside its context, revealing
inconsistency that accumulates along the trajectory.

Experiments
We compare 27 models: 19 camera- or action-conditioned world models and eight TI2V models. Adapters
map requested trajectories to native camera conditions, discrete commands, or text instructions, starting
from the same initial image. Table 2 reports all six tiers and the overall score. Appendices D and E

5.1

SolarWM-H3 leads overall. SolarWM-H3 ranks first overall at 76.32, with the strongest generated-content
persistence and temporal stability among world models. Lyra2 and EVOKE rank second and third,
leading control and visual quality, respectively. Gen3C leads input preservation, while HY-WorldPlay
leads 3D self-consistency. Seedance2.5 leads the TI2V group overall, whereas Cosmos3-Super leads its
visual quality, generated-content persistence, and 3D self-consistency. No model leads all six tiers.

Semantic similarity alone does not ensure visual fidelity. High semantic similarity between a first visit
and revisit can coexist with poor perceptual fidelity (Figure 3, bottom two rows). MiniMax-H3, for
example, scores 77.27 on different-view DINO but only 17.32 on normalized same-view LPIPS (Table 4).
A revisit can match the scene’s general content while changing textures and object appearance. Our tests
therefore combine semantic, perceptual, and pixel-level comparisons to check both whether the same
content reappears and whether its visual details are preserved.
Appearance consistency does not establish spatial agreement.
Lyra2 leads several revisit-appearance
measures, whereas HY-WorldPlay leads reprojected depth consistency and SolarWM-H3 leads surface
agreement (Table 4). The appearance tests compare how the revisited content looks; the depth and surface
tests check whether it occupies the same 3D locations. Preserving visual details and preserving the 3D
layout therefore require separate checks.
Local cross-view agreement does not ensure coherent scene reconstruction. FantasyWorld ranks second
among world models in cross-view feature consistency at both temporal intervals, but second to last in
reconstruction PSNR (Appendix E.1.5). HY-WorldPlay likewise leads depth consistency and regional
scale agreement without achieving the best reconstruction score. These ranking differences show that
strong agreement between pairs of views does not necessarily mean that the generated views can be
reconstructed into a coherent 3D scene. Reconstruction consequently provides a separate test of whether
the video depicts one coherent scene throughout exploration.

5.3
Relationships among Quality, Control, and Consistency
We examine Spearman correlations between tier scores. Figure 5 reports model-level correlations separately
for short and long videos within each model group.
Visual quality does not ensure 3D consistency. LingBot-World v2 ranks second among world models in
visual quality, but twelfth in both input preservation and generated-content persistence, and fourteenth in
3D self-consistency. Gen3C shows the reverse: seventeenth in visual quality but first in input preservation.
Across all models, visual quality has a rank correlation of only 0.33 with the mean of the three consistency
tiers among world models and 0.21 among TI2V models. Figure 4 illustrates this discrepancy: LingBot-
World v2 generates a detailed view but yields a less faithful reconstruction and weaker depth agreement
than HY-WorldPlay. Visual quality alone therefore gives little assurance that a model preserves scene
content or maintains a consistent 3D scene. This separation is consistent with WorldLens (Liang et al.,
2026b), which reports distinct strengths in visual fidelity, geometry, and downstream utility for driving
world models.
Strong visual quality does not guarantee faithful execution of the requested scene and camera motion. Quality
and control have weak rank agreement among world models (0.08 for short videos and 0.30 for long
videos), and negative correlations among TI2V models (−0.43 and −0.26). Short-video TI2V quality
also correlates negatively with input preservation (−0.62). In Table 2, Cosmos3-Super leads quality but
ranks sixth in control, while Seedance2.5 leads input preservation but ranks seventh in quality. Higher
visual quality does not imply more accurate camera motion, and in short TI2V videos it can coincide with
poorer input preservation.
Control and 3D self-consistency measure different capabilities. Lyra2 ranks first in control but seventh in
3D self-consistency, while HY-WorldPlay ranks sixteenth and first, respectively. Across world models, the
rank correlation between the two tiers is only 0.09 in short videos and −0.04 in long videos (Figure 5).
A model can generate views that agree with one another even when the camera follows the wrong path.
Internal geometric agreement and accurate execution of camera commands are thus separate requirements
for interactive world generation.
Preserving the input does not ensure preserving newly generated content. World-model input preservation
and generated-content persistence have strongly aligned model rankings, but much weaker agreement
across cases within each model. For short videos, the model-level correlation is 0.87, compared with a
mean within-model correlation of 0.35; the same pattern holds for long videos. Strong models tend to
perform well on both tasks, yet a successful input-preservation case can still lose newly generated content

Following the camera path is not enough to preserve content during long exploration. Among world models, the
correlation between control and input preservation falls from 0.63 in short videos to 0.32 in long videos;
for generated-content persistence, it falls from 0.53 to 0.22. Longer exploration requires models to preserve
scene content across additional movements and revisits, even when they follow the requested camera path.
5.4
Control and Consistency across Trajectory Templates
Figure 6 compares all six trajectory templates using equal model weights within each group. The templates
range from reversing a translation to circling an anchor or revealing an occluded region. These comparisons
show which camera motions expose failures that are less apparent on a simple forward–backward path.
TI2V models fall behind in camera control on every trajectory template. TI2V models score below world models
in control on all six templates, for both short and long videos. On short forward–backward trajectories,
their mean control is 44.05, compared with 62.41 for world models. On orbit trajectories, the gap widens
to 12.95 versus 53.15. Meanwhile, TI2V visual quality remains within 76.70–77.94 across the short-video
templates. These models can produce good-looking frames across camera motions, but translating a text
instruction into the specified trajectory remains difficult, especially when position and viewing direction
must change together.
Orbit motion challenges both camera control and scene preservation. For short world-model videos, orbit
has the lowest mean control, input-preservation, and generated-content-persistence scores among the six
templates. Control falls from 62.41 on forward–backward motion to 53.15 on orbit, while input preservation
and generated-content persistence reach 61.83 and 60.97. Circling an anchor repeatedly changes visible
surfaces and occlusions. Simple reversal tests therefore underestimate the challenge of preserving a scene
across different viewpoints.
Complex trajectories can still end in forgetting despite stronger control. For long world-model videos, peek-
and-return has the highest control score (55.09) but the lowest generated-content persistence (37.32).
Models can therefore follow this camera motion yet fail to preserve the content revealed during the peek.
Preserving newly revealed content through changes in visibility remains difficult even when camera control
is comparatively strong.

5.5
Performance Degradation during Long Exploration
Figures 7 and 8 show temporal performance in visual quality, control, and local geometry for all models in
successive five-second windows. Local geometry includes depth, feature, and regional-scale consistency.
Control declines most over time in leading models from both groups. Across SolarWM-H3, Lyra2, EVOKE,
Echo-WM, and Gen3C, control drops by an average of 26.98 points from the first to the final five-second
window of minute-long videos. For Seedance2.5, Kling3.0, and Cosmos3-Super, the corresponding decline
is 23.25 points. Control declines more than visual quality or local geometry in every one of these eight
models. SolarWM-H3 falls from 91.91 to 64.50, and Cosmos3-Super from 60.26 to 34.94. Sustaining
accurate camera motion remains difficult for both groups.
TI2V visual quality remains stable while local geometry deteriorates. During minute-long generation, local
geometry declines more than visual quality in seven of the eight TI2V models. The mean declines are 7.13
points for local geometry and only 2.08 for visual quality. Wan2.7, for example, loses 16.26 points in local
geometry but only 3.11 in quality. Cosmos3-Super’s quality improves slightly while its geometry falls by
5.34 points. Visually stable frames therefore do not ensure that geometric agreement is maintained during
long exploration.
Long trajectories make newly generated content much harder to preserve. Figure 6 shows lower generated-
content persistence (Tier 4) on long videos for every trajectory template in both model groups. On
forward–backward trajectories, scores fall from 66.21 to 40.96 for world models and from 67.74 to 44.58
for TI2V models. On peek-and-return, they fall from 64.29 to 37.32 and from 69.52 to 42.83, respectively.
The drop spans roughly 15–27 points across the six templates. Long exploration exposes forgetting even
on simple forward–backward paths.

Conclusion
Orbia provides 720 exploration-and-revisit cases across indoor and outdoor environments and six motion
protocols to evaluate input preservation, generated-content persistence, and 3D self-consistency. By
distinguishing observed content from newly generated regions, and combining appearance, geometry, and
held-out reconstruction tests, the benchmark diagnoses complementary aspects of whether generated
videos depict a persistent 3D world.
Our evaluation of 27 text-, action-, and camera-conditioned models shows that strong visual quality and
accurate control do not ensure 3D consistency, while local cross-view agreement can coexist with poor scene
reconstruction. Preserving the input scene also does not reliably predict whether newly generated content
survives a revisit. Minute-long exploration further exposes declining control, content forgetting, and
geometric deterioration that visually stable frames can conceal. These findings motivate evaluating world
models through both sustained exploration and explicit revisits, with separate tests of content retention
and spatial coherence. We hope Orbia supports more reliable comparisons and guides the development of
models that maintain persistent spatial memory, follow camera commands, and preserve coherent scenes
throughout exploration.

Dataset Overview and Annotated Examples
This appendix describes the composition and reference annotations of Orbia. We report dataset statistics in
Section A.1, describe the exploration templates in Section A.2, and present complete annotated examples
in Section A.3.
A.1
Dataset Statistics
Orbia contains 720 evaluation cases and 859 anchor evaluation targets. The dataset combines varied scenes
with registered reference views and repeatable camera trajectories, enabling comparisons of how models
preserve observed content and maintain newly generated regions during exploration.
Scene coverage. Public datasets cover indoor rooms, streets, and natural environments. Self-captured
videos add outdoor landmarks, while UE provides virtual scenes with rendered depth and known camera
poses. This combination supports evaluation across real and simulated environments. We scanned 110,406
public videos and scenes, reduced them to 10,944 machine-selected candidates and an 881-case diversity
pool, and retained 240 public-data cases. Table 5 lists the raw sample counts and final source composition.
Annotated references.
Each case provides an input image and a registered query view, with depth
and anchor masks. Anchors occupy a median of 5.2% of the input image and 5.4% of the query image,
so changes to these objects can be overlooked in whole-image scores. Their masks and depth enable
object-level appearance and geometry comparisons. At the query view, input preservation is evaluated
on surfaces visible in the input; newly revealed regions are evaluated at later registered revisits to the
corresponding scene surfaces.
Exploration protocols.
Each template contributes 80 short and 40 long cases. Forward–backward and
lateral trajectories primarily change camera position, while yaw and orbit introduce larger orientation
changes (Table 7). Long extensions preserve the short case’s initial scene and references while adding
more revisits and longer intervals between them (Table 6), allowing us to assess content preservation as
exploration continues.
A.2
Exploration-and-Revisit Templates
The six trajectory templates vary how the camera explores a scene and revisits previously observed regions
(Figure 12). Each case starts at P0 and includes a registered query pose Pq and a return pose Pr. The
paired poses (Pfirst, Previsit) provide two observations of content revealed during exploration. Section A.3
shows the trajectories and reference views for annotated examples from each of the four scene source
groups.
• Forward–backward. The camera advances into the scene and retraces its path while maintaining
its viewing direction. Changes in object size and occlusion test whether scene depth and object
placement remain consistent as the camera approaches and retreats.
• Lateral return. The camera moves sideways and returns while keeping a similar viewing direction.
Nearby and distant surfaces shift by different amounts, testing whether the model preserves their
relative depth and recovers surfaces that become temporarily occluded.
• Yaw return. The camera rotates to reveal another part of the scene and then turns back, with little
translation. This tests whether content remains recognizable after leaving the field of view and
appearing again.
• Arc return. The camera follows a curved trajectory through the scene before returning. The changing
position and viewing direction reveal different sides of objects, testing whether their appearance and
geometry remain consistent across changing viewpoints along the curved trajectory.
• Orbit around an anchor. The camera moves around an anchor while looking toward it. Observing the
same object from multiple directions tests whether its shape and position relative to surrounding
objects remain consistent when viewed from different sides and at different points along the trajectory.
• Peek-and-return. The camera passes an anchor near the starting view, explores a previously unseen
region, and returns. A second excursion revisits that region, providing the pair (Pfirst, Previsit) to
test whether newly generated content is retained between visits.

B
Dataset Construction and Annotation
Constructing an Orbia case requires a pair of registered reference views, anchors that can be verified
across those views, and a camera trajectory that supports exploration and revisits. We describe how these
components are assembled from different sources, extended to one minute, and reviewed before release.
Table 5 reports dataset statistics, and Appendix A.3 presents annotated examples.
B.1
From Source Observations to Evaluation Cases
Following Section 3.2, our pipeline connects observed scene references with designed camera motion in four
stages. Two registered RGB-D views provide the input and query references at P0 and Pq. A synthesized
trajectory passes through these poses and returns to P0 at Pr, enabling controlled exploration without
recording every intermediate view.
• Select reference views. Prepare depth and camera annotations, then identify input–query pairs
compatible with the translation and rotation of a trajectory template. Candidate pairs should
provide a meaningful viewpoint change while keeping a recognizable landmark visible in both views.
• Verify anchors across views. Obtain an object mask at each endpoint and check that both masks
identify the same static object or structure. Project the input anchor into the query view using
depth and cameras to identify the surfaces that can be compared across views.
• Construct exploration and revisits. Fit a smooth trajectory through the reference poses and check
its motion against the available scene geometry. Select a first visit and a later revisit to a region
outside the input view, recording the intervening exploration and the surfaces visible at both visits.
• Rank, review, and annotate. Rank candidates by image, anchor, trajectory, and revisit quality. Human
reviewers inspect the proposed annotations and trajectories, correct unsuitable choices, and select
the final cases. A VLM produces a factual scene caption for model conditioning.
B.2
Construction from Different Data Sources
The sources differ in which components are available initially. Public videos require view selection from
recorded camera motion. ScanNet++ additionally provides a semantic mesh for anchor and visibility
annotation. For UE and self-capture, we choose landmarks and trajectory templates during acquisition.
The procedures below adapt the common pipeline to these differences.
B.2.1
Public Videos: View Selection and Anchor Verification
SpatialVID (Wang et al., 2026a), Sekai (Li et al., 2025d), and MiraData (Ju et al., 2024) broaden the
range of real-world environments. Their recorded camera paths usually lack the revisits required by our
protocols, so we extract suitable reference views and synthesize a new trajectory through them.
Prepare geometry and rank view pairs. We retain SpatialVID’s released depth and cameras and recover
missing geometry for Sekai and MiraData with ViPE (Huang et al., 2025a). SpatialVID’s sparse geometry
indices are mapped to the corresponding video frames, while the Sekai/MiraData adapter uses per-frame
geometry. We filter registered pose pairs by each template’s translation and rotation requirements.
Ranking favors motion compatible with the template and sufficient viewpoint change, while balancing
template coverage and distributing candidates across different scenes and times within each video. Ranked
alternatives allow another pair to be examined when the first fails annotation.
Propose and segment landmarks. A VLM (Qwen Team, 2026) examines five frames spanning the
candidate pair, including both endpoints and three approximately evenly spaced intermediate frames.
Figure 17 presents the anchor-selection instructions. It proposes static, bounded landmarks that are
visually distinctive and recognizable at both endpoints. SAM3 (Carion et al., 2026) segments each proposal
independently in the two reference images. We check mask area, connectivity, and completeness, remove
duplicate proposals, and track shortlisted objects between views to verify that the endpoint masks identify
the same physical landmark.
Filter fragmented masks and identity mismatches. The SpatialVID strict anchor profile accepts
endpoint masks covering 0.3%–50% of the image and requires tracking agreement of at least 0.8 IoU
at both endpoints. Mask integrity is assessed through connected components and internal gaps. The

C.2

Visual quality measures frame appearance and temporal smoothness. We adopt five components from

1. Aesthetic Quality

average the frame scores and multiply by ten.
2. Imaging Quality

median statistics reduce the influence of a few extreme depth samples; cells without enough correspondences
are excluded.

Context and held-out frames are uniformly sampled from the estimated sequence. DA3 (Lin et al., 2026;
Kerbl et al., 2023) predicts Gaussians in a feedforward pass using context RGB, recovered poses, and
intrinsics. Each fidelity measure is averaged over the 16 held-out views on pixels with finite positive
rendered depth.

C.7
Evaluation Pipeline and Score Aggregation
The evaluator recovers geometry and constructs Gaussian and feature representations for metric comparison.
Measurements are normalized and combined into tier scores and the overall score.
Recover cameras and depth. We decode each generated video on its requested timeline, sample frames
at stride two, and include the registered query, return, and revisit frames. DA3 Giant 1.1 (Lin et al.,
2026) estimates poses, intrinsics, depth, and confidence at processing resolution 504. Sequences with
at most 240 sampled frames are processed jointly. Longer sequences use 120-frame streaming chunks
with 30-frame overlap and SALAD loop closure. Chunk alignment places the estimates in a common
coordinate system. Each recovered observation is stored with its video-frame index for subsequent event
and cross-view comparisons.
Predict Gaussians and render held-out views. From the sampled sequence, we select 64 context frames
and 16 disjoint held-out frames. The context RGB images and recovered cameras are passed to DA3’s
feedforward Gaussian prediction. Images are resized to 504 × 504 with matching intrinsics, and held-out
cameras are expressed in the reconstruction’s coordinates and scale. We render RGB and depth at the 16
held-out cameras with gsplat, using a black background. These renders and their corresponding generated
frames supply the reconstruction measurements in Tier 5.
Extract appearance and identity features. We extract DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) global and patch
descriptors from the selected images. Camera and depth correspondences identify patch pairs for cross-view
and revisit comparisons. Registered reference masks define scene and anchor regions for input preservation;
SAM3.1 (Carion et al., 2026) tracks input anchors to the four identity-scoring times. PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPS compare the image pairs specified by each metric. Visual-quality predictors operate on their own
frame and triplet schedules, as described in Tier 2.
Compute event and temporal measurements. Recovered cameras are aligned to the requested trajectory
for pose and action scoring. Depth, DINO correspondences, and point clouds provide the geometric
measurements; query and return observations use registered references, while revisit measurements compare
the selected generated observations. The same video estimates support five-second window measurements
for Tier 6. We first reduce frame, anchor, and frame-pair observations to case-level components, then
apply the score mappings and aggregation below.
Normalization and aggregation.
We convert metric components to a common 0–100 scale, with higher
scores indicating better performance. Unit-range similarities are scaled directly; errors and PSNR use
fixed linear mappings calibrated with equal weight per model. For Tiers 3–5, we normalize case-level
measurements, average each component across valid cases, and apply the tier weights defined above. Tier 6
completes its temporal aggregation within each video before averaging across videos. The final six tier
scores are combined as

Model Adapters and Generation Settings
We group the evaluated models by the control interface used in our experiments: camera-conditioned,
action-conditioned, and text-conditioned. For each model, we describe below its generation setting. The
benchmark provides an initial image I0, a scene caption, and a camera trajectory {Pt}. Adapters convert
the trajectory to the model’s coordinate convention and temporal sampling. Image resizing or cropping
is accompanied by the corresponding intrinsic transformation when the model accepts calibration. For
chunked generators, we extend the terminal camera pose to the required length and trim the extra output
frames. Models with fixed generation windows continue from their own generated images or retained

context. Evaluated videos use the benchmark’s 16-FPS timeline; when a model uses a different native
frame rate, its controls or text timings follow that native clock before output conversion.
D.1
Camera-Conditioned Models
These models receive numerical camera conditions derived from {Pt}, expressed relative to the initial

front end. This applies to Echo-WM and Matrix game 3.5; HY-WorldPlay also receives camera poses and
derives its auxiliary action labels internally. Cosmos3-Nano’s continuous camera increments belong to

SolarWM-H3 (Huang et al., 2026) generates videos autoregressively with camera conditioning. We use

TI2V Models
TI2V models receive I0 and a prompt combining the scene caption with a motion script derived from the

From poses to motion descriptions. We analyze translation and rotation separately, smooth their trends
over approximately 0.6 seconds, and group them into motion phases: travel, turning, pauses, reversal,
and return. The script states the direction and duration of each phase, with turn angles derived from
the corresponding poses. For curved trajectories, the script describes both the path and the viewing
direction; orbit prompts name the public anchor when available. Brief fluctuations are merged into longer
phases, while meaningful pauses and direction changes are retained. This simplification changes the text
description, leaving the requested trajectory used for evaluation unchanged.

the resulting motion text to the scene caption. A continuation prompt starts from its supplied generated

E.1.3
Tier 3: Input Preservation
Recovering the initial scene remains harder than retaining recognizable anchors.
Return scores vary the most across models in both groups, while Identity scores are generally high and
more similar across models (Table 3). Identity is evaluated during the input-to-query interval, when the
initial observation is more recent. Return requires recovery of the starting scene after the full exploration.
The contrast points to a longer-term memory challenge: recognizable objects can survive early exploration
even when the original view is not faithfully recovered after completing the full exploration trajectory and
returning to the initial viewpoint.
Stored scene evidence can support recovery after exploration.
Gen3C leads world-model Return and overall input preservation despite ranking 17th in visual quality. Its
persistent 3D cache supplies target-view renderings during generation (Ren et al., 2025), keeping earlier
scene evidence accessible when the camera returns. This cache may help it recover the original view after
exploration. SolarWM-H3 instead leads Query, and Cosmos3-Nano leads Identity, showing that different
models lead recovery of the starting view, fidelity at the requested query viewpoint, and recognition of the
original anchors.
TI2V models have higher mean Identity and Return scores but lower mean Query fidelity than world
models. Seedance2.5 leads both checkpoints within its group; other models retain recognizable content
with weaker viewpoint-specific fidelity. Figures 30 and 31 compare these checkpoints with their registered
references.

E.1.5
Tier 5: 3D Self-Consistency
Wider temporal separation exposes inconsistencies hidden between nearby views.
Every model has lower feature consistency at medium than at short temporal offsets, with greater between-
model variation at the medium interval (Table 11). Depth and regional scale also separate world models
more strongly than short-range features. Together, these results locate the harder part of 3D consistency:
maintaining corresponding content and spatial relationships as the camera moves farther through the
scene.
High reconstruction scores can coexist with weak depth and scale agreement.
HY-WorldPlay leads depth and regional scale. Its Reconstituted Context Memory retrieves geometrically
relevant historical frames, and Context Forcing preserves memory use during distillation (Sun et al., 2025),
consistent with its local geometric strength. Astra achieves strong reconstruction scores but weaker depth
and regional-scale agreement. Reconstruction measures how well a shared 3D representation reproduces the
generated views; low-detail outputs may make this task easier without yielding equally strong geometric
agreement. ABot-World also combines high reconstruction PSNR with low depth and scale consistency.
Five TI2V models exceed the best world-model self-consistency aggregate, with their largest group
advantages in depth and regional scale. Their weaker control results indicate that internally compatible
generated views can still follow a different path from the requested cameras.
E.1.6
Tier 6: Temporal Stability
Models differ most in temporal control.
Temporal control scores vary more across models than quality or geometry scores, in both average
performance and stability (Table 12). TI2V models have higher group means in quality and local geometry
but weaker control, bringing their aggregate Tier 6 performance close to that of world models. Seedance2.5
leads its group overall through stronger control, while Cosmos3-Super leads quality and geometry.
55


Different models lead in average performance and temporal stability.
Genie3 leads the temporal control score, while Lyra2 has the highest mean control level. EVOKE leads
world-model temporal quality; SolarWM-H3 leads geometric stability and the overall tier, while HY-
WorldPlay has the highest mean geometry level. SolarWM’s camera-conditioned autoregressive training,
distribution-matching distillation (Huang et al., 2026), and retained rollout history offer a possible
explanation for its sustained performance. Figures 32–34 show how the generated views evolve; Section 5.5
examines the corresponding temporal performance.
