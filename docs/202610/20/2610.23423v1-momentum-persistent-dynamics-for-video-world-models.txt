ABSTRACT
Video world models must capture how a scene evolves beyond the current cam-
era view to generate consistent observations when its content reappears. Existing
approaches rely on observation history, static point clouds, or image caches con-
structed from past frames. These representations are often expensive and, more
importantly, cannot update dynamic scene content outside the field of view: gen-
erated vehicles and pedestrians may be forgotten after leaving the field of view or
reappear in physically inaccurate states. We represent an evolving scene through
synchronized videos from reference-camera rigs, each comprising eight cameras
that jointly cover the surrounding scene. We connect these rigs in a graph and
propagate video generation between neighboring rigs to coordinate scene dynam-
ics across locations and time. We introduce Momentum, a two-stage pipeline:
WorldUpdate jointly generates reference-camera videos as a shared memory of
scene dynamics, and WorldInteract grounds camera-controlled video generation
in this evolving memory, allowing moving content to remain represented outside
the target view and reappear consistently. To support training and evaluation,
we develop a CARLA pipeline that captures synchronized videos from reference
camera rigs and a moving target camera, together with an evaluation framework
measuring consistency within the generated world state and the target observa-
tions. Experiments on generated street scenes show that grounding target videos
in scene dynamics generated by Momentum improves dynamic scene consistency
by 126.2% in unseen scenes over static image conditioning, while also improving
visual fidelity. Our models and source code will be made publicly available.

INTRODUCTION
Video world models simulate how visual environments evolve over time and respond to actions,
supporting applications in game generation (Team et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025b; Qian et al., 2026),
robotic learning and control (Gao et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026), autonomous driving simulation
(Wayve, 2026; Basant et al., 2026), and virtual-world exploration (Ball et al., 2025; Team et al.,
2026; Sun et al., 2025). In these settings, the generated video reveals only a partial view of an en-
vironment whose contents and dynamics extend beyond what is currently visible. As the viewpoint
changes, the generated observations should remain consistent with both the scene’s spatial struc-
ture and the motion of objects within it. A vehicle approaching an intersection should follow the
same course whether the generated video follows it, shows it from another viewpoint, or turns away
and later shows it again. This requires grounding generation to a world representation that evolves
through time.
Existing memory mechanisms provide part of this grounding through observation history or per-
sistent spatial representations. Conditioning on past frames preserves local continuity, but a finite
context limits how much of the environment remains accessible (Zhang et al., 2026; Savov et al.,
2026; Cai et al., 2026). Image caches (Xiao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Yu et al., 2025a), depth
maps (Ren et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026), and reconstructed point clouds (Ren et al., 2025; Li et al.,

ever, stored observations describe the scene at the time they were captured. Without a mechanism for
advancing their contents, they cannot provide temporally updated grounding for a moving camera.
The challenge is therefore to maintain a representation that specifies both what exists at a location
and how its state changes over time.
Recent approaches have begun to model dynamics beyond the current camera view through explicit
object-level motion plans or entity-centered simulation within a static background (Wang et al.,
2026a; Duan et al., 2026). These advances support persistent dynamics for selected entities, while
maintaining the evolution of general scene content remains relatively unexplored. We pursue a
representation that evolves scene content across a coordinated network of reference cameras, without
requiring explicit object trajectories, identity tracking, or a static-dynamic scene decomposition.
Seoul World Model (SWM) offers a useful foundation through image-based spatial grounding (Seo
et al., 2026). It conditions camera-controlled video generation on retrieved street-view images, using
geometric reprojection and semantic referencing to anchor generated observations to a particular
environment. These references ground the scene’s spatial structure and appearance, while motion
is synthesized within the generated video. We extend this principle to spatiotemporal grounding:
synchronized videos from spatially distributed reference cameras provide a shared account of both
scene appearance and motion. The target camera is thus grounded in the scene at the corresponding
location and time, allowing different camera trajectories to observe the same unfolding dynamics.
We introduce Momentum, a two-stage pipeline in which WorldUpdate generates an evolving world
state and WorldInteract uses it to ground camera-controlled video generation. The method is pre-
sented on Fig. 1. The state is organized as a graph of panoramic rigs, each comprising eight refer-
ence cameras. WorldUpdate jointly generates videos for the eight cameras of each rig and propagates
generation through the graph, conditioning each rig on geometrically reprojected video from an al-
ready generated neighbor. This couples the evolution of scene content across reference viewpoints.
WorldInteract then generates video along a target camera trajectory using the resulting world rollout
as spatiotemporal grounding. The reference videos therefore serve both as the simulation of scene
dynamics and as their persistent representation, enabling motion outside the target camera’s view to
remain part of the state used to generate subsequent observations.

To develop this pipeline, we build an SWM-based image-grounded model on Cosmos Predict 2.5 and
a CARLA data-generation framework that provides training and evaluation data for both stages. The
framework captures static reference images and synchronized videos from fixed reference-camera
rigs to supervise world-state generation. It also records videos along diverse moving target-camera
trajectories, providing supervision for camera-controlled generation grounded in the reference ob-
servations. These observations provide supervision for generating a dynamic world state and for
rendering camera-controlled video consistent with that state. We develop an evaluation framework
for dynamic grounding, combining visual, camera-control, and scene-consistency metrics to assess
the evolving world representation and the consistency of target videos with its dynamics. We evalu-
ate our SWM-based baseline on real-world driving data and assess dynamic grounding on CARLA
scenes from seen and unseen towns. Generating reference videos introduces an additional inference
stage, trading computation for a shared representation of scene dynamics beyond the current view.
This cost can be amortized across multiple target-camera trajectories grounded in the same world
rollout, while improving dynamic consistency over static image grounding. With ground-truth ref-
erence videos, the Dynamically Grounded WM improves visual fidelity, camera control, and scene
consistency over static grounding across single- and multi-rig settings. End-to-end, Momentum gen-
erates its own reference videos and improves dynamic scene consistency by 130.2% in seen towns
and 126.2% in an unseen town compared with the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on static
images.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce a spatiotemporal world representation comprising a graph of reference-
camera rigs with synchronized multi-view videos, providing shared spatial and dynamic
grounding across camera viewpoints and time.
• We propose Momentum, a two-stage pipeline that generates scene dynamics through graph-
propagated multi-view generation in WorldUpdate and grounds camera-controlled obser-
vations in those dynamics through WorldInteract.
• We develop a CARLA data-generation pipeline for synchronized reference and target cam-
era observations, an evaluation framework for assessing dynamic scene consistency in both
the generated world representation and the resulting target videos, and an image-grounded
world model based on SWM.

RELATED WORK
2.1
VIDEO WORLD MODELS
Building on learned models of environment dynamics (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018), generative video
world models increasingly support interactive visual environments across domains. Genie (Bruce
et al., 2024; Ball et al., 2025), LingBot-World (Team et al., 2026), and HY-WorldPlay (Sun et al.,
2025) enable controllable exploration. DIAMOND (Alonso et al., 2024), GameFactory (Yu et al.,
2025b), and Matrix-Game 3.5 (Qian et al., 2026) develop interactive game-like simulation, while
Pandora (Xiang et al., 2024) uses natural-language actions to guide video evolution. Driving models
such as GAIA (Wayve, 2026) and OmniDreams (Basant et al., 2026) generate controllable obser-
vations for simulation and evaluation. In robotics, DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026a) learns action-
conditioned simulation from human-video priors, DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026) jointly predicts future
video and robot actions for control, and RoboWorld (Jeon et al., 2026) supports robot-policy eval-
uation through video simulation. Cosmos (Agarwal et al., 2025; 2026) provides pretrained world-
model capabilities for physical AI; we build on Cosmos Predict 2.5 (Ali et al., 2025). Our focus
is on organizing generated dynamics into a persistent world state that grounds observations across
target-camera trajectories.
2.2
MEMORY AND DYNAMICS IN VIDEO WORLD MODELS
Memory mechanisms extend generation beyond a short observation window. StateSpaceDiffuser
(Savov et al., 2026) uses recurrent state-space modeling, FramePack (Zhang et al., 2026) compresses
frame history, and Mixture of Contexts (Cai et al., 2026) learns sparse attention over informative
temporal contexts. WorldMem (Xiao et al., 2026), VRAG (Chen et al., 2026b), Context-as-Memory

zation further supports consistency through 3D caches in GEN3C (Ren et al., 2025), surfel indexing

alone does not advance the represented scene through time.
Dynamic geometric methods such as NVFi (Li et al., 2023), FreeGave (Li et al., 2025b), and TRACE

object-motion controls; our model generates scene evolution without prescribed object trajectories.
Persistent dynamics beyond the target view remain relatively unexplored. HyDRA (Chen et al.,
2026a) retrieves motion and appearance cues for hidden subjects, while MosaicMem (Yu et al.,
2026) and Mem-World (Zheng et al., 2026) combine spatial memory with dynamic video generation.
WorldDirector (Wang et al., 2026a) plans object trajectories through an LLM, and LiveWorld (Duan
et al., 2026) advances active entities through virtual monitors against a static background. Momen-
tum instead organizes its evolving state by spatial regions: coordinated reference-camera videos
generate and represent scene dynamics, then ground target-camera observations. This formulation
supports spatial extension through additional rigs without explicit entity tracking or a static–dynamic
decomposition.

METHODOLOGY
3.1
PROBLEM STATEMENT
Given a history of H observations I1:H, the corresponding camera poses c1:H, future camera tra-
jectory cH+1:H+T , and a world grounding G, a grounded camera-controlled video world model
f(I1:H, c1:H+T , G) predicts the corresponding future observations ˆIH+1:H+T , where G provides
additional information about the scene beyond that contained in the observation history, such as
spatial context or previously unobserved regions of the environment.
Existing grounded world models such as SWM use G as a collection of static spatial references,
which may originate from different points in time and therefore do not constitute a temporally evolv-
ing representation of the dynamic scene. We instead model the grounding as a time-indexed world
state SH:H+T , allowing the grounding itself to evolve throughout generation. The prediction can

then be written as ˆIH+1:H+T = h(I1:H, cH+1:H+T , SH:H+T ), where the state sequence is obtained
from an initial grounded state through SH:H+T = g(SH).
In our model, St is represented by N spatially distributed reference-camera rigs, each comprising
eight co-located cameras with uniformly spaced orientations. Thus, St = {In,k

Unlike the static spatial references used in SWM, all reference cameras are temporally synchronized
and generate video, so SH:H+T forms a spatiotemporally evolving representation of the scene.
3.2
DATA GENERATION FRAMEWORK
Training and evaluating our models requires reference observations paired with target-camera tra-
jectories, with synchronized video for dynamic grounding. No large-scale public corpus meets these
requirements, and SWM’s real-world data is static and proprietary. We therefore develop an open
CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al., 2017) framework for static and dynamic grounding, across six towns
with randomized weather, illumination, vehicles, and pedestrians. Further details - in App. A.1.
Static grounding data.
We pair target videos with panoramic reference rigs covering the same
maps. Each rig contains eight outward-facing cameras spaced 45◦apart in yaw, positioned 2 m
above the road. Rigs follow the road graph at 20 m intervals with small random positional offsets.
Captures are separated by 2.4 s, equivalent to 30 km/h travel, allowing dynamic content to evolve
between neighboring captures as in street-view acquisition. Target videos span 36 s at 10 fps along
vehicle (autopilot), pedestrian (AI-controlled walker), or flyover (freely moving elevated camera)
trajectories. References and targets are captured in independent sessions with independently sam-
pled conditions, sharing static structure while differing in transient content.
Dynamic grounding data.
To supervise evolving reference views, we capture reference and target
videos in a shared simulation rollout. A moving target camera traverses a randomly selected inter-
section covered by one panoramic rig (single-rig) or up to four spatially distributed rigs (multi-rig).
Each session spans 300 frames at 10 fps, with synchronized observations depicting the same scene
evolution. To reduce storage, rigs record new observations only when the target camera is within
25 m; otherwise, their latest frames are repeated to retain temporal alignment. Active intervals pro-
vide eight synchronized streams per rig for world-state supervision, while target trajectories revisit
shared content from different viewpoints and headings to supervise grounded camera-controlled
generation.
We collect 20 sessions per setting per town for training and two evaluation sets per setting of 30
sessions each: Test 1 uses unseen intersections in training towns, and Test 2 uses a town excluded
from training to assess generalization.
3.3
STATIC GROUNDING
Our Statically Grounded World Model follows the SWM architecture (Seo et al., 2026). As it is
currently not publicly available, we build an open implementation of it. Details appear in App. A.2.
Backbone and rollout.
The backbone is a Diffusion Transformer operating in the latent space of
a causal 3D VAE, initialized from Cosmos Predict 2.5-2B (Ali et al., 2025). Generation proceeds
autoregressively in chunks, denoising chunk i conditioned on a text prompt P (i) (we use a constant
preset prompt), target-camera trajectory C(i), and H history latents from chunk i −1. Per-frame
Pl¨ucker ray maps, encoded by a shallow convolutional encoder and added to latent tokens, provide
camera poses for history, target, reference, and sink latents. We train under teacher forcing on the
CARLA data of Sec. 3.2.
Retrieval-augmented grounding.
Each chunk retrieves up to K reference images near the target
trajectory, with camera poses and metric depth supplied by CARLA during training and estimated
with Depth Anything V3 (Lin et al., 2025) at inference. Two pathways incorporate these references.
Geometric referencing unprojects the nearest references for each target frame into a point cloud and
forward-splats it into the target viewpoint; the resulting warped video is encoded and concatenated
channel-wise with noisy target latents for dense spatial conditioning. Semantic referencing encodes
each reference into a single latent, appended to the token sequence at a RoPE temporal position
5


separated from the generation window by Gref. Target latents attend to all K references to recover
appearance information lost through depth errors or occlusion.
A virtual lookahead sink adds a retrieved latent at RoPE position ∆VL beyond the chunk as a clean
anchor, refreshed each generation step. References and targets come from different scene realiza-
tions, encouraging reliance on persistent structure rather than transient content. Sec. 4 compares this
baseline with SWM’s reported scores.
3.4
MOMENTUM
Momentum is a two-stage pipeline that generates an evolving world state and uses it to ground
camera-controlled video. The architecture of our method is shown on Fig. 2. The state St com-
prises the reference-camera rig views, advanced through time rather than held fixed. WorldUpdate
implements the state transition SH:H+T = g(SH), generating synchronized videos across rigs to
represent scene dynamics independently of the target-camera trajectory. WorldInteract implements
the observation model h, generating target-camera video conditioned on the observation history,
camera trajectory, and corresponding world state. The reference videos thus serve both as the simu-
lation of scene dynamics and as their persistent representation, grounding observations when moving
content leaves and later re-enters the target view.
Dynamics are represented without explicit entity detection, identity tracking, or re-identification,
so for a fixed rig configuration the generation workload does not grow with the number of moving
entities. The two stages operate independently: we store completed world rollouts in the dataset
format of Sec. 3.2, allowing WorldInteract to consume generated and recorded reference videos
through the same interface. A single world rollout can ground multiple target-camera trajectories,
be inspected independently, and be generated at a different resolution or on a different machine from
the target video.
3.4.1
WORLDUPDATE
WorldUpdate is the component of Momentum responsible for advancing the world state through syn-
chronized reference-camera video generation. It requires consistency both across cameras within a
rig and across spatially distributed rigs observing shared content. To meet these requirements, we in-
troduce the Multi-Rig Dynamically Grounded World Model (Multi-Rig DGWM) as an architecture
for this component, combining joint multi-view generation with geometric conditioning between
rigs.
Multi-view generation.
Multi-Rig DGWM builds on the pretrained multi-view video diffusion
transformer of Cosmos-Predict 2.5 (Ali et al., 2025), trained on autonomous driving data. It ex-
tends its seven-view configuration to the eight cameras of our rigs by adding an extra learnable
view embedding. View-stacked attention exchanges information across cameras during joint gener-
ation, supporting cross-view consistency. We incorporate the warped-video channels of geometric
referencing and the Pl¨ucker camera residual from the grounded model. Semantic reference tokens,
the virtual lookahead sink, and their associated attention masks are omitted: each fixed camera’s
generated history anchors its viewpoint and appearance, while reprojected neighbor videos provide
spatially aligned conditioning on scene evolution.
Multi-rig consistency.
Independent rig generation can produce conflicting depictions of shared
content, including objects duplicating or disappearing between viewpoints. We therefore condition
each rig on a neighbor’s synchronized video, unprojecting its views with depth and reprojecting them
into the receiving rig using the known relative poses. During training, conditioning comes from the
ground-truth video of the nearest other rig, allowing training samples to be processed independently.
At inference, neighboring videos must themselves be generated, requiring a schedule that makes
each conditioning source available before its dependent rig.
Graph propagation.
We construct a graph connecting rigs close within a range to provide useful
reprojection conditioning. Generation proceeds in chunks of one model window, overlapping by
the conditioning frames to maintain temporal continuity. Within each chunk, we traverse the graph
breadth-first from a source rig, which generates from its own history without neighbor condition-

3.4.2
WORLDINTERACT
WorldInteract is the component of Momentum that generates target-camera video grounded in the
evolving world state. It must follow the specified camera trajectory while preserving the scene
appearance and motion represented by the reference videos. For this component, we introduce
the Dynamically Grounded World Model (Dynamically Grounded WM), retaining the architecture
of the Statically Grounded World Model (Sec. 3.3) and fine-tuning it on synchronized dynamic
grounding data (Sec. 3.2).
For each chunk, the model retrieves K nearby rig views as semantic references and reprojects the
nearest reference view into each target frame. Unlike static grounding, geometric conditioning uses
the reference video frame at the corresponding target time, conveying the scene’s evolving appear-
ance and motion. Semantic references are instead sampled at the chunk’s start, providing a common
temporal anchor across the retrieved viewpoints.

EXPERIMENTS
Experimental Setup.
We train Statically Grounded WM, Dynamically Grounded WM and Multi-
Rig DGWM on the CARLA training set (Sec. 3.2) on 6 towns, according to the training schedule
outlined in App. A.2 and App. A.3. We evaluate them in the roles of components of Momentum.
We evaluate on Test 1 and Test 2, as collected in Sec. 3.2. Unless otherwise stated, we use the 4-rig
version of each set and choose 2 views of 1 closest rig for reprojection. We evaluate visual fidelity
- PSNR, LPIPS, camera trajectory accuracy - rotation and translation error and scene consistency -
SCS-static (Bagchi et al. (2026)), which we adapt to dynamic objects in SCS-dyn. See App. B.
Baseline Validation.
We evaluate our Statically Grounded World Model on the publicly available
Ann Arbor driving data from MARS (Li et al., 2024), following SWM’s evaluation protocol, using
masked versions of PSNR and LPIPS (see App. B). This comparison assesses whether our reimple-
mentation provides a competitive baseline despite training exclusively on synthetic data, whereas
SWM additionally uses video interpolated from proprietary real-world panoramas. As shown in
Tab. 1, our model achieves higher mPSNR, lower mLPIPS, and lower rotation error than the re-
ported SWM results, although translation error remains higher. These results establish a competitive
image-grounded baseline for evaluating the benefits of dynamic grounding.
WorldInteract Consistency Evaluation.
We evaluate model choices for WorldInteract using
ground-truth reference observations, isolating target-video generation from errors in WorldUpdate.
We compare the Statically Grounded World Model conditioned on either fixed reference images
from t = 0 or synchronized reference video against the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on
video. Image conditioning reprojects the initial observations into each target viewpoint, while video
conditioning uses time-aligned observations. Across scenes containing one or four reference-camera
rigs, the Dynamically Grounded WM achieves the best visual fidelity, camera-trajectory accuracy,
and static and dynamic scene consistency in both seen and unseen towns (Tab. 2). Supplying video
conditioning to the statically trained model alone does not yield comparable gains, demonstrating
the importance of training with synchronized dynamic grounding.

End-to-End Evaluation.
We evaluate Momentum from static reference images, without ground-
truth reference videos. The baselines directly condition target-video generation on these images,
while Momentum uses WorldUpdate to generate an evolving world state through graph propaga-
tion and WorldInteract to ground target observations in that state. As shown in Tab. 4, Momentum
improves visual fidelity and both static and dynamic scene consistency over both baselines in seen
and unseen towns. It also achieves the best camera-trajectory accuracy in the unseen town, while
matching rotation accuracy but increasing translation error relative to the image-conditioned Dy-
namically Grounded WM in seen towns. Most notably, SCS-dyn improves over this baseline by
130.2% and 126.2% in seen and unseen towns, respectively, demonstrating the benefit of generating
scene dynamics beyond those represented by static references.
Qualitative Results.
We qualitatively show examples of WorldUpdate and WorldInteract, com-
paring static and dynamic grounding, single-view and multi-rig models. In Fig. 4a, it is apparent
that the dynamically grounded model preserves moving objects (cars, pedestrians) significantly bet-
ter across the inference window. In Fig. 4b, the multi-rig model renders objects consistently across
views and predicts their movement, while the single-view model is inconsistent and tends to predict
no movement (both models are seeded with single frame at t=0).
5
CONCLUSION
We introduced Momentum, a two-stage pipeline that grounds camera-controlled video generation
in an evolving world state. Synchronized reference-camera videos represent scene appearance and
dynamics beyond the target view, without explicit entity tracking or a static–dynamic decomposition.
9


WorldUpdate advances this state through graph-propagated generation, while WorldInteract uses it
to ground target-camera observations. We developed the Dynamically Grounded WM and Multi-
Rig DGWM for these roles, together with a CARLA data-generation framework, an SWM-based
Statically Grounded World Model, and an evaluation framework. Experiments in seen and unseen
towns demonstrate improved visual fidelity, controllability and scene consistency through dynamic
grounding. Compared with the Dynamically Grounded WM conditioned on static image references,
Momentum improves dynamic scene consistency (SCS-dyn) by 130.2% in seen towns and 126.2%
in the unseen town. These results open a path toward spatially extensible video world models in
which multiple viewpoints and interactions share a consistently evolving scene.

APPENDIX
A.1
DATA GENERATION IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
All observations are rendered using 1280 × 720 pinhole cameras with a 90◦field of view. Each
capture stores RGB video together with per-frame camera extrinsics, intrinsics, and GNSS mea-
surements; depth is stored losslessly. Data collection is configured declaratively, supports resuming
interrupted sessions, and can be distributed across multiple CARLA servers.
Static grounding.
Panoramic rigs are placed at road waypoints with a random positional offset
of up to 1.5 m. Each rig contains eight co-located cameras with yaw offsets spaced by 45◦and
records both RGB and depth. Target vehicle trajectories use an autopilot vehicle travelling at a
randomized fraction below the local speed limit. Pedestrian trajectories use an eye-height camera
at 1.65 m mounted to an AI walker with randomly sampled navigation goals. Flyover trajectories
follow collision-checked random walks at 2–8 m altitude, with camera pitch sampled around −20◦
with ±15◦variation and additional yaw perturbations. We render 20 sessions per town and trajectory
type.
Dynamic grounding.
Each session is centred on a randomly selected intersection with at least
three approach directions, bounding-box half-extent of at least 5 m, and sufficient nearby vehicle
spawn points and pedestrian navigation area within 60 m. In the multi-rig setting, up to four rigs
are placed on road waypoints within 40 m of the intersection and at least 12 m apart, with one rig
located at the junction centre. The single-rig setting retains only the central rig.
13


We spawn up to 10 vehicles and 25 pedestrians around the selected region. The target camera is
placed at an eye height of 1.8–2.2 m and follows a smoothed, collision-checked trajectory visiting
the rigs in randomized order. The path remains within 30 m of the nearest reference rig in the multi-
rig setting and 20 m in the single-rig setting, promoting repeated observation of shared geometry
from different headings, including U-turns.
Each session consists of a single synchronous CARLA rollout of 300 frames at 10 fps. Every ref-
erence rig records eight RGB and eight depth streams aligned with the target-camera timeline. To
reduce rendering cost, reference rigs are active only while the target camera is within 25 m; out-
side this range, the most recent frame is repeated to preserve temporal alignment. Test sessions use
disjoint random seeds, and Test 1 additionally excludes intersections used during training.
A.2
STATICALLY GROUNDED WORLD MODEL IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Architecture.
The backbone is the 2B Cosmos-Predict2.5 DiT: 28 blocks of width 2048 with 16
heads, spatial patch size 2, AdaLN-LoRA of rank 256, and 3D RoPE. The VAE compresses by
4× temporally and 8× spatially into 16 latent channels. Three modifications are made. First, the
input embedder is widened from 16 to 32 latent channels so that the warped video of the geometric
pathway can be concatenated to the noisy target; with the condition and padding masks, the network
sees 34 channels. Second, separate patch embedders, weight-initialised from the base embedder, are
added for reference and sink latents. Third, RoPE temporal indices are decoupled from position in
the token sequence, allowing references and the lookahead sink to be placed outside the generation
window, and attention masks are added for reference and sink padding. All remaining blocks are
inherited unchanged.
Chunk geometry.
Each chunk covers 77 pixel frames, which the causal VAE encodes to L = 20
latents, preceded by H = 5 history latents, for a 25-latent generation window. We use K = 5
references, a reference gap of Gref = 50 latent positions, an inter-reference spacing of 1, and a
lookahead offset of ∆VL = 5 latent frames. Clean conditioning latents (history, references, and
sink) are assigned timestep 0 under the base model’s per-frame AdaLN.
Training.
We fine-tune from the public Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B checkpoint for 10K iterations at
704 × 1280 resolution with AdamW (lr = 4.8 × 10−5, weight decay 0.001, β = (0.9, 0.999)),
100 warm-up steps, and gradient clipping at 0.1, under the base rectified-flow recipe (logit-normal
timestep sampling, shift 5). Training is sharded with FSDP across 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs at a per-
GPU batch size of 1. To avoid over-reliance on any single conditioning signal, we perturb history
latents with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.1 with probability 0.5, and drop the reference
and warped-video pathways independently with probability 0.2 each.
Inference.
Chunks are rolled out sequentially with a rectified-flow ODE sampler using 35 steps
and classifier-free guidance of 3.0, with only the history latents carried forward; the camera trajec-
tory, retrieved references, and sink are re-derived at every chunk.
A.3
MOMENTUM IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
WorldUpdate: Multi-Rig DGWM.
The rig model is initialised from the released 2B multi-view
Cosmos-Predict2.5 checkpoint, with an eighth view-embedding row appended to its seven-slot table
and the warped-video latent appended to the input embedder, giving 41 input channels (16 noisy,
1 condition mask, 1 padding mask, 7 view, 16 warped). Every pretrained column keeps its index,
so the conversion only adds zero-initialised weights. All eight views of a rig are generated jointly
at 192 × 352 per view with 8 latent frames per view, corresponding to 29 pixel frames per chunk.
Generation is conditioned on a single history latent (h = 1), with the initial chunk conditioned on
one ground-truth frame per view of each rig.
We fine-tune for 10K iterations with AdamW (lr = 3×10−5, weight decay 10−3, β = (0.9, 0.999)),
100 warm-up steps, and gradient clipping at 0.1, sharded with FSDP. The warped latent is dropped
with probability 0.2 per sample, which both teaches the model to generate without a reprojection
source and provides the branch without reprojection conditioning for classifier-free guidance. This
rate is chosen to approximate how often a rig is the graph source at inference.
14


WorldInteract: Dynamically Grounded World Model.
The target-camera model is fine-tuned
from the statically grounded checkpoint described in App. A.2 on the dynamic grounding sessions,
retaining that model’s chunk geometry, optimiser settings, and conditioning dropout. It generates
target-camera video at 704 × 1280 resolution in chunks of 77 pixel frames (L = 20 latents), condi-
tioned on H = 5 history latents. At inference, it uses the same 35-step rectified-flow ODE sampler
and classifier-free guidance of 3.0 as the statically grounded model.
References are bound to the first frame of the chunk’s window. The sink is read ∆VL = 5 latent
frames past the target end, clamped to the length of the available state. During training, a sample’s
geometric pathway is disabled unless at least 95% of its target frames fall within a window in which
the source rig was actively rendering.
Rig connectivity and source selection.
Reference rigs actively render within a range of r = 25 m.
This range is recorded with each session and also determines the edges of the rig graph used by
WorldUpdate. For target-camera generation, WorldInteract reprojects the nearest in-range rig by
default. Rigs outside the active rendering range hold their last frame and are excluded as reprojection
sources.

Results are shown in Tab. 5. A baseline that freezes the conditioning frame achieves the best PSNR
and LPIPS. This is a property of the setting: the rig cameras are stationary, so most of the image
is static in time and visual metrics reward generating no motion. SCS-dyn is affected in the same
way. It is an overlap score, and over one evaluation step a moving object still overlaps most of
itself, so a frozen frame is credited with the overlap the object retains, while a model that moves
it is penalised wherever its motion departs from the single recorded future. Both must therefore be
read together with the staticness gap. WorldInteract stays close to the frozen baseline in staticness,
and inherits its PSNR, LPIPS and SCS-dyn from that. WorldUpdate has the lowest staticness gap in
every configuration, moving the scene by roughly the amount the ground truth does, and pays for it
on every metric that rewards freezing, while preserving static structure best of all generative models
(SCS-st) and exceeding the frozen baseline on it in the seen towns. This experiment thus exposes a
trade-off between a preference for static content and dynamic accuracy across models, and a bias of
the metrics themselves towards the former.
