World-T3: Test-Time Training as Persistent
Memory for Real-Time Video World Models

Recent advances in video world models have enabled plausible interactive environments, yet long-horizon
generation places a fundamental demand on visual memory: the previously observed world must remain
consistent as the model continues to generate. Existing autoregressive approaches often implicitly rely on
the generative model itself to retain such information, leading to spatial memory decay beyond the active
context. We introduce World-T3, a camera-controlled video world model with a test-time training memory
that consolidates past observations into dedicated model parameters. To robustly update this memory, we
propose a recursive least-squares rule that incrementally absorbs novel visual features via online pose-keyed
memory updates. Our TTT memory reads out previously observed scene features from the fast-weight state
by camera pose, enabling consistent generation with constant storage and per-step computation regardless of
rollout length. To facilitate real-time interactive generation with this robust memory, we further distill the
autoregressive model into a few-step generator via dual-teacher distribution matching. On a long-horizon revisit
benchmark, World-T3 raises revisit PSNR from 13.52 to 18.31 dB and reduces absolute rotation error from
3.344◦to 1.075◦relative to the strongest baseline on both metrics, while sustaining 20.6 FPS at 1280 × 704
on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU.

Introduction
Video world models aim to simulate how a world evolves in response to user control, emerging as a powerful interface
for embodied simulation, playable environment generation, and virtual exploration (Yang et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2026c; Bruce et al., 2024; Che et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026a; Shen et al., 2026). Conditioned on a
reference image and a scene description, recent work utilizes autoregressive video diffusion models (VDMs) to allow
1
arXiv:2610.17434v1  [cs.CV]  12 Oct 2026


users to explore and interact with generated environments through sequential control inputs, such as 6-DoF camera
poses and natural language instructions (Team et al., 2026c,b; Zhu et al., 2026a; Team et al., 2026a; Xu et al., 2026a).
However, as these models move from short-horizon generation toward sustained interaction, generating a plausible next
observation is no longer sufficient. The autoregressive VDMs tend to remain locally coherent while gradually losing
earlier scene information once it falls outside the active history context, causing previously established content to be
resynthesized from the generative prior rather than preserved as persistent world state.
Existing approaches attempt to preserve scene information beyond the local generation window by maintaining explicit
3D scene representations (Huang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b), retrieving historical frames via
camera and geometric cues (Li et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026d), or
compressing past features into compact memory tokens (Kim et al., 2026; Bian et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). While
these methods improve long-horizon consistency, maintaining an expanding collection of reconstructed geometry,
historical frames, or tokens often introduces unbounded storage overhead and escalating attention complexity as rollouts
progress. This motivates a complementary question: can a video world model consolidate successive observations into
an online-updated, parametric memory that enables recovery of previous scene content, while keeping memory storage
and per-step access costs independent of rollout length?
To this end, we propose World-T3, a camera-controlled video world model that consolidates observation history into
a fixed-size, pose-indexed parametric memory via test-time training (TTT). As generated video blocks leave the
local context, their pose-content associations are written into dedicated fast-weight memory, which is subsequently
queried by incoming camera poses to recover features of previously observed regions. A critical challenge in online
parametric updating is handling smooth camera trajectories, where highly correlated pose features naturally introduce
spatial redundancy across consecutive frames. To ensure that each update selectively incorporates novel visual content
without degrading previously stored information, we propose Test-Time Memory Consolidation (§3.2.2), an online
recursive least-squares update rule that incorporates the accumulated covariance of pose keys into each write. By
solving a regularized regression objective over past pose-content associations, our method preconditions incoming key
features to isolate non-redundant spatial information. Our TTT memory storage and per-step update complexity remain
independent of rollout length, allowing history to be sequentially consolidated and accessed within a fixed budget.
Specifically, starting from a pretrained video diffusion model (Wan et al., 2025), we develop World-T3 through a
progressive three-stage training pipeline that establishes camera control, learns persistent scene memory, and enables
few-step autoregressive generation (§3). Training draws on a curated corpus of 908K camera-annotated clips from
real-world footage and synthetic environments (§B). In Stage 1, we adapt the pretrained backbone into a bidirectional
teacher conditioned on 6-DoF camera trajectories. In Stage 2, we then convert it into a block-causal autoregressive
model with bounded visual context and jointly train the backbone and memory module to consolidate past observations
and retrieve them by camera pose. In Stage 3, to achieve real-time interactive generation, we design a dual-teacher
distribution matching distillation framework (Song et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026b; Yin et al., 2025)
that compresses the multi-step autoregressive model into a 4-step generator, leveraging both the bidirectional and
memory-augmented teachers to balance local visual quality and long-horizon memory consistency. At deployment, our
World-T3 sustains 20.6 FPS generation at 1280 × 704 on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU with live online memory updates.
Our main contributions are: (i) We introduce World-T3, a camera-controlled video world model that incorporates a
pose-indexed test-time training memory. Through an online recursive least-squares update rule, our model robustly
consolidates historical observations into a parametric memory whose storage and per-step computation remain constant
regardless of rollout length. (ii) We design a progressive three-stage training framework that converts a pretrained
video diffusion backbone into an autoregressive world model with TTT memory, and further turn it into a few-step
generator via dual-teacher distribution matching distillation. (iii) Against five recent video world models, the four-step
World-T3 ranks first on all six evaluated revisit-consistency measures and is the only evaluated model to exceed the
16 FPS playback rate for real-time generation on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU.

Related Work
Video World Models. Recent advances in video generation have given rise to interactive world models that condition
future observations on camera trajectories or action sequences (Team et al., 2026c,b; Zhu et al., 2026a; Team et al.,
2026a; Xu et al., 2026a; Bruce et al., 2024; Che et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). To incorporate
2


camera control, various representations have been explored, including pose vectors, dense Plücker ray maps, and relative
positional encodings that integrate the geometric signal directly into the attention mechanism (He et al., 2024; Wang
et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). While these approaches produce visually compelling results over short
horizons, they suffer from progressive camera drift and temporal incoherence as the generation window extends (Xu
et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). The fundamental issue is persistence: a revisited
region should faithfully preserve the state it exhibited on first encounter, rather than being hallucinated anew from the
model’s learned prior (Lian et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026). A parallel line of studies tackles this desideratum, whether
by reconstructing and reprojecting previously observed geometry (Huang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026a; Wang et al.,
2026b), retrieving earlier frames according to spatial coverage (Li et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026; Xu
et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026d), or distilling the ever-growing observation history into a fixed-size token buffer (Kim
et al., 2026; Bian et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). These strategies, however, face an inherent dilemma: structured
memory grows in cost with rollout length, while fixed-capacity alternatives inevitably dilute earlier observations as
new content is compressed. In contrast, World-T3 absorbs the growing history into the network weights via test-time
training, incurring constant memory cost regardless of sequence length while preserving previously encountered states
within the expressive capacity of the parameter space.
Test-Time Training as Memory. Test-time training casts persistent memory as an online associative-memory
problem that continuously absorbs new observations into a fixed-size parametric state (Behrouz et al., 2026). Successive
works have progressively coarsened the write granularity from a single gradient step per token (Sun et al., 2024), to
objectives amortised over windows (Behrouz et al., 2025), to chunk-level updates sized to saturate modern accelera-
tors (Zhang et al., 2026b). The same principle has since migrated to visual and embodied domains: video diffusion
models adopt it for minute-long coherent generation (Dalal et al., 2025), 3D reconstruction systems recast recurrent
updating as online learning over image streams (Chen et al., 2026b), and spatial understanding and robot control leverage
it to maintain persistent state over long streams or to distill reusable memory from unlabelled demonstrations (Liu
et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026b). Throughout this progression, a shared assumption remains: the
gradient-based write presumes successive keys are diverse enough for a local step to meaningfully separate them. When
keys are nearly collinear along camera poses in a smooth trajectory, each update largely reiterates what the store already
contains. World-T3 addresses this redundancy by solving the minimiser of the accumulated objective, providing video
world models with a memory faithfully preserving scene state across long camera trajectories.
3
World-T3
World-T3 is built on three successive stages from a pretrained text-image-conditioned video diffusion model (VDM).
(i) Bidirectional SFT endows VDM with camera control and adapts it to the domain of freely navigated scenes using
bidirectional attention, establishing high-fidelity generative priors (§3.1). (ii) Autoregressive Training introduces
block-causal attention to enable interactive generation, augmented with persistent test-time training (TTT) memory for
scene consistency (§3.2). (iii) Few-Step Distillation further distills denoising sampling to only four steps for real-time
streaming, leveraging dual-teacher guidance to preserve short-term visual quality and long-term memory (§3.3).

3.2
Stage 2: Autoregressive Teacher with TTT Memory
Stage 1 endows the bidirectional backbone with camera control over fixed-length sequences. Despite strong generative
priors, its non-causal nature precludes interactive and extended rollout. In Stage 2, we convert this model into an
autoregressive VDM via block-causal attention. However, autoregressive generation over extended horizons inherently
suffers from context decay, leading to severe visual hallucinations upon revisiting previous viewpoints. To bridge this
gap, we integrate a test-time training (TTT) memory into the autoregressive framework, preserving long-horizon scene
consistency across rollout trajectories.
Specifically, generation produces a sequence of N latent blocks x1:N = (x1, . . . , xN), each containing B latent frames.

3.2.2
Test-Time Memory Consolidation
Long-horizon scene generation with viewpoint revisits demands that the model consistently recovers previously observed
visual content. To achieve this, we propose Test-Time Memory Consolidation, a test-time training module that represents
each generated block as a key-value association between its camera pose and visual content. As generation progresses,
blocks evicted from the active context window are consolidated into a fast-weight memory state via closed-form online
updates (Sun et al., 2024; Behrouz et al., 2026, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b; Dalal et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b). The
updated memory reconciles new observations with scene history, enabling subsequent generation steps to query and
retrieve long-term visual information using camera pose as the key.
Pose-Keyed Fast Weights. Each transformer layer in the backbone is augmented with a lightweight TTT module as
parametric scene memory. The memory architecture consists of a two-layer MLP: a fixed projection layer W1 that
maps pose keys into a non-linear feature space via ϕ(k) = gelu(W1k), and a dynamically updated readout matrix W2
that stores accumulated scene representations across time. Crucially, memory operations are decoupled from diffusion
noise, as keys depend solely on camera raymaps, while values are derived from clean latent representations.

3.3
Stage 3: Dual-Teacher Few-Step Distillation
The autoregressive model in Stage 2 requires tens of sampling steps per block during generation, which is computation-
ally incompatible with real-time interactive generation. Therefore, the goal of Stage 3 is to accelerate the generation
process to a few steps while preserving both visual quality and long-term memory.
Following Zhao et al. (2026b), we initialize the few-step student via Consistency Distillation, which discretizes the
continuous flow-matching into a 4-step schedule.
Dual-Teacher Distribution Matching Distillation. While consistency distillation enables fast sampling, direct
multi-step jumps tend to degrade frame-level visual fidelity and spatial consistency across frames. To recover both, we
design a dual-teacher framework with Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) (Yin et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2026),
which factorizes the real-data score into complementary guidance:
• Bidirectional Score (sbidir), provided by the Stage 1 teacher operating over its fixed temporal window, enforcing
short-range visual realism and motion coherence.
• Memory-Augmented Autoregressive Score (smem), provided by the Stage 2 teacher with TTT memory, evaluating
latents along extended rollouts to preserve long-horizon spatial consistency.
To compute distribution matching gradients, each teacher is paired with an architecturally isomorphic critic network
trained on student-generated samples. Specifically, criticbidir mirrors the bidirectional teacher for local quality, while
criticmem mirrors the autoregressive teacher with an identical TTT memory module. The generator gradient is then
formulated by distribution matching updates:

4.1
Evaluation Protocol
Revisit Benchmark. To rigorously test persistent scene memory upon viewpoint revisits, we construct a dedicated
benchmark comprising 120 clips with out-and-back camera trajectories sampled from the test set of our curated data.
Each sequence spans 160 frames (10 s at 16 FPS) at 1280×704 resolution, with the camera reversing its path at frame
80. By design, these trajectories force the camera to re-observe the initial environment after a substantial temporal
excursion. All evaluated methods are conditioned on identical initial frames, text prompts, and prescribed 6-DoF camera
trajectories. Appendix D.1 provides more details on the curation and preprocessing.
Baselines. We compare against Matrix-Game 3.0 (Wang et al., 2026d), HY-World 1.5 (Sun et al., 2025), DreamX-
AR (Team et al., 2026b), LingBot-Fast (Team et al., 2026c), and SANA-WM (Zhu et al., 2026a), using their official
checkpoints and code. We evaluate both the Stage 2 autoregressive teacher and the Stage 3 few-step student of World-T3.
Revisit Fidelity. Adapting the revisit protocol of WorldMark (Xu et al., 2026c), we quantify persistent memory
retention by aggregating revisit consistency across four temporal gaps. Since the trajectory reverses at frame 80, we
pair outbound frames t with return frames 160 −t at the identical requested camera pose. Specifically, we evaluate
the zero-indexed frame pairs (8, 152), (16, 144), (24, 136), and (32, 128), corresponding to temporal separations of
36, 32, 28, and 24 latent frames, respectively. Crucially, all four intervals exceed our 21-latent-frame active context
window. We average the DINOv2 cosine distances over these pairs and report one minus this mean distance as
semantic similarity. To assess reference fidelity, we also compute the PSNR between each generated return view and
its corresponding VAE-decoded ground-truth (GT) view, averaging across the four paired frames. We compare all
views at their prescribed timestamps without post-hoc alignment, ensuring both metrics remain naturally sensitive to
trajectory-following deviations. Further details are provided in Appendix D.2.
Camera Control and Visual Quality. We reconstruct camera trajectories from generated videos using VGGT-
Ω(Wang et al., 2026a) and report absolute rotation error (RotErr), camera-matrix error (CamMC), alongside consecutive-
frame rotational and translational relative pose errors (RPE). We align translations using a globally fitted scalar,
preserving the unified initial-frame coordinate system. All camera-following metrics are macro-averaged across
source datasets, with VAE-decoded GT serving as a reference for estimation error. For visual quality, we report the
average across seven VBench (Huang et al., 2024) dimensions: imaging quality, aesthetic quality, subject consistency,
background consistency, motion smoothness, temporal flickering, and overall consistency. Detailed metric definitions
and aggregation rules are provided in Appendix D.2. Statistical significance in paired comparisons is assessed using
exact two-sided sign tests, as elaborated in Appendix D.5.

Revisit Fidelity and Camera Control
Revisits beyond the Recent Context. Our World-T3 significantly improves both semantic return consistency
and pixel-level reference fidelity (Table 1). Compared with SANA-WM, the strongest baseline on revisit fidelity, our

Stage 3 model roughly halves the DINOv2 feature distance (reducing error from 0.0800 to 0.0390) and raises PSNR
by 4.79 dB. Both gains are supported by paired sign tests (Appendix D.5). These two metrics provide complementary
evidence: outbound-return similarity measures the retention of generated content, whereas GT-referenced PSNR
measures agreement with the reference scene. Their joint improvement demonstrates superior faithful scene recovery
ability of our method beyond the active context. Additional comprehensive evaluations across DINOv2, PSNR, SSIM,
and LPIPS (Appendix D.4) show that both our Stage 2 teacher and Stage 3 student consistently outperform all baselines
on the revisit measures.
Trajectory Following. Faithful scene revisits inherently necessitate precisely reaching the requested camera viewpoint.
Relative to SANA-WM, our World-T3 Stage 3 reduces absolute RotErr from 3.344◦to 1.075◦, alongside lower rotational
and translational RPE (Table 1). Notably, several baselines exhibit small consecutive-frame errors with markedly higher
absolute errors, a pattern reflecting accumulated trajectory drift. The lower errors of World-T3 on both local and global
scales indicate more coherent local motion and overall camera following. Together with the revisit results, these findings
show improved scene recovery while executing the prescribed camera excursion.
Qualitative Revisits. Figure 4 compares our World-T3 with representative baselines on out-and-back camera
trajectories. In the indoor scene featuring complex geometries (e.g., staircases, windows, and reflections), and the
outdoor environment containing fine structures, comparing the initial and return views reveals that existing baselines
often suffer from changes in scene layout, object appearance, and viewpoint upon revisiting. In contrast, our method
accurately retains object identities and structural alignment. Additional qualitative results along 30-second real-world
camera trajectories are provided in Appendix A.

4.3
Step Distillation and Efficiency
Stage 3 distillation (§3.3) drastically reduces sampling from 50 to 4 steps while retaining strong revisit performance. The
student model increases revisit PSNR from 16.69 to 18.31 dB and improves revisit LPIPS, with both gains supported
by paired statistical tests (Appendix D.4). Its mean DINOv2 revisit distance is also lower, achieving 72/120 paired
wins (p = 0.0353) against the teacher. Paired tests detect no statistically significant discrepancy between the teacher
and student models across all four camera metrics (Appendix D.7). The primary trade-off is in visual quality and local
consistency: VBench decreases from 75.15 to 74.52, and Local Memory from 0.9442 to 0.9258. The 4-step student
therefore combines enhanced pixel-level revisit fidelity with modest compromises in these localized perceptual scores.
To enable real-time interactive generation, we combine memory-read caching, compilation, efficient attention kernels,
and optimized VAE decoding to reduce the end-to-end latency and peak memory of the model inference (Appendix E).
With online TTT memory updates active, the 4-step student sustains an end-to-end throughput of 20.6 FPS at 1280×704
on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU (Table 1, Figure 1), exceeding the 16 FPS real-time rate. The fixed-size parametric
memory and bounded recent context keep memory storage and per-step costs independent of rollout duration (§3.2).
9


5
Conclusion
We presented World-T3, a camera-controlled world model that consolidates historical observations into a parametric
memory by test-time training (TTT). To address spatial redundancy along smooth camera trajectories, we introduced
an online recursive least-squares update rule that selectively writes novel visual contexts while preventing stored
scene memory from degradation. We instantiated World-T3 through progressive three-stage training that transitions
a pretrained video diffusion model into an autoregressive world model with TTT memory, followed by dual-teacher
distillation for few-step generation. Evaluations show that World-T3 reliably preserves persistent world state, achieving
superior revisit consistency and sustaining 20.6 FPS at 1280 × 704 on one NVIDIA B200 GPU.

B
Training Data
Learning a camera-controllable video world model that maintains persistent scene memory imposes four complementary
requirements on the training corpus: visual richness and diversity, precise camera pose annotations to ground the
control signal, sequence lengths commensurate with the target generation horizon, and viewpoint revisits, whereby a
previously observed region must be faithfully recalled even after substantial intervening content has elapsed beyond the
model’s context. To satisfy these desiderata, we curate a base collection of 601K clips that provides broad coverage of
appearance and camera motion and an augmented set of 307K clips designed for viewpoint recurrence.
B.1
Corpus Composition
Following recently released work in video world models (Zhu et al., 2026a; Team et al., 2026b), our training corpus
draws on both real-world footage and synthetic renderings to jointly cover natural appearance, diverse camera dynamics,
and precise geometric annotation. Every clip is preprocessed into a unified data structure comprising video latents,
per-frame camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and a free-form language scene caption, then resampled to 1280 × 704
resolution at 16 FPS.
Real-World Sources. Captured video provides the naturalistic appearance and complex illumination. Our real-world
subset spans indoor and outdoor environments, encompassing casual orbiting and sweeping motions around objects
or scenes, egocentric urban traversal through city streets, and smooth architectural walkthroughs of interior spaces.
Together, these sources furnish a broad distribution over both scene category and camera trajectory, with an emphasis
on forward-moving, first-person viewpoints in open environments.
Synthetic Sources. Game-engine renderings complement real capture along two axes. First, they supply ground-truth
camera pose free of estimation error, serving as a noise-free supervision signal. Second, they introduce trajectory
patterns such as sustained aerial flight and systematic ground-level exploration that are difficult to acquire in the physical
world, thereby extending the motion distribution covered by the corpus.
B.2
Camera Annotation
To obtain coherent camera parameterization, we apply VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026a) to all training clips, recovering
per-frame intrinsics and extrinsics within a unified scale and anchoring each trajectory to the initial frame as the world
origin. The resulting camera trajectories serve as the conditioning signal for controllable video generation and further
induce a per-clip co-visibility matrix that encodes pairwise surface overlap between frames, quantifying the degree of
viewpoint revisit throughout each sequence.
B.3
Data Filtering
We filter each clip through a two-stage pipeline: objective criteria drawn from the imagery and the recovered geometry,
and a semantic assessment produced by vision-language models.

Objective Scoring. The objective criteria span both visual content and camera geometry, following the conventions
established for camera-controllable corpora (Zhu et al., 2026a; Team et al., 2026c; Xu et al., 2026a; Team et al., 2026a).
Perceptual quality is assessed with DOVER (Wu et al., 2023), which produces complementary technical and aesthetic
scores: the technical score captures low-level distortions such as blur and artefacts, while the aesthetic score reflects
content and composition. To characterize scene dynamics, we compute optical flow magnitude via UniMatch (Xu et al.,
2023) between frames and impose bilateral bounds. Clips whose flow falls below a lower threshold convey negligible
information per unit duration, whereas those exceeding an upper threshold suffer from motion blur and inter-frame
disocclusion. Mean luminance is similarly constrained to a permissible band, eliminating under-exposed footage in
which scene structure is irrecoverable and over-exposed footage in which highlight detail is clipped. On the geometric
side, we retain only clips whose estimated field of view and focal variation remain within the regime where the Plücker
ray representation of §3.1 provides a faithful camera embedding.
Semantic Profiling. Beyond photometric and geometric criteria, we employ a vision-language model to perform
semantic assessment. Video frames are sampled uniformly across each clip and presented to the model, which annotates
the scene along several aspects: the count of dynamic entities (pedestrians, vehicles, animals), the presence of synthetic
overlays (UI chrome, loading screens, watermarks), perceptual blur, and viewpoint characterization. Responses conform
to a structured schema in which factual observations and a concise rationale precede the binary usability verdict,
enforcing a chain-of-thought discipline that grounds the decision in explicit evidence. Final admission into the corpus is
gated jointly on the entity count and the usability label.
B.4
Text Captioning
Captions serve as the textual conditioning signal and are generated by a vision-language model prompted to produce
scene-static descriptions (Team et al., 2026c; Zhu et al., 2026a) in a single paragraph. A scene-static caption characterizes
the environment in terms of its constituent objects, spatial arrangement, materials, colour palette, and illumination,
together with dynamics intrinsic to the scene itself, such as flowing water or drifting fog. Crucially, the caption abstracts
away all camera-related language: viewpoint references, cinematographic terminology, and motion verbs such as
pan, dolly, or fly over are systematically excluded. The guiding principle is invariance: a given scene should elicit
semantically equivalent captions regardless of the trajectory along which it is observed, ensuring that camera motion
is communicated exclusively through the pose conditioning branch and that the two modalities of control remain
disentangled.
B.5
Revisit Construction
Revisits, denoting returns to previously observed regions, arise sparingly in natural video, since a camera traversing
a scene seldom retraces its path. As a result, the co-visibility statistic remains low across most of the base set. This
scarcity hinders learning an effective memory mechanism, which relies on repeated observations of the same content.
To expose the model to such recurrence in real-world data, we construct revisits through a clip replay procedure.
Specifically, we loop and re-index the camera trajectory of a video sequence so that the temporal path periodically
returns to previously observed viewpoints at distinct temporal intervals within the same training sequence. We confine
the replay to static scenes and exclude dynamic entities that change between visits to avoid abnormal movements. For
synthetic data, we further design camera trajectories as closed loops through the rendered environments, so that the
viewpoint deliberately returns to previously visited locations. Taken together, these procedures yield 307K clips with
recurring viewpoints.

C.1
Shared Configuration
We initialize from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, whose diffusion transformer (DiT) consists of 30 layers, a hidden dimension of
3072, and 24 attention heads with a head dimension of 128. All training runs use 1280 × 704 videos at 16 FPS and
BF16 mixed precision. The frozen WanVideoVAE compresses raw input videos into 48-channel latents with 16×
spatial and 4× temporal downsampling factors. Specifically, a video clip of T frames maps to F = 1 + (T −1)/4
latent frames over a 44 × 80 spatial grid, with the DiT processing patches of size (1, 2, 2). Accordingly, 21 and 63
latent frames correspond to 81 and 249 raw video frames, respectively. Unless otherwise specified, all temporal metrics
below, including context windows, sinks, block sizes, and rollout lengths, are quantified in terms of latent frames.
The initial video frame conditions generation via its clean latent at diffusion timestep zero, which is directly concatenated
along the temporal sequence. The standard denoising loss excludes this clean initial frame. Textual conditioning
is injected via cross-attention over cached T5 embeddings, with both the text encoder and VAE remaining frozen
throughout training. Camera control is parameterized using Plücker raymaps injected through camera-dependent
adaptive layer normalization (CamAdaLN).
Optimization is performed using AdamW across all training runs, adhering to the stage-specific schedules listed in
Table 3. With a gradient accumulation factor of one, the effective global batch size equals the per-GPU batch size
multiplied by the number of active data-parallel workers.
C.2
Stage 1: Bidirectional Camera-Conditioned SFT
In Stage 1, we train rank-stabilized LoRA adapters of rank r = 256 and scaling factor α = 16 on all attention
projections (query, key, value, output) and feed-forward networks, alongside rank-512 CamAdaLN modules. All
remaining pretrained backbone weights are held frozen. Training optimizes a camera-conditioned denoising loss with
full bidirectional attention across sequences of 21 latent frames. To enable classifier-free guidance, camera conditioning
is randomly dropped with a probability of 0.05, with the dropout decision synchronized across the batch. We employ
distributed data parallelism with AdamW. The learning rate linearly warms up for 500 iterations to a peak of 5 × 10−5,
followed by cosine decay toward a floor of 5 × 10−7 over the total horizon.
C.3
Stage 2: Autoregressive Adaptation and Memory Training
Block-Causal Adaptation. Starting from the Stage 1 checkpoint, we continue training the same LoRA adapters
and camera modules for 10,000 iterations using masked teacher forcing. Attention is converted to block-causal, with
17


three latent frames per block, and each target block attends to itself and preceding context within a 21-latent window.
We retain distributed data parallelism, AdamW, and the peak learning rate of 5 × 10−5. After 500 warm-up iterations,
cosine decay reaches 5 × 10−6 at iteration 10,000.
Memory-Augmented Fine-Tuning. We merge the iteration-10,000 LoRA weights into the backbone, instantiate the
TTT memory modules, and jointly fine-tune the entire DiT backbone, camera modules, and TTT memory parameters
for 15,000 iterations. The memory parameter group uses a learning-rate multiplier of 15, giving a peak learning rate of
7.5 × 10−4, while the backbone peak remains 5 × 10−5. The backbone schedule uses 500 warm-up iterations followed
by global-step cosine decay with a floor of 5 × 10−6. We employ FSDP2 full sharding for the DiT backbone and
replicate the memory modules across workers.
Each training episode contains 63 latent frames, with three latent frames per autoregressive block, a 21-latent sliding
attention window, and a persistent three-latent sink block. Memory writes use the recursive least-squares readout update
(rls_w2) with regularization λ = 10−3, performing 14 online memory consolidation updates per episode. Keys and
values from clean context blocks are cached and detached from the compute graph. Revisit clips are constructed by
decoding an 81-frame source segment, alternating forward and reverse traversal to form 249 frames, and re-encoding to
63 latents, with camera poses following the same temporal indexing.
C.4
Stage 3: Few-Step Distillation
Consistency Distillation. We initialize the student model weights from the Stage 2 memory teacher, and jointly
train DiT backbone, camera modules, and TTT memory parameters for 2500 iterations on the same training corpus in
this stage. Consistency Distillation (CD) retains the configuration from the previous stage, including 63-latent episodes,
three-latent blocks, 21-latent window, three-latent sink, and the RLS memory update rules. The generation schedule
employs a discretization grid of N = 48, with a 4-step sampling schedule evaluated at step indices [0, 12, 24, 36]. The
teacher guidance scale is fixed to 1.0. All trainable parameter groups, including the memory modules, adopt a peak
learning rate of 2 × 10−6, with 200 warm-up iterations and cosine decay to 10% of the peak value. Gradient norms are
clipped at 0.5, with a gradient-spike threshold set to 3.0. We use FSDP2, seed 0, and an exponential moving average
(EMA) with a decay factor of 0.99, starting at iteration 200 and updated every iteration. The iteration-2500 EMA
checkpoint serves as the initializer for the DMD generator.
Dual-Teacher Distribution Matching Distillation. DMD initializes the generator weights from the trained CD
model while resetting the optimizer state. It incorporates both the frozen Stage 1 bidirectional teacher and the frozen
Stage 2 memory-augmented teacher to provide dual-teacher guidance. Each teacher is paired with a trainable critic
network possessing an identical architecture, initialized via weight copies. Both memory-augmented scoring networks
construct their fast-weight memory states directly from the student’s own generated rollouts.
The DMD training phase spans 800 iterations. Iterations 1–399 use only the bidirectional score term, while iterations
400–800 use guidance from both the bidirectional and memory-augmented teachers. At iteration 400, the generator
learning rate undergoes an additional 50-step boundary warm-up. Critics are updated every iteration, whereas the
generator updates every fifth iteration, yielding 160 generator optimization steps across the run. The peak learning rates
are 2 × 10−6 for the generator and 4 × 10−7 for the critics, with 50 initial warm-up iterations and cosine decay to 10%
of each peak value. AdamW hyperparameters are set to (β1, β2) = (0, 0.999) with zero weight decay and a gradient
clipping threshold of 1.0. We use FSDP2, seed 0, and generator EMA with decay 0.99 starting from iteration 200.
For each training iteration, the rollout length is sampled uniformly from {21, 24, . . . , 63} latent frames and synchronized
across parallel workers. The DMD loss is applied to the final contiguous 21 latent frames, using the same 4-step
denoising schedule as CD. The final model is taken from the iteration-800 EMA checkpoint after the dual-teacher
distillation phase.

D.1
Evaluation Set and Shared Preprocessing
The revisit evaluation benchmark consists of 120 video clips curated from an unseen, held-out test split of our dataset.
For rigorous and unbiased evaluation of memory retention, clip selection is governed by a filtering pipeline based
18


on trajectory geometry. Specifically, we enforce a geometric constraint requiring a normalized angular yaw sweep
(total sweep relative to horizontal field of view) of at least 0.30. The resulting benchmark exhibits a median angular
sweep of 38◦and a median sweep/FOV ratio of 0.46. To guarantee fair representation, clips are stratified across source
domains according to their natural proportions in the dataset pool, mitigating motion distribution bias and avoiding
overrepresentation of specific camera dynamics.
All evaluated methods are conditioned on identical per-clip random seeds, text prompts, and prescribed 6-DoF camera
trajectories. Every sequence is generated and evaluated for exactly 160 frames at 16 FPS and 1280×704 resolution. We
enforce strict frame-count truncation (rather than time-based truncation) to ensure uniform evaluation sequence lengths.
Videos are encoded using standard H.264 (CRF 12, yuv420p). The ground-truth (GT) reference is decoded from 41
cached VAE latents (161 frames) and truncated to 160 frames. Note that GT passes through one video-encoding stage,
whereas generated outputs are re-encoded from their native videos and thus pass through two. This minor encoding
asymmetry can slightly favor GT.

Additional Results and Trade-offs
As detailed in Tables 1 and 4, the student model’s revisit fidelity gains coexist with slightly lower VBench visual quality
scores compared to its teacher. Notably, the decoded GT reference achieves an imaging quality score of 74.00, lower
than several generated baselines, highlighting that VBench imaging quality measures stylized sharpness rather than
absolute distance to real-world ground truth.
Table 5 shows that our Stage 3 student and Stage 2 teacher rank first and second among all methods in most DINOv2
and SSIM metrics for both self-consistency and GT reference. Interestingly, while the student exhibits a higher

E
Inference Efficiency
Our 4-step World-T3 student achieves 20.6 FPS at 1280 × 704 on a single NVIDIA B200 GPU with online memory
updates enabled, as shown in Figure 1b. The teacher uses 50 denoising steps per block and the student uses 4, a 12.5×
reduction in step count. We report measured speedup separately from this ratio. This section describes how the 4-step
student reaches real-time generation on a single GPU. All runtime efficiency optimizations do not change the model
architecture and preserve the numerical fidelity: the student weights, its 4-step schedule, the latent-frame context
window size, and the TTT memory with online updates are unchanged. Appendix E.1 defines the workload and settings,
Appendix E.2 introduces the optimizations, Appendix E.3 demonstrates their measured effect, and Appendix E.4 tests
that the optimization remains numerically equivalent to the baseline. Appendix E.5 compares inference cost across the
baselines of Table 1 on the same hardware.
E.1
Workload and Measurement Settings
Per-Block Workload. The World-T3 student inherits the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B backbone (Wan et al., 2025): 30 DiT
blocks of width 3072 with 24 attention heads, and a causal 3D VAE with 16× spatial and 4× temporal compression.
At 1280 × 704, a latent frame contains 880 tokens. A generation block holds B = 3 latent frames (2,640 tokens)
and decodes to 12 video frames, i.e., 0.75 s at 16 FPS. For every block, the implementation performs four steps: (i) it
encodes the clean context, i.e., the persistent sink and the most recent clean blocks, at most 18 latent frames or 15,840
tokens, under the block-causal mask and caches the resulting keys and values, (ii) it runs the four denoising forwards of
the target block, each attending to the cached context and to the block itself, (iii) it writes the block evicted from the
window into the TTT memory (Eq. 4) and reads the memory at the target pose (Eq. 5), and (iv) it decodes the finished
block with the causal VAE decoder, carrying the decoder’s temporal convolution cache from block to block. The context
is re-encoded at every block rather than extended incrementally: when the window slides, each context block loses
its oldest predecessor, so its hidden states change. Once the window is full, the per-block cost is therefore constant
and does not depend on the rollout length. The fixed-size fast-weight and RLS states likewise keep memory storage
constant (Sec. 3.2).
Metrics. End-to-end latency is the wall-clock time from the inputs on disk (camera trajectory, first frame, and text
prompt) to the last generated frame as 8-bit RGB in host memory. It includes first-frame VAE encoding, text encoding,
camera embedding, sampling, TTT memory updates, decoding, and the device-to-host copy, and it excludes one-time
model loading and video-container writing. We report it directly, as frames divided by latency (FPS), and as wall-clock
22


seconds per second of generated video. Peak memory is the device memory in use, polled through NVML every
50–100 ms during the timed rollouts.
Settings. Every configuration runs in its own warm process on NVIDIA B200 GPUs (180 GB HBM3e): one discarded
warm-up rollout absorbs compilation, kernel autotuning, and allocator growth, and we report the median of three timed
rollouts. Runs execute one at a time on an 8-GPU node. We time two lengths: L5 (81 frames, 5.06 s), the training
horizon, and L30 (465 frames, 29.06 s), where the window has been full for most of the rollout. Our model runs in
default bf16 with PyTorch 2.15 (development build), CUDA 12.8, and cuDNN 9.18.
E.2
Optimizations
We group the optimizations by the source of the saving. Table 7 applies them cumulatively, in the order in which we
added them.
Reusing Features across Denoising Steps and Blocks. The keys and values of the clean context are computed
once per block and reused by all four denoising steps. This KV cache is part of the starting point. We extend reuse
in three places. First, the TTT memory state Mb and the target pose key kb do not change during the four steps, so
the memory feature mb of Eq. 5 is computed once per block instead of once per step, and the output is bit-identical.
Second, context keys are cached before rotary position embedding (RoPE) (Su et al., 2024) because their positions shift
as the window slides, and we rotate them once per block rather than at every step. Third, under the block-causal mask,
the keys and values of a context block depend only on that block and the context blocks before it. Blocks that form an
unchanged prefix of the previous block’s context therefore have unchanged keys and values and are not recomputed: all
earlier context blocks while the window is still filling, and the sink afterwards.
Compilation and Attention Kernels. We compile the per-layer bodies of the denoising forward and of the context
encoder with torch.compile (Ansel et al., 2024), which fuses the elementwise chains around attention (adaptive
layer-norm modulation, gating, normalization) and keeps their intermediates in FP32. Self-attention in both passes uses
FlashAttention-4 (Zadouri et al., 2026), which targets the Blackwell architecture, in place of FlexAttention (Dong et al.,
2025). The block-causal context attention is issued as one dense call per query block over its visible key prefix, and
both calls are registered as custom operators so that they remain inside the compiled graphs. The reference RoPE casts
queries and keys to FP64 and multiplies them by complex128 frequencies, and inside a compiled graph this runs as
separate unfused kernels with FP64 temporaries. We apply the same rotation in real FP32 arithmetic, whose relative
error (about 6 × 10−8) is far below the bf16 resolution of the rotated tensors, so the rotation fuses with the preceding
normalization.
VAE Decoding. We enable cuDNN 9 (Chetlur et al., 2014) convolutions, store decoder weights and activations in
channels-last (NDHWC) layout, and let cuDNN select its fastest engine for each convolution shape by benchmarking
all candidates, restricting the compiler’s autotuning to cuDNN convolutions. The causal convolutions of the reference
decoder also materialize a zero-padded copy of every input. We pass the spatial padding to the convolution itself and
keep only the temporal padding, which comes from the causal cache. Decoding block by block with the causal cache
performs the same convolutions as decoding the full latent sequence at once.
Host-Side Overhead. Two host-side operations left the GPU idle between kernels. The RoPE frequency tables were
rebuilt on the CPU and copied from pageable host memory at every denoising step, five 16 MB transfers per block. We
build them once per window shape and keep them on the device. The per-block Gaussian noise is drawn from a CPU
generator for reproducibility, and each draw with its synchronous copy stalled the stream. A background thread now
draws the noise of upcoming blocks from the same generator in the same order into pinned memory, so the transfer is
asynchronous. Both changes leave the output bit-identical.
Multiple GPUs. In the Pipelined Streaming Rollout, a second GPU decodes block b while the first samples block b+1.
The decoder state stays on the second GPU, so the decoding is the same as in the serial schedule. For higher single-stream
throughput, we additionally split the tokens of each forward across GPUs with Ulysses sequence parallelism (Jacobs
et al., 2023), which exchanges them through all-to-all communication before and after attention, replay the per-layer
loops as CUDA graphs (NVIDIA, 2025) to remove kernel-launch overhead, and split the decoder layer-wise into a
two-stage pipeline over two further GPUs.

F
Limitations
Although World-T3 demonstrates robust persistent scene memory and interactive generation in real-time, several
limitations remain to be addressed in future work.
Our framework relies on 3D foundation models, such as VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026a), to extract 6-DoF camera
trajectories from video corpora for conditioning. However, off-the-shelf pose estimators occasionally exhibit tracking
noise or subtle drift, particularly in challenging visual environments with weak textures or rapid camera maneuvers.
Since our test-time memory consolidation performs pose-keyed retrieval, such inaccuracies in the camera trajectory
can degrade the alignment between queries and fast-weight states. Over extended generation horizons, such pose
estimation errors may also accumulate and lead to suboptimal memory recall during viewpoint revisits. Addressing
25


this limitation necessitates training on higher-quality camera trajectory datasets or incorporating more robust camera
estimation pipelines.
Additionally, while our pose-indexed test-time training memory improves temporal stability and viewpoint revisit
consistency over extended rollouts, maintaining pristine visual fidelity and generating well beyond the training context
length remain open challenges in video world modeling. As autoregressive rollouts proceed significantly past the
temporal window seen during training, video frames can occasionally experience gradual visual attenuation or fine-
grained texture degradation due to compounding errors. Achieving photorealistic high-resolution generation and
unbounded temporal extrapolation demands further advances in scalable generative architectures, higher-capacity latent
spaces, and dynamic context management strategies.
Despite these limitations, World-T3 provides a viable and efficient path toward persistent memory in interactive video
world models, and we hope our TTT memory formulation inspires future explorations in real-time long-horizon scene
generation.
