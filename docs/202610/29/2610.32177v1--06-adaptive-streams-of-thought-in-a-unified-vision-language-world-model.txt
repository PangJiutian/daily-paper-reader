Ψ-0.6: Adaptive Streams of Thought in a Unified Vision-
Language World Model
Khai Loong Aw*, Klemen Kotar*, Baihan Zhang*, Yifan Liu*,
Lilian Chen, Atlas Kazemian, Rahul Venkatesh, Daniel Yamins
Stanford University
Abstract. Humans reason about the physical world by flexibly combining different forms of inference
as a problem unfolds. Achieving this flexibility is a major open challenge in AI: capabilities often
remain separated across specialized models, limiting how they can be composed and jointly adapted
to a task. We introduce Ψ-0.6, a unified vision-language world model that adaptively composes
perception, language, geometry, and simulation within a shared reasoning process we call streams
of thought. These streams interleave images, text, and explicit representations of geometry and
motion, all of which the model can generate and interpret through a shared token interface. Ψ-
0.6 surpasses leading open-weight models zero-shot on multi-view spatial reasoning, with further
gains from reasoning through geometric intermediates. It also supports counterfactual simulation:
generating and interpreting imagined scenes to determine, for example, whether moving an object
would obstruct a pathway. Reinforcement learning from task feedback jointly optimizes the selection,
generation, and use of intermediate representations, improving visual question-answering accuracy
with gains that transfer to unseen benchmarks. Ψ-0.6 offers a path toward adaptive physical reasoning
– not only predicting the physical world but also choosing the representations through which to reason.
1 Introduction
“Solving a problem simply means representing it so as to make the solution transparent.”
— Herbert A. Simon, The Sciences of the Artificial
Humans reason flexibly about the physical world, shifting between different forms of inference as a
problem unfolds. When assembling furniture, we read instructions, change viewpoint, and mentally
rotate a panel to imagine whether it will fit. These abilities are remarkably powerful, in three related
but distinct senses. First, they are unified: diverse capabilities are expressed in a single system.
Second, they are compositional: intermediate inferences and imagined states can be combined
into solutions that no single operation provides. Third, they are adaptive: which representations
are useful depends on the task, and experience can improve how they are selected and used. For
example, words express goals and relations; geometric representations expose spatial structure;
and imagined scenes reveal possible action consequences [1, 2]. Here we ask: how can an AI
system learn which representations are useful for solving problems in the physical world?
This remains a major challenge for current physical AI systems, as capabilities often remain
fragmented across specialized models, limiting how flexibly they can be combined and adapted to a
task. Vision encoders estimate and perceive, vision-language models (VLMs) describe and answer,
and world models (WMs) predict and imagine – each with a separate architecture, training, and
interface. This separation makes it difficult to compose these capabilities within a shared reasoning
process and jointly optimize how these models’ predictions are generated and used through
downstream task feedback. A growing body of work builds unified multimodal models [3, 4, 5, 6, 7],
but these capabilities are not integrated into a shared reasoning process. Another line of work
allows language or vision-language models to invoke external models, tools, or programs [8, 9, 10].
However, the invoked components are typically treated as fixed black boxes whose prediction
mechanisms are not jointly optimized with the reasoner for downstream tasks.

To achieve flexible reasoning, we turn to a powerful recipe that already exists – in large language
models (LLMs): (i) a unified model provides a broad repertoire of capabilities, (ii) a generic

prompting interface makes those capabilities accessible and composable across tasks [11, 12], and
(iii) reinforcement learning on task outcomes shapes the intermediate reasoning strategies used to
solve a problem [13]. Chain-of-thought has been central to the reasoning capabilities of modern
LLMs, motivating the question of whether similarly self-directed, adaptive reasoning can extend to
the physical world. Doing so requires making visual and geometric predictions available not only as
task outputs, but as intermediate operations that a model can generate, compose, and learn to use.
We introduce Ψ-0.6, a unified vision-language world model that adaptively composes perception,
language, geometry, and simulation within a shared reasoning process we call streams of thought.
These are sequences interleaving images and text with explicit representations of geometry and
motion (Fig. 1). Ψ-0.6 builds on the Probabilistic Structure Integration (PSI; Ψ) framework [14],
extending it to language and flexible reasoning. The model predicts images, text, camera pose,
depth, optical flow, and point tracks as tokens with a shared transformer. An any-to-any prompting
interface allows it to condition on and generate these representations in different combinations, so
the output of one operation can become context for the next (§2.2). For example, an image pair can
prompt a camera-pose estimate, while an image and camera pose can prompt a new view.
Unification thus turns representations into reasoning operations: the same model can generate
them, condition on them, compose them, and learn when they are useful. World modeling extends
these streams beyond observed scenes through counterfactual simulation: asked whether moving
an object would obstruct a pathway, the model can translate the specified displacement into an
optical-flow prediction, generate the resulting scene, and answer using its own prediction. Crucially,
the reasoner and imaginer are the same model: predicted scenes are generated and interpreted
within the same token interface rather than supplied by a separate visual generator.
Beyond prescribed reasoning pathways, Ψ-0.6 learns representation-adaptive streams of thought
through reinforcement learning on task feedback. Because geometric intermediates, imagined
views, and text are all actions of the same policy, downstream reward can shape both which
representations are generated and their contents. Unlike VLMs that invoke fixed external tools,
Ψ-0.6 generates these intermediates within the reasoner itself, enabling their generation and use to
be jointly optimized end to end (§6).
Our experiments demonstrate three benefits of unification: strong zero-shot capabilities, composi-
tional physical reasoning, and the ability to learn adaptive reasoning strategies from task feedback.
On MindCube [15], a challenging benchmark of multi-view spatial reasoning, Ψ-0.6 outperforms
leading open-weight models in zero-shot evaluation, with further gains from geometric context and
composed streams of thought (§4). On FlowBench [16], when given a specified counterfactual
motion, its world-modeling capabilities surpass those of dedicated expert models that receive
privileged inputs. In simulation, its performance on counterfactual questions comes close to that of
other leading open-source VLMs, and it transfers to real robot data after training only on simulation
examples (§5). Reinforcement learning yields mean relative accuracy gains over a video-only
question-answering baseline on held-out data and across four unseen benchmarks, demonstrating
transfer of the learned policy (§6). Together, these results demonstrate our core thesis that unifying
perception, geometry, language, and imagination allows task feedback to shape not only what a
model predicts but also how it reasons.

2 A Unified Model with Adaptive Streams of Thought
Ψ-0.6 combines three conceptual components, extending a familiar LLM recipe beyond language:
(i) a unified model provides a broad repertoire of visual, geometric, and linguistic capabilities;
(ii) a generic prompting interface flexibly accesses and composes these capabilities; and (iii)
reinforcement learning teaches the model to direct its own reasoning from task feedback. Here
we explain how these components support capability unification, compositional unification, and
optimization unification, respectively.

2.1 Unified Multimodal Model
Architecture: one vocabulary, one transformer. Images, camera pose, depth, optical flow, point
tracks, and text are serialized as tokens in a sequence processed by our 8B-parameter autore-
gressive transformer. Perception, description, and simulation thus become different conditional
predictions.
2.2 Generic Prompting Interface: Streams of Thought
A generic prompting interface makes the model’s capabilities available as flexible reasoning oper-
ations. Ψ-0.6 reasons through streams of thought: sequences interleaving images and text with
explicit visual abstractions, including camera pose, depth, optical flow, and point tracks. These
representations offer complementary projections of the world, allowing each reasoning step to use
a representation suited to the problem – text, geometry, or a predicted scene.
Any-to-any prompting: all tasks are token orderings. A prompt specifies what is given and what
is requested. Different token choices and orderings express different inference pathways through
the same model. Any modality combination can condition prediction, enabling richly controllable
world modeling over spatial, temporal, and semantic aspects of a scene. Let RGB0 denote an
image, while C0→1 and F0→1 denote camera motion and optical flow. Then RGB0, C0→1 →RGB1
and RGB0, RGB1 →C0→1 express novel view synthesis and pose estimation; replacing C with F
gives motion-conditioned generation and flow estimation. These are conditional predictions of the
same model. A camera pose can therefore serve as an estimate when inferred from images, a
command when supplied to generate a view, or an intermediate when used to answer a spatial
question. In this sense, the model acts as a “multiverse generator” over a “garden of forking
paths” [17]. Prompts control both what is constrained and what is left for the model to infer.
Compositional reasoning: predictions become context. Streams of thought compose these
conditional predictions into multistep reasoning processes, with the output of one prediction pro-
viding context for the next. For example, the model can generate a view from an image and a
user-specified camera pose, then use the generated view as context for answering a spatial ques-
tion (Fig. 2A). Visual and geometric predictions are therefore not only task outputs, but intermediate
operations that connect perception, language, and imagination within one reasoning process. A
prompt can prescribe these operations; their selection can also become part of what the model
learns.
Ψ-0.6 builds on Probabilistic Structure Integration (PSI; Ψ) [14], inheriting two further properties
that make its prompting interface unusually flexible: fine-grained control over individual patches
and probabilistic prediction, which allows unspecified content to remain open to multiple plausible
continuations. We kindly refer readers to Appendix Sec B.1 for details.
2.3 Reinforcement Learning: Adaptive Stream-of-Thought Reasoning
Beyond executing prescribed reasoning pathways, Ψ-0.6 learns representation-adaptive streams of
thought – choosing which representation to use and when – from task feedback. Within the shared
token interface, these choices become policy actions, making the reasoning strategy itself a target
of optimization. We instantiate this approach through reinforcement learning and apply it to visual
question answering. The policy selects visual representations, including depth, camera motion,

2.4 Training Recipe: Pretraining, Mid-training, and Post-training
Pretraining builds a broad base of predictive knowledge, mid-training integrates additional repre-
sentations and data, and post-training teaches task-directed use of the resulting capabilities. In
each supervised stage, we form different dataset groups (e.g., image captioning, video question
answering), each with its own datasets and sequence notations, and allocate a training ratio to each.
Training on these dataset groups is unified under a simple next-token prediction objective. Thus,
sequence construction determines both what the model observes and what it learns to predict.
Pretraining: visual world modeling and geometric integration. The model is pretrained on a
large video dataset spanning natural scenes, human activity, and robot interaction. Training begins
with RGB and camera conditioning and continues with depth and flow integrated into the sequence.
Pure RGB sequences train appearance and motion prediction; interleaved sequences additionally
train relationships between images and geometric structure. Keeping both in the mixture exposes
the model to prediction with different amounts and types of conditioning information. This stage
extends Probabilistic Structure Integration (PSI; Ψ) [14], part of a lineage of models demonstrating
state-of-the-art visual understanding and world modeling across optical flow and point tracking [18],
depth estimation and novel-view synthesis [19], object segmentation [20], physical object discovery
and 3D manipulation [21], and perceptual simulation [22]. Ψ-0.6 extends these visual prediction
and control capabilities to language-grounded reasoning in subsequent stages.
Mid-training: vision-language alignment. The next step is learning language through text-only
data from FineWeb-Edu and DataComp-LM (DCLM), followed by vision-language alignment by
adding images and videos paired with captions, both global and temporally indexed.
Post-training: useful, controllable, user-directed reasoning. Instruction tuning introduces image
and video question answering and text-to-video control of camera and object motion. Post-training
also teaches spatial reasoning (§4) and answering questions by counterfactually imagining object
motion (§5). Pretraining and mid-training sequences are retained to preserve the model’s broad
predictive capabilities. Beyond these next-token prediction stages, reinforcement learning trains
adaptive streams of thought from task feedback, improving how the model selects intermediate
representations for downstream reasoning (§6).
3 Related Work
Unified multimodal models and reasoning. A growing line of work unifies increasingly broad
sets of modalities and capabilities within a single model (Fig. 3). Unified-IO, Unified-IO 2, and

interleave visual and textual reasoning steps [23, 24, 25], while COOPER incorporates depth
and segmentation for spatial reasoning [26]. These works demonstrate that shared multimodal
representations can support not only diverse tasks, but also compositional reasoning. Ψ-0.6 builds
on these advances by integrating vision-language reasoning with physical world modeling through

Tool-augmented LLMs and VLMs. Another line of work enables compositional reasoning by
allowing language or vision-language models to invoke external models, tools, or programs [8, 9, 10]

through the same shared token interface, rather than delegated to separately parameterized

the model’s own computation, enabling end-to-end optimization of the reasoning process; we
discuss the resulting advantages for co-adaptation, counterfactual generation, and task-dependent
intermediate representations in §6.
4 Capability and Compositional Unification: Spatial Intelligence
Ψ-0.6 represents our hypothesis for building unified VLMs with capable spatial intelligence. First,
we start from a visual world model backbone trained to jointly model appearance, geometry,
8

and motion [14], then extend it with language. Standard VLMs typically attach a visual encoder
to a pretrained LLM, whose initial predictive knowledge comes from text [27, 28]. Language
pretraining does not directly supervise how appearance changes with viewpoint or object motion.
We hypothesize that learning these relationships through visual and geometric prediction provides a
stronger foundation for spatial reasoning, with language supplying an interface to these capabilities.
Second, we ground reasoning in explicit geometric abstractions, whereas standard VLMs reason
and answer primarily in text. A single transformer interleaves RGB frames, camera pose, depth,
optical flow, point tracks, and text as a unified token sequence. These visual streams of thought
express spatial structure and motion in intermediate predictions that can be inspected to diagnose
errors. Third, unifying world-model and vision-language capabilities yields compositional, end-
to-end reasoning. Flexible token orderings express pose estimation, novel view synthesis, and
question answering, allowing one task’s output to condition the next. A spatial question can thus
combine capabilities of the same model architecture – estimate the camera motion the question
describes, get to the view that reveals the answer, then read it.
MINDCUBE tests spatial reasoning across multiple views of a scene [15]. It is challenging in two
ways: (i) its questions involve counterfactual camera motions (e.g., turn left) rather than answering
from static images/video, and (ii) its images are separated by large angles (e.g., 90 degrees),
which is unlike how most video models and VLMs are trained. MINDCUBE is organized into three
categories (Fig. 4A), all requiring integrating observations across large viewpoint changes and
reasoning in the reference frame specified by the question.
Zero-shot. Frozen Ψ-0.6 reaches 49.7–51.3% across RGB-only, RGB-depth, and RGB-camera con-
figurations (Fig. 4C). Ψ-0.6 is state-of-the-art relative to open-source generalist VLMs (DeepSeek-
VL2-Small, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, Qwen3-VL-8B), and spatial specialists (RoboBrain, Space-Qwen,
Spatial-MLLM).
Compositional Prompting. Beyond directly answering the question, Ψ-0.6 can combine its VLM
capabilities with its vision and world-modeling capabilities (Figure 4B). Ψ-0.6 uses inverse dynamics:
predict relative camera poses, select the real input view matching the question’s requested turn, and
read the answer from it. Alternatively, Ψ-0.6 uses novel view synthesis (NVS): generate an RGB
frame at the target camera pose and read the answer from this imagined view. Inverse dynamics
requires the viewpoint to be one of the inputs, but NVS additionally allows a viewpoint absent from
the inputs. Using inverse dynamics, Ψ-0.6 reaches 57.7%, compared to 51.3% zero-shot.
SFT. MINDCUBE fine-tuning yields 68.7% with RGB-only inputs and 76.3% with RGB+camera inputs
on the held-out Tiny test split (Fig. 4C). The RGB-only result exceeds the displayed SFT-only
Qwen2.5-VL and 3DThinker baselines. The RGB+camera result exceeds InternVL3’s map-then-
reason configuration and Qwen2.5-VL with SFT+RL, is comparable to 3DThinker with SFT+RL
(76.0%), and trails GeoThinker (83.0%) [15, 29, 30].

Benchmark. FLOWBENCH [16] evaluates visual world models on spatial questions involving
counterfactual object motions. It comprises two splits: FLOWBENCH-SIM, featuring scalable and
accurate datasets in simulated indoor scenes [32, 33], and FLOWBENCH-REAL, augmenting five
robot datasets (DROID, Bridge V2, Language-Table, RT-1, and Berkeley UR5) with counterfactual
motion questions across diverse embodiments and environments (Appendix A.2).
World modeling: Ψ-0.6 outperforms state-of-the-art expert models. Text- and action-conditioned
future prediction is one of the pressing abilities needed in world modeling, with promising appli-
cations like planning in robotics. To improve text-to-flow predictions, Ψ-0.6 employs two powerful
decoding techniques. Critically, these are enabled by Ψ-0.6’s fine-grained control and probabilistic
predictions, attributes that are absent from many other world models. First, before generating flow,
Ψ-0.6 computes the probability of motion (“pMotion”) at every patch with a single feedforward pass.
Then, Ψ-0.6 generates flow predictions sequentially from patches most likely to move to least likely.

Whereas Ψ-0.6 used prescribed reasoning pathways in the previous sections, here we show that it
can learn streams of thought that are self-generated, representation-adaptive, and unfold over time.

optimize the reasoning process end to end (optimization unification).

Learning to Select Streams of Thought Although our framework is generic, here we show a
proof-of-concept specifically for visual question-answering. Given a video v and question q, the
policy emits a notation z selecting from depth, camera-motion, and optical-flow streams (Fig. 6A).
For example, d0, c2→3, f2→3 requests depth at the first frame and camera motion and flow between
two later frames. This turns Ψ-0.6’s generic prompting interface into a policy over reasoning
representations. At a high level, reinforcement learning alternates between two phases (Fig. 6B):
• Collect data with the policy. For each question, sample notations z from the current policy
(initialized with a uniform distribution), and score each by how much it lowers the ground truth’s
loss. We build a dataset using videos from EgoDex [43], Something-Something v2 (SSv2) [44],
PE [45], and OpenVid-1M [46]. Since these datasets provide captions rather than questions, we
convert their captions into question-answer pairs (Appendix A.7).
• Train the policy on the collected data. Initially, we use filtered behavior cloning (rejection-
sampling fine-tuning) on the sampled notations that beat RGB-only to teach the model to emit
notations; subsequently, we use on-policy GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) to update
the policy’s LoRA parameters with rewards relative to the other samples in the group, refining
which streams it selects.
Learned policy generalizes accuracy improvements to unseen benchmarks. The RL-trained
policy improves accuracy on held-out examples from the training distributions (a mean relative gain
of 5.6% over video-only question answering), with the largest gains on EgoDex, the most challenging
dataset. Encouragingly, this suggests that adaptive reasoning may provide greater benefits on
harder problems, where additional intermediate computation is most useful. Critically, these gains
transfer to four completely unseen benchmarks (a mean relative gain of 9.0%), providing evidence
that the learned policy generalizes beyond its training data. Another encouraging signal is the best-
of-k performance is substantially higher than single-rollout (Fig. 6C), indicating further headroom
for policy optimization. The selected streams are diverse and, in some cases, interpretable;
for example, repeated-action questions can elicit sequences of optical-flow predictions, object
interactions can combine depth and flow, and observer-motion questions can combine depth with
camera motion.
7 Discussion
We introduce Ψ-0.6, a unified vision-language world model that turns visual, geometric, linguistic,
and world-model predictions into composable reasoning operations. Through capability, compo-
sitional, and optimization unification, a single model can perceive, imagine, reason, and learn
which representations to invoke as a problem unfolds. Streams of thought support stronger spatial
reasoning, counterfactual imagination, and representation-adaptive reasoning learned from task
feedback. Importantly, the medium of reasoning need not be fixed: models can learn not only what
to predict, but which representation to reason through and when. This opens a path toward agents
whose reasoning continually reorganizes itself across representations as they learn from embodied
experience.
More broadly, reinforcement learning over streams of thought could support a remarkably general
recipe for representation-adaptive reasoning across diverse domains and modalities. Within a
shared predictive interface, observations in any modality can supply feedback for learning how to
reason through others. For example, given a video, a stream of thought through depth, optical
flow, or camera motion could be rewarded by how well it supports predictions of future visual

input. This serves as a label-free, task-independent source of reward without human annotations or
separate RL environments. Once perception and prediction are part of the reasoner, observations
of the world can provide supervision for not only what an embodied agent predicts, but which
representation of the physical world to reason through before acting. Extending Ψ-0.6 with actions
and proprioception would allow perception, physical reasoning, imagination, and control to become
composable operations of a single agent.
7.1 Implications for physical reasoning and learning
Benefits of geometric representations in stream-of-thought reasoning.
First, geometric
representations provide an explicit decomposition of the problem.
One might ask why such
intermediates should be useful when all the information is contained in the original video itself.
But the same is true in mathematical reasoning: all the information is contained in the problem
statement, yet explicitly producing and using intermediate chains of thought [47] can decompose a
difficult computation into simpler steps, improving a model’s final output. Geometric representations
can play an analogous role for visual reasoning, exposing depth, motion, correspondence, and
camera transformation rather than requiring them to be repeatedly inferred from pixels. Empirically,
we find that using these intermediates improves both understanding, such as video question-
answering, and fine-grained prediction and control, such as reasoning through optical flow or
camera motion.
Second, geometric representations can focus learning on the crucial aspects of the physical world.
These are lower-dimensional projections that deliberately abstract away incidental visual detail while
preserving factors of the environment – such as geometry, motion, correspondence, and viewpoint
– that might generalize beyond the particular objects, scenes, or environments encountered during
training. Predicting several such representations from the same experience can also provide a
form of “data augmentation”: a single video supplies multiple aligned, structured views of the same
underlying physical event. Together, these may improve sample efficiency, particularly in regimes
where both the quantity and diversity of data are limited [48], such as robotics, biology, or the
experimental sciences.
Third, these intermediates combine the twin advantages of interpretability and compactness.
“Thinking with images/video” reasons by generating or editing pixels, making intermediate states
directly interpretable [49, 50, 51, 52, 53], whereas latent reasoning compresses intermediate
computation into continuous representations that are difficult to inspect directly [54, 55, 56, 57, 58].
Ψ-0.6 occupies a middle ground by externalizing compact intermediates as physically meaningful
tokens: their semantics are grounded in recognizable properties of the physical world, but they
need not reproduce every visual detail to expose the structure relevant to a computation.
7.2 Limitations
Scale.
Our results establish the conceptual and functional consequences of unifying vision,
language, and world modeling, rather than exhaust the performance achievable by this framework.
Ψ-0.6 was developed at substantially smaller scale in training data, compute, research, and
engineering effort than contemporary frontier multi-modal systems. We expect scaling these
resources will strengthen all of Ψ-0.6’s capabilities; our preliminary experiments already suggest
continued gains with scale, motivating further study.

Learning synergy and specialization.
Ψ-0.6 advances the broader trend toward unified models
by bringing vision, language, and world modeling into a shared reasoning process. Unification can
promote positive transfer through shared knowledge [59], but can also introduce interference when
capabilities compete for limited capacity or require conflicting computations [60, 61, 62]. Ψ-0.6
demonstrates functional unification and inference-time gains from composing capabilities, but we
do not isolate positive and negative transfer among those capabilities during training. A natural next
step is a sparse mixture-of-experts architecture, which could retain the advantages of unification
while managing the cost of a large model [63, 64]. By activating only a subset of experts per
token, it allows specialization and greater total capacity without a proportional increase in inference
computation.
7.3 Key open problems
From visual proxy actions to embodied control.
Our next step is extending the framework
from reasoning about actions to acting in the physical world. In Ψ-0.6, optical flow and point tracks
already function as proxy actions: they specify how parts of a visual scene should move, but do not
directly actuate the world. One route toward robotics would be to train an inverse-kinematics (IK)
model conditioned on flow or point predictions, analogous to RGB-based IK models [65, 66, 67].
More generally, robot joint commands, end-effector poses, gripper states, and other control signals
could become additional token modalities. Policy prediction and world simulation could then become
different queries to the same model, extending the unified interface from perception and imagination
to physical control.
Combining such action tokens with adaptive streams of thought would enable a richer form of
embodied reasoning. Rather than mapping observations directly to commands, the model could
learn when to inspect geometry, predict motion, imagine the consequences of an action, evaluate
progress, seek another observation, or act. Our current adaptive-stream experiments optimize
downstream task performance on visual question answering rather than physical control. In an
embodied agent, reward could jointly optimize what action to take, what reasoning to perform before
acting, and how much computation that reasoning should consume. This would extend our adaptive
streams of thought from choosing how to reason about the world to choosing how to reason and
act within it.
Generic verification for the physical world.
Physical reasoning may provide an unusually rich
source of supervision because different representations of the same world must satisfy shared
constraints. Depth, camera motion, optical flow, point tracks, and appearance should agree across
viewpoints and time, while forward and inverse predictions create additional cycles that can be
checked. For example, a camera motion inferred from two views should generate a compatible
second view when supplied back to the model.
Recent work shows that cross-modal cycle
consistency can itself provide a dense, label-free reward for multimodal reasoning [68]. Such
signals suggest a route toward generic physical verifiers: mechanisms that score intermediate
reasoning through consistency with the structure of the world rather than requiring human labels for
every trajectory.
Autodiscovery.
Verification could, in turn, support an automatic loop for the discovery of reasoning
intermediates. Ψ-0.6 currently reasons through deliberately chosen abstractions whose physical
meaning is known in advance. Adaptive streams of thought learn which of these representations to

use and when; future systems could instead ask which intermediate representations should exist at
all. A model could propose compact intermediate variables, evaluate whether reasoning through
them improves predictive consistency or downstream task performance [69], and retain useful
abstractions within its own reasoning interface. Work on latent-action world models already shows
that useful transition representations can emerge from unlabeled video rather than being specified
a priori [70, 71]. Extending this idea from action representations to reasoning representations
suggests a closed loop: propose an abstraction, evaluate the consequences of using it, and
reincorporate successful abstractions into future computation. In this view, not only the reasoning
policy, but the vocabulary through which a model reasons, can itself become an object of learning.

A.1 Spatial Intelligence Reasoning: MindCube
An interesting observation is that different spatial tasks expose different bottlenecks. MINDCUBE
separates in-place camera turns (Rotation), movement around a scene (Around), and relative object
positions across views (Among; Fig. 4A). Direct RGB+depth prompting is strongest on Around and
near chance on Rotation. Structured prompting improves the displayed Rotation and Among results
most. The SFT configuration is strong on Around and Among but remains near chance on Rotation
– we hypothesize that this is because the Rotation training data contains very limited scene diversity,
making it challenging for learning to generalize to the test set.
Inference protocol for the fine-tuned models. All fine-tuned Ψ-0.6 results on MINDCUBE (Fig. 4C)
use direct answering: the model emits the answer immediately after the inputs, without generating
a text reasoning trace. We fine-tune on the 10k MINDCUBE training questions – which are disjoint
from the 1,050-question Tiny split used for evaluation – mixed with the model’s general training
data. Each training sequence places the question and its answer options first, then the input views
(optionally with depth and/or relative camera poses), and ends with the answer.
A.2 Details of FlowBench
FLOWBENCH [16] is a robotics and computer vision benchmark that evaluates the capability of
visual world models to answer questions based on specified counterfactual motions. FLOWBENCH
contains diverse categories of questions, such as “occlusion” (“if we move the table 0.50 meters
to the right, will it occlude the view of the chair?”) and “closer to” (“if we move the eraser 5cm
to the left, which three objects would it be closest to?”). FLOWBENCH contains two main splits.
First, FLOWBENCH-SIM is built in a simulator [32, 33] and contains diverse indoor scenes with
full rooms depicted, and involves moving objects with various scales from chairs to potatoes.
Second, FLOWBENCH-REAL was built by adding counterfactual motion questions to five robot
datasets: DROID, Bridge V2, Language-Table, RT-1, and Berkeley UR5. It spans five distinct robot
embodiments, with scenes ranging from laboratory tabletops to toy kitchens and kitchen counters,
and moved objects ranging from colored blocks and toy food to cups, bowls, bottles, utensils, and
cloths.
A.3 Details for baselines in flow prediction
Baselines. We compare our model against expert models that can map one RGB frame plus a
language instruction to a dense displacement field. Each is fine-tuned on the same FlowBench-
training set used for training Ψ-0.6, and evaluated on the identical benchmark. We first report a
zero-flow predictor: under a static camera only 0.6% of pixels move, so predicting nothing is already
a fine approximation, and any method whose wrong vectors exceed the true magnitude scores
worse. FOFPred [34] is the closest published match, image plus action phrase to HSV-encoded
flow, fine-tuned with LoRA [72] after raising its magnitude normaliser so our displacements are not
clipped. As instruction editors we fine-tune InstructPix2Pix [36] in three variants differing only in
supervision target (badge-free render, Set-of-Mark render, and a mask-weighted loss on the object’s
before/after footprints), and Qwen-Image-Edit [35] with 4-bit QLoRA [73]. The mask weighting
targets an identity shortcut: rgb0 and rgb1 differ in under 1% of pixels on average, so a plain

Read-out and oracles. Editors emit images, not flow, and correspondence models cannot convert

To improve text-to-flow predictions, Ψ-0.6 further employs two powerful decoding techniques.
Critically, these are enabled by Ψ-0.6’s fine-grained control and probabilistic predictions, attributes
that are absent from many other world models. First, before generating flow, Ψ-0.6 computes
the probability of motion (“pMotion”) at every patch with a single feedforward pass. Then, Ψ-0.6
generates flow predictions sequentially from patches most likely to move to least likely. The intuition
behind this is: in FLOWBENCH, motion typically involves a single, often small object, so predicting
flow for the patches most likely to move first helps the model focus on the relevant region. Second,
our model uses a form of classifier-free guidance (CFG) [37]: at every generated token, compare
the flow prediction logits with and without conditioning on the text instruction, then amplify the
difference – this amplifies the effect of the instruction.
Table 4 shows that pMotion raises accuracy from 31.8% to 44.2% and reduces directional error from
14.1◦to 6.6◦. CFG at scale 1.5 further improves accuracy to 54.0%, lowers rEPEmove to 0.463, and
reduces the blank rate to 5.6%. Stronger guidance further improves flow metrics but compromises
reconstruction: scale 2.5 eliminates blanks while reducing reconstruction gains from +1.49 to

A.7 Question–answer data for stream selection
The stream-selection policy (§6) is trained and evaluated on videos from four sources: EgoDex [43],
Something-Something v2 (SSv2) [44], PE [45], and OpenVid-1M [46]. None of these datasets
provides question–answer pairs. Each provides a caption or label per clip, which we convert into
open-ended question–answer pairs. SSv2 labels (e.g., “covering glass with handkerchief”) and
EgoDex task descriptions (e.g., “Place the lid onto the cup on the wooden table.”) are short and
formulaic, so we convert them deterministically. PE and OpenVid captions are longer free-form
descriptions. For these, we prompt Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [78] with the caption alone, without the
video, to write one to three question–answer pairs. The prompt asks for temporal, spatial, causal,
descriptive, action, or narrative questions that are answerable from the caption and add no new
information.
Unseen evaluation benchmarks.
We evaluate transfer of the stream-selection policy on four
multiple-choice video question-answering benchmarks, none of which appears in training: HanDyVQA
[79], which probes fine-grained hand–object interaction dynamics in egocentric video; TGIF-QA [80],
which tests spatio-temporal reasoning such as repeated actions and state transitions; STAR [81],
which tests situated reasoning about interactions, sequences, predictions, and feasibility in real-
world videos; and EgoPlan-Bench2 [82], which asks for the next action toward a stated goal in
everyday egocentric tasks. The policy selects streams from the video and question alone, without
access to the answer options.

B Supplementary Text
B.1 Preliminaries on Probabilistic Structure Integration (PSI)
Ψ-0.6 builds on Probabilistic Structure Integration (PSI) [14], inheriting two further properties that
make its prompting interface unusually flexible: fine-grained control over individual patches and
probabilistic prediction, which allows unspecified content to remain open to multiple plausible
continuations.
Fine-grained control. Ψ-0.6 represents visual fields as independently encoded spatial patches,
using local patch quantization together with explicit pointer tokens. Each RGB, flow, or depth patch
is represented by content tokens describing what the patch contains and a pointer token specifying
its modality, spatial location, and temporal location—that is, where and when the content belongs.
Because content and location are represented separately, a prompt can address only selected
regions of a visual field, leave the remaining regions unspecified, or relocate existing content by
changing its pointer while preserving its content tokens. This enables fine-grained conditioning and
targeted scene edits. For example, in Fig. 2B, moving a patch on the upper part of a can downward
while keeping the base fixed specifies a compression, whereas moving it upward without fixing
the base specifies a lift. The same representation also allows patches to be generated in arbitrary
spatial order, including sequentially (autoregressively), in parallel, or through a combination of both.
Probabilistic prediction. Prompts specify constraints rather than a single deterministic target.
For any partially specified scene, Ψ-0.6 represents a distribution over compatible continuations,
allowing the same prompt to yield alternative predictions through sampling.
B.2 Supplementary Discussion
Unifying reasoning and imagination.
The value of a unified predictive model becomes particu-
larly clear when reasoning requires considering a world that has not yet been observed. A growing
line of work augments language-based reasoners with external visual generators or world models.
Mindjourney [83] invokes a frozen Stable Virtual Camera [84]; SandboxVLM introduces expert
models and 3D reconstruction [85]; Thinking with Novel Views [86] uses an external “Painter”;
APC [87] chains multiple foundation models; and AVIC [88] learns when to invoke a world model.
These approaches demonstrate the value of visual imagination, but retain a separation between the
system that reasons and the system that imagines. World2VLM [89] finds benefits from distilling
imagined experience into a VLM rather than relying only on runtime coupling. Ψ-0.6 pushes this
integration further: the model that interprets an observation is also the model that predicts its
geometry, motion, and possible transformations. Imagined states are generated and consumed
within the same model and token interface as the rest of the reasoning process. When the reasoner
and the imaginer are the same model, imagination becomes a first-class reasoning operation rather
than a separately invoked tool.

C Model and Training Details for Reproducibility
We describe the implementation of the unified model, its prompting interface, the training of the
stream-selection policy, and the supervised training recipe, following the organization of §2. The
model inherits the patch-based representation and conditional-prediction approach of PSI [14];
here we specify the tokenization, transformer, sequence construction, and optimization needed to
implement these mechanisms. The base recipe describes Ψ-0.6 after approximately 1.2 million
supervised training steps, followed by context-length extension. The initialization of the stream-
selection policy and its answer scorer is described in §C.3.
C.1 Unified Multimodal Model
C.1.1
A common token and position interface
Each sequence element represents one image, a depth map, an optical-flow field, a relative camera
pose, or a text segment. We serialize elements into a stream of discrete token IDs. Each token also
carries a position (x, y, t, c): two spatial coordinates, a temporal index, and a channel specifying the
representation and, where applicable, its code component. The transformer input is the sum of a
token embedding and a channel embedding. Spatial and temporal positions enter attention through
rotary embeddings.
For a visual patch, serialization emits a fixed pointer token followed by the patch’s content codes.
The pointer has token ID 65,000; its position identifies the requested patch, and its channel is
the first content channel of that modality. The same pointer ID is reused across locations and
modalities. Pointers supply the location as conditioning information, and prediction loss is applied
to the supervised content codes.
The transformer has one 65,536-entry token embedding and output vocabulary. Code indices
overlap across modalities: for example, a visual code and a text token can have the same integer ID,
with their channel embeddings distinguishing their roles. Table 5 summarizes the representations
used in the supervised training mixture for Ψ-0.6.

C.1.2
Visual quantization and geometric labels
Local patch codes.
RGB, depth, and flow use separately trained, frozen patch quantizers. Their
encoders apply a Haar wavelet transform within each 16×16 patch, project the patch to 512
features, and process it with three residual blocks using 1×1 convolutions. Each residual block uses
channel-wise layer normalization (LayerNorm, ϵ = 10−5), a hidden width of 1,024, and sigmoid
linear unit (SiLU) activations. Encoding is therefore local to each patch. Finite scalar quantization
(FSQ) maps six scalar components, with (8, 8, 8, 5, 5, 5) levels, to one of 8353 = 64, 000 discrete
indices. Quantizer training uses a straight-through estimator (STE) for rounding. There are four
such codes per RGB patch and two per depth or flow patch. The first code has an auxiliary
coarse-reconstruction objective. Decoders use nine residual blocks with 3×3 convolutions on the
code grid followed by an inverse Haar wavelet transform, so reconstruction can combine information
across neighboring patches.
The RGB quantizer was trained on ImageNet and OpenImages with an L1 reconstruction term, a
DINOv2 feature term, and a coarse L1 term weighted by 0.01. The flow quantizer uses squared
reconstruction error and a coarse squared error term weighted by 0.01. These quantizers are trained
independently and frozen during autoregressive transformer training, which uses precomputed
visual codes.
Depth and camera geometry.
The corpus preprocessing pipeline uses Depth Anything 3 (DA3)
to estimate depth, camera intrinsics, and camera poses from video. Depth is converted to inverse
depth, normalized to a clip’s inverse-depth range, and quantized as a three-channel replicated
scalar image. Reconstruction uses the stored per-clip normalization bounds to convert decoded
values back to depth.
Optical flow.
The flow preprocessing pipeline estimates displacement with AllTracker in two-frame
mode. Horizontal and vertical displacement are expressed in pixels at the preprocessing resolution,
divided by 40 before quantization, and multiplied by 40 after decoding. Each stored field has
an associated frame gap. Training sequences use only frame pairs for which the required flow
field is available. Flow positions carry the source time, while a gap-dependent pair of channels
distinguishes the two content codes and the temporal interval.

C.2 Generic Prompting Interface: Streams-of-Thought
C.2.1
Notation and sequence construction
A notation lists the elements of a sequence in causal order. Node elements refer to one time
index, as in RGB0 or D0; edge elements refer to a pair, as in C0→1 or F0→1. Here D, C, and F
denote depth, relative camera pose, and optical flow, respectively. At inference, an arrow separates
supplied context from requested outputs. For example, RGB0, C0→1 →RGB1 requests a new
image conditioned on a source image and camera motion, whereas RGB0, RGB1 →C0→1 requests
the relative camera pose. Longer streams concatenate these operations, making generated content
available to later predictions.
Training uses complete sequences sampled from a finite, weighted set of notations for each dataset
group. The temporal sampler maps the notation’s indices to available frames and geometric edges
in a clip. Each element is then loaded, allocated a token budget, and serialized. Examples from the
instruction mixture include RGB0, C0→1, F0→1, RGB1 for geometric video modeling; RGB0, D0, T0
for image description; and Q, RGB0, D0, A for answering an image question with depth, where T0 is
a caption and Q and A are question and answer text. Video-question sequences can place the
question before or after the images, and geometric representations can be interleaved with images

or placed between the question and answer.
Token allocation and patch order.
During base training, the 4,096-token budget includes
pointers and conditioning tokens. Text and fixed-length camera elements are accounted for first.
The remaining budget is distributed approximately uniformly in patch count across image, depth,
and flow elements, subject to their available patch counts. The allocator accounts for five tokens
per RGB patch and three per depth or flow patch, with stochastic remainder allocation and a final
length adjustment to meet the sequence budget. Within each visual element, patches are randomly
permuted and the allocated subset is retained; text retains its linguistic order. Short sequences are
padded with masked positions.
Element selection and supervision.
Sampling different notations exposes the model to different
conditioning sets and prediction orders. Each dataset group’s loss mask specifies the conditioning
elements and supervised targets. The first RGB element usually serves as context; the final
global-caption group also supervises it, supporting text-first image prediction. Question-answer
groups supervise answer text, with their image, depth, flow, pose, and question tokens providing
context. The group masks and mixture weights are described in §C.4.
C.2.2
Generating and composing elements
Generation begins by serializing the supplied elements and constructing a template for the re-
quested element. The template supplies pointer tokens, positions, and channels, while leaving
content slots to be sampled from the transformer’s output distribution. Content IDs are sampled
within the configured range of the target modality. The resulting codes can be retained as context
for the next element or decoded into a visual representation.
To accelerate visual inference, the decoder combines an autoregressive patch prefix with parallel
decoding of the remaining patches. It first generates a chosen number of patches sequentially,
caching attention keys and values to establish shared structure. The remaining patches are
processed concurrently, with content codes within each patch generated sequentially. Each parallel
patch attends to the supplied context, the completed prefix, and its own preceding content codes.
This reduces the number of sequential decoding steps needed to complete a visual output. The
sequential budget is measured in patches.
Temperature, top-k, nucleus threshold, patch ordering, and sequential patch budget control infer-
ence. Generic generation defaults to temperature 1, top-k 1,000, nucleus threshold 1, and 32
sequential patches. Text is decoded in token order and truncated at its first end-of-text token within
a specified output budget.

C.3 Training Adaptive Streams-of-Thought
The adaptive-stream extension trains a policy to emit a notation from a video and question. The
policy learns to select and order depth, camera-motion, and flow representations. These selected
elements are assembled with the video and question as context for an answer scorer, whose answer
loss supplies the policy’s training signal.
Policy initialization by scored search.
For each training video-question pair, 64 candidate
notations are scored using the model’s teacher-forced mean cross-entropy on the reference answer.
Candidate selection considers the ten lowest-loss notations and prioritizes improvements over
RGB-only answering. Sampling weights are proportional to exp(−ℓ/0.5 −0.01m), where ℓis
answer loss and m is the number of selected elements. The model is then supervised on the
notation and answer tokens, with the video and question as context. These examples are mixed
with replay of the vision-language training data. The four notation-training sources are EgoDex,
Something-Something v2, PE-Video, and OpenVid.

C.4.4
Optimization and infrastructure
Supervised training uses AdamW, Adam with decoupled weight decay, with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95,
ϵ = 10−8, weight decay 0.1 on matrix parameters, and global gradient norm clipping at 1.0.
Normalization gains are not weight-decayed. The learning rate warms up linearly for the first 2,000
steps to 3×10−4, then remains constant until the final 50,000 supervised training steps, when it
decreases linearly toward zero.
The base training stages in Table 7 use a global batch of 512 sequences, with 4,096 positions per
sequence. Training uses bfloat16 (BF16) autocasting, full-precision (FP32) master weights and
optimizer state, and activation checkpointing with recomputation at transformer-block boundaries.
The implementation uses PyTorch/XLA single-program, multiple-data (SPMD) execution with fully
sharded data parallelism (FSDP) and a causal FlashAttention kernel.

C.4.5
Context-length extension
For tasks requiring longer multimodal sequences, we continue training instruction-trained models
with sequence budgets of 16,384 or 32,768 tokens. We retain the transformer architecture, modality

and text. During this phase, context parallelism distributes positions within each sequence across
devices to manage memory use.
