## ABSTRACT 

Game world models have made rapid progress toward real-time interactive gen-eration. Yet action control is still predominantly implemented as feature condi-tioning, leaving the model to learn from training data how each action should move the visual world. This formulation is poorly matched to a broad class of 

planar-camera worlds , such as top-down and isometric games. In these worlds, player motion induces a geometrically well-defined planar camera displacement. We observe that the key to spatially consistent control in such worlds is to rep-resent the effect of each action directly in world coordinates. As a result, tokens corresponding to the same world location retain the same positional ad-dress across time. These world coordinates further expose a spatial asymmetry in generation: regions covered by the initial observation are geometrically known, while newly revealed regions remain to be synthesized. We therefore propose 

Coordinate-Conditioned Noise Scheduling (CNS) , which assigns token-wise denoising schedules according to this known–new structure. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Game world models are emerging as a promising paradigm for interactive visual simulation (Bruce et al., 2024; Parker-Holder et al., 2024). By conditioning video generation on user actions, they allow users to explore and manipulate generated worlds. Recent advances have substantially im-proved visual fidelity, temporal coherence, and generation efficiency, bringing these models closer to real-time playable environments (Valevski et al., 2025; Decart & Etched, 2024; Parker-Holder & Fruchter, 2025). Yet realistic video alone is not enough for interaction. The generated world must also respond to user actions accurately and in a spatially consistent way. Existing game world models typically incorporate user actions as additional conditioning signals. Depending on the architecture, actions may be represented as discrete (Bruce et al., 2024) or tex-tual (Zhu et al., 2026) tokens, or projected into the model as continuous features (Wang et al., 2026a). They may also modulate intermediate activations through adaptive normalization (Alaya-World Team et al., 2026) or enter through dedicated cross-attention modules (Wang et al., 2026b). Despite their different implementations, these approaches share the same underlying formulation: an action is encoded as a feature, and the generative model is expected to learn how that feature should control the visual world. In other words, both the direction and magnitude of the resulting motion are learned from data rather than explicitly specified by the control interface. This raises a fundamental question: How should action control be represented when its visual effect is governed by explicit geometry instead of being learned from conditioning features? 

This question becomes particularly relevant in planar-camera worlds , such as top-down and isomet-ric games. In these worlds, the player often stays near the screen center while the camera follows the player, causing the static scene to translate almost rigidly in the image plane. In this setting, the effect of an action is largely geometric. Once the action-to-camera scale is calibrated, the ac-tion directly determines both the direction and magnitude of the camera displacement. Yet existing interfaces still require the model to learn this geometric translation from data. As a result, the gen-erated motion tends to regress toward motion patterns observed during training, failing to precisely follow the requested displacement. More fundamentally, feature-based action representations do not provide the model with an explicit world coordinate system. Without such coordinates, the model cannot directly tell whether a current token refers to a world location that has already been observed. When the camera returns to a previously seen location, the scene there may no longer look the same. The problem is therefore not merely insufficient conditioning capacity, but a mismatch between feature-based action representations and the geometric structure of planar-camera worlds. To address this mismatch, we propose ActionRoPE , a parameter-free action interface for planar-camera world models. Instead of encoding an action as an additional feature, ActionRoPE writes the resulting camera displacement directly into the positional coordinates of video tokens. Con-cretely, it converts screen-space RoPE (Su et al., 2024) into action-aligned world-space RoPE, so that tokens corresponding to the same world location retain the same positional address across time (Figure 1). In this way, the action directly specifies the direction and magnitude of the resulting motion, enabling precise and geometric control. At the same time, the world coordinates naturally reveal which tokens belong to the scene already determined by the first frame and which belong to newly exposed regions. Building on this structure, we further introduce Coordinate-Conditioned Noise Scheduling (CNS) . Unlike applying an undifferentiated denoising schedule to all spatial to-kens, CNS assigns token-wise schedules (Chen et al., 2024; Ruhe et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024) according to a known–new partition. Specifically, as the camera moves, tokens inside the first-frame region are denoised and stabilized earlier, while newly revealed tokens keep the original schedule. This allows newly revealed regions to be generated with reference to the already settled scene. Be-sides a 0.015 M-parameter input channel that feeds the known–new partition to the network, both components add no learnable parameters and leave the underlying attention architecture unchanged. We evaluate ActionRoPE against four representative action-conditioning strategies on the same backbone and under the same training setting. Across motion scaling, directional compliance, and 2Preprint closed-loop revisit, our method yields more precise and spatially consistent control, reducing the re-visit drift by 66% over the strongest baseline. CNS further improves world consistency by reducing the revisit error. The same interface also works on backbones of different sizes (Team Wan, 2025; Chen et al., 2025; HaCohen et al., 2024) and transfers to out-of-distribution scenes. This suggests that the coordinate-based control interface is not tied to a particular architecture or training visual domain. Together, our results show that representing action through world coordinates provides a simple yet effective alternative to conventional feature-based control. To sum up, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce ActionRoPE , a parameter-free action-control formulation for planar-camera world models that represents camera motion directly in world-coordinate rotary positions. It turns action control from learned feature conditioning into explicit coordinate alignment. • Building on the coordinates exposed by ActionRoPE, we propose Coordinate-Conditioned Noise Scheduling (CNS) , which distinguishes geometrically known and newly revealed regions. It assigns token-wise denoising schedules according to this par-tition, with only a 0.015 M-parameter input channel and no extra inference passes. • We establish an evaluation protocol for action control covering motion fidelity, directional compliance, and closed-loop revisit. On the same backbone, our method outperforms four representative action-conditioning mechanisms and reduces the revisit drift by 66% over the strongest baseline. The same interface also transfers to backbones ranging from 1.3B 

to 33B parameters and to out-of-distribution scenes. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 INTERACTIVE GAME WORLD MODELS 

Game world models have evolved from passive gameplay generation toward interactive visual sim-ulation, where future observations are generated in response to user actions (Yu et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2026). Genie (Bruce et al., 2024) learns latent actions from unlabeled gameplay, while sub-sequent diffusion-based models adopt explicit controls (Alonso et al., 2024), including keyboard inputs (Valevski et al., 2025; Decart & Etched, 2024), cross-scene control (Yu et al., 2025b), and real-time streaming generation (Zhang et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b). Recent work further incorporates richer states and interaction signals (Lin et al., 2026; Deng et al., 2026; Tong et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). Despite these differences, actions are typically injected as conditioning features, such as latent tokens, additive embeddings, language, adaptive normalization, or cross-attention (Bruce et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026; AlayaWorld Team et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). The resulting visual motion is therefore still learned from data. Our work studies a setting where the action-to-motion relation is explicitly geometric. In planar-camera worlds, calibrated player actions determine planar camera displacement, allowing us to rep-resent control directly through world coordinates rather than learned action features. 2.2 GEOMETRY -A WARE POSITIONAL ENCODING 

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) are widely used to encode spatio-temporal token coordinates in video DiTs (Su et al., 2024; Team Wan, 2025). Recent work has further incorporated camera geometry directly into positional representations rather than treating it only as an external condi-tion (Miyato et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). ReDirector (Park et al., 2025), ReRoPE (Li et al., 2026), SCoPE (Yin et al., 2026), and Matrix-Game 3.5 (Qian et al., 2026) encode camera transformations, viewing rays, or projection geometry into RoPE-like coordinates, improving geometric consistency under viewpoint changes. These methods, however, are designed for viewpoint control in general 3D scenes and take the camera pose or trajectory as input. Our method adopts the same geometric view of positional encoding but applies it specifically to action control. Instead of encoding an external 3D camera pose, ActionRoPE directly converts the action-driven planar displacement into world coordinates, so that the same world location retains the same positional address throughout the camera motion. 3Preprint 2.3 ADAPTIVE DIFFUSION SCHEDULING 

Standard diffusion models apply a shared noise level across all tokens at each denoising step. Sev-eral works instead assign different noise levels to different tokens. Diffusion Forcing (Chen et al., 2024), Rolling Diffusion (Ruhe et al., 2024), FIFO-Diffusion (Kim et al., 2024), and streaming generators (Chen et al., 2025; Sand.ai et al., 2025) vary noise levels across temporal positions, and recent image-generation methods further explore spatially adaptive schedules (Papa et al., 2026; Schusterbauer et al., 2026). Related inpainting approaches exploit known regions through explicit resampling or content injection (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Team Wan, 2025; HaCohen et al., 2024). These methods show that different regions can benefit from different denoising schedules, but the distinction is typically defined by time, an external mask, or explicit observed content. In contrast, our method uses the world coordinates provided by ActionRoPE to define this distinction directly. Locations that are already covered by the initial observation are treated as geometrically known, while newly revealed locations still remain uncertain. CNS then uses this known–new struc-ture to assign token-wise denoising schedules, allowing known regions to stabilize earlier without explicitly copying or reinserting any observed content. 

## 3 METHOD 

As shown in Figure 2, we propose ActionRoPE to turn a pretrained image-to-video diffusion trans-former into an action-controlled world model without adding a control pathway. We first formalize planar-camera worlds, in which the effect of an action is a camera displacement (Section 3.1). We then write this displacement into the rotary positions, so that a token is addressed by where it is in the world rather than where it is on the screen (Section 3.2). The same coordinates also separate known regions from newly revealed ones. Building on this partition, we introduce Coordinate-Conditioned Noise Scheduling (CNS), which denoises known tokens earlier than newly revealed ones during both training and sampling (Section 3.3). Finally, Section 3.4 summarizes training and inference. 3.1 PLANAR -C AMERA WORLDS 

We consider a class of interactive game worlds in which the dominant camera motion is approxi-mately planar, including top-down and isometric games. Given an initial frame and an action se-quence, each action moves the player. The camera then follows the player and the static world translates approximately rigidly in the image plane. Once the action-to-camera scale is calibrated, the visual effect of an action is therefore largely determined by geometry. Existing action-conditioning mechanisms, however, generally treat actions as additional feature sig-nals, whether as tokens, added features, modulated activations, or cross-attention inputs. Despite their different implementations, these approaches share the same formulation: 

ak → Cond( ak) → Video Generator → ∆k, (1) where the model learns from data how an action should translate into the resulting visual motion. Consequently, both the direction and the magnitude of the motion are determined implicitly by the training distribution rather than explicitly by the control interface. This formulation is unnecessarily indirect for planar-camera worlds. Here, ∆k = (∆ xk, ∆yk)

denotes the cumulative camera displacement at temporal cell k, i.e., the k-th latent frame of the video model. For a static world point, its screen-space location changes with ∆k, but its location in the underlying world remains unchanged. Thus, rather than learning the effect of an action as a feature transformation, we seek to represent action-induced motion directly through the positional coordinates used by the video model. Our main observation is simple: in planar-camera worlds, action control can be formulated as coordinate alignment . ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Dataset. We build both training and evaluation on No Rest for the Wicked , a 2.5D action game rendered from a fixed top-down perspective. The camera follows the player, so the scene translates almost rigidly in the image plane. We record 50 .9 hours of automated exploration and cut it into 

22 ,782 clips of 81 frames at 16 fps and 832 ×480 resolution. Of these, 18 ,528 clips are used for training, and 982 held-out clips form the evaluation set. At test time, each model receives only the first frame of an evaluation clip and never its ground-truth continuation. 

Backbones and training. We use Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (Team Wan, 2025) as our main backbone and implement ActionRoPE, CNS and all four baselines on it. All Wan models are fully fine-tuned from the same pretrained weights for 5,000 steps with AdamW, a learning rate of 10 −5 and a batch size of 16 . To examine whether our interface generalizes across architectures, we further change the backbone to SkyReels-V2-DF 1.3B (Chen et al., 2025), LTX-2.5 22B (HaCohen et al., 2024) and MiniMax-H3 33B (MiniMax, 2026) and evaluate under the same setting. 7Preprint

Metrics. For each evaluation clip, the model generates 81 frames from the first frame under a scripted action sequence. We measure the camera motion by matching SIFT features on the back-ground, excluding a 160 -pixel box around the player. Motion fidelity tests whether the camera moves by the commanded amount: on 100 first frames, we command straight walks at 0.5–1.5× the nom-inal speed, and we regress the measured displacement on the commanded one and report the slope and R2, both ideally 1. Directional compliance tests whether it moves in the commanded direction: on all 982 first frames, we command a 5-second straight walk, and we report the median angular error. Revisit consistency tests whether the world stays fixed over time: we command a 2.5-second walk away followed by a return along the same path back to the start, and we compare the final frame with the first frame by PSNR, SSIM, normalized cross-correlation (NCC), DINOv2 feature similarity (Oquab et al., 2024) and residual geometric drift in pixels. 4.2 QUALITATIVE RESULTS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented ActionRoPE, a parameter-free action interface for planar-camera game world models. It writes action-driven camera motion directly into the rotary positions of video tokens, turning action control from a learned mapping into coordinate alignment. Building on the world coordinates, CNS lets known regions settle earlier, so that newly revealed content is generated with reference to them. Experiments show that our method follows the commanded motion more precisely and keeps the world more consistent on revisits than representative action-injection mechanisms. C.2 TRAINING AND SAMPLING 

All models start from the public pretrained weights and are trained for 5,000 steps with AdamW, a weight decay of 0.01 , a linear warm-up followed by a constant learning rate, bf16 precision and gradient clipping at 1.0. The flow-matching loss excludes the first latent frame, and for our models, newly revealed tokens receive a weight of 2. Text prompts describe only the scene, using one of 20 Preprint 

18 scene descriptions of the game, and the scene description is dropped with probability 0.1. At evaluation, all models use this scene-free prompt, the same first frames and commands, and a fixed random seed. Sampling uses the number of steps and guidance scale in Table 7, with one model evaluation per step except for the guided LTX-2.5. C.3 BASELINES 

All baselines are built on the same Wan2.2 backbone with standard RoPE and no mask channel, and are trained with the same data, learning rate, batch size, number of steps and seed as our model. All of them are driven by the same per-frame camera displacement as our model (Section 4.1), which each baseline converts into its own action format; Prompt and Cross-Attn quantize it into discrete direction labels and therefore keep the direction but not the speed. Since they have no known/new partition, they use a uniform loss weight. Newly added layers are zero-initialized, so each model starts from the pretrained backbone. • Prompt (Zhu et al., 2026) appends the action of each latent frame to its text prompt, as one of nine labels (idle and eight directions), and the tokens of each latent frame attend only to their own prompt. It adds no parameters and receives the direction but not the speed. • Linear (Wang et al., 2026a) maps the cumulative displacement ∆k/s through one bias-free linear layer per block and adds the result to the tokens of latent frame k at the input of each of the 30 blocks ( 0.18 M parameters). • AdaLN (AlayaWorld Team et al., 2026) encodes the cumulative displacement and its per-frame increment with sinusoidal features, maps them with an MLP, and adds the result to the timestep modulation of every block, per latent frame ( 66 .5M parameters). • Cross-Attn (Wang et al., 2026b) follows the keyboard branch of Matrix-Game 3, where each latent frame is represented by a four-key vector (up, down, left, right) and the video tokens attend to these action tokens along time in the first 15 blocks, between the text cross-attention and the feed-forward layer ( 98 .6M parameters). Like Prompt, it receives the direction but not the speed. 

## D DATASET 

The recordings consist of 50 .9 hours of automated exploration in two sessions, one with the in-game day–night cycle and weather ( 34 .55 h) and one with fixed daytime and clear weather ( 16 .39 h). The engine logs the player position at 20 Hz, and we map it to screen displacement with a calibrated linear map that also accounts for terrain height. We fit the scale of this map against the background motion measured in the videos ( R2 of 0.99 horizontally and 0.89 vertically). As a check on the action labels, the eight-way movement direction predicted from the logs agrees with that measured by optical flow on 87 .7% of 42 ,456 samples, and on 98 .0% within one neighboring direction. We remove corrupted segments, teleport windows, periods in which the player is stuck for at least 

2 s, and low-light frames, leaving 33 .35 usable hours. These are cut into 20 ,882 clips of 81 frames with a stride of 84 frames and resized to 832 ×480 . The dataset further contains 1,900 transition clips that splice two scenes. Since adjacent clips overlap heavily, we split training and evaluation by entire exploration segments, and exclude 16 buffer segments from both. From the 20 ,983 clips of the training split, we remove one clip with invalid labels, 257 transition clips whose source clips lie in the evaluation split, and 2,197 clips in which the game world runs slower than 0.8× real time, which leaves 18 ,528 training clips. The evaluation set contains the 982 held-out clips with valid labels that run at least at 0.9× real time. 

## E EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Commands. The nominal speed ( 1.0×) is the median walking speed in the training data, 4.33 px per frame horizontally. Motion fidelity uses the first 100 evaluation first frames and commands a straight walk to the right for 80 frames at 0.5, 0.75 , 1.0, 1.25 and 1.5× this speed. Directional compliance commands the same walk at 1.0× on all 982 first frames. Revisit consistency commands 21 Preprint a walk to the right for 40 frames followed by a walk to the left for 40 frames, so that the commanded camera position at the final frame equals the initial one. 

Measuring motion. We detect SIFT features on contrast-enhanced grayscale frames, excluding a 160 ×160 -pixel box at the screen center where the player stays, and fit an affine motion with RANSAC. The displacement between the first and the last frame is measured directly, and through intermediate frames when direct matching fails. Clips whose motion cannot be measured, mostly very dark first frames, are excluded from the medians. 

Metrics. For motion fidelity, we regress the measured displacement on the commanded one through the origin and report the slope. R2 is computed around the mean of the measured dis-placement, so it becomes negative when the measured displacement does not vary with the com-mand. For directional compliance, we report the median absolute angle between the measured and commanded displacement. For revisit consistency, we compare the final frame with the first frame outside the central box. Before computing PSNR, we match the mean and standard deviation of each color channel of the final frame to the first frame, so that global brightness changes are not counted. SSIM uses an 11 ×11 Gaussian window with σ=1 .5 on grayscale frames, and NCC is computed on grayscale frames. DINO is the cosine similarity between the DINOv2-base class tokens of the two frames, with the central box filled with gray. The residual drift is the magnitude of the measured displacement between the first and the final frame. 

Out-of-distribution first frames. The 50 out-of-distribution first frames depict scenes and art styles that do not appear in the game, such as cyberpunk cities, deserts, oil paintings and pixel art, each with the player at the screen center. They are evaluated with the same commands and metrics, with motion fidelity on the first 10 of them. 

## F LIMITATIONS 

ActionRoPE assumes that the static world translates approximately rigidly in the image plane, which holds for top-down and isometric cameras that follow the player. It does not model zoom or strong parallax, and moving objects do not follow the world coordinates. The knownness κ covers only the first-frame footprint, so regions that become visible later are not treated as known when the camera returns to them. 22
