ABSTRACT
Video world models predict future frames from past frames and actions. They
serve as simulators in which agents train, plan or are evaluated, and that humans
can control interactively. They generate in the latent space of a video autoen-
coder, whose compression sets their cost per frame and how long each action
waits to take effect. Yet world models often reuse the autoencoders of text-to-
video generators, built for offline generation with no agent waiting and, without
their training data and code, not retrainable at another compression. We there-
fore ask how, where and how much a world model should compress. We build
CADRE, an open family of causal diffusion representation autoencoders span-
ning multiple spatio-temporal compressions. Its members differ only in a small
bottleneck on frozen features and share a diffusion decoder self-distilled to one or
a few steps. We train the same world model on each autoencoder in two domains,
manipulation and navigation, with two environments each. CADRE forms most
of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera. Our study also
leads to four findings. At strong compression, a diffusion decoder is sharper than
perceptual regression. In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more ef-
fective in the autoencoder than in the world model. Manipulation and navigation
call for opposite compressions: temporal compression costs little when the camera
is still, and spatial compression when it moves. Surprisingly, with a still camera,
coarser spatial tokens hurt motion far more than image quality. Code, models and
a live demo are at https://kyutai.org/blog/2026-10-22-cadre.

INTRODUCTION
A world model is a learned simulator of an environment (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018). Given past
frames and an agent’s actions, a video world model generates the next frames, and can be used to
train agents (Hafner et al., 2025; Alonso et al., 2024), evaluate robot policies (Yang et al., 2024),
plan the outcome of actions (Bar et al., 2025), or simply play a game (Bruce et al., 2024; Valevski
et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026). To enable all these applications, two different aspects have to be
optimized: the throughput that measures the number of frames generated per second, and the latency
that measures the delay between the action and its effect on the output. Throughput limits how fast
a simulation can happen, whereas latency limits how reactive and faithful a simulation can be.
Most video world models operate in the latent space of a video autoencoder (Bruce et al., 2024;
Valevski et al., 2025). To avoid designing an ad hoc autoencoder, many methods (He et al., 2025;
Li et al., 2025a; Zhu et al., 2026a) reuse the autoencoder of a text-to-video generator such as LTX-
2 (HaCohen et al., 2026), Wan 2.2 (Wan et al., 2025) or HunyuanVideo (Kong et al., 2024). They
thus inherit the throughput and latency that come with it. Yet, such autoencoders were not designed
with the throughput-latency trade-off of world models in mind, and often perform massive temporal
compression since it has low impact on visual quality. A typical 8× temporal compression delays
every action by at least 400 ms at 20 frames per second. Humans notice when their own movement
appears more than about 230 ms late (Shimada et al., 2009), and interaction feels immediate only
below 100 ms (Miller, 1968; Claypool & Claypool, 2006). This raises the question of how other
throughput-latency trade-offs affect rollout quality and action following.
To address this question, we propose CADRE, an open family of causal diffusion representation
autoencoders built for interactive world models. Its members differ only in a small bottleneck on
frozen features, which sets the compression, and share a causal diffusion decoder that needs one or a
few steps. To study compression across settings, we train the same world model, a causal diffusion
transformer following MIRA (Hu et al., 2026), on each autoencoder, in two domains: manipulation
(by a robot and by human hands) and navigation (driving and indoor). We compare CADRE with
the autoencoders of Wan 2.2, Cosmos (NVIDIA et al., 2025) and LTX-2, the image autoencoder
DC-AE (Chen et al., 2025b) and the MIRA autoencoder retrained on our data. CADRE forms most
of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera (Figure 1).
Interestingly, our study points to four findings. (1) At strong compression, a diffusion decoder is
sharper than perceptual regression, and a few-step flow model trained by progressive self-distillation
is a good fit. (2) In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more effective in the autoencoder
than in the world model: patching inside the world model loses detail, and at eight frames also
action following. (3) Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions. In manipulation
the camera is still, so temporal compression costs little while spatial compression is costly. In
navigation the camera moves and the balance reverses, so it pays to keep frames and compress
spatially, the low-latency choice. (4) Surprisingly, with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt
motion far more than image quality, and, in robot manipulation, action following too.
Contributions.

Compression sets the world model’s cost. The world model we study is a causal, space-time-

current action or action chunk (Section 4, Appendix D). Its cost grows with the number of tokens it

becomes N = (T/τ)(H/s)(W/s) latent tokens for the world model.
A latent frame waits for all its actions. Every frame registers one action. A latent frame spans τ
frames, so the world model can predict it only once all τ conditioning actions are known. It therefore
consumes actions in chunks, and the actions of one chunk shape the next (Figure 2). The agent thus
commits to τ actions before seeing their effect, as action-chunking policies do (Zhao et al., 2023;
Chi et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025). Causality holds between chunks. Within a chunk, the τ frames
decode from the same latent, and their order is learned from real video.
Temporal compression delays every action. The first action of a chunk waits τ/f for the chunk to

3
CADRE: CAUSAL DIFFUSION REPRESENTATION AUTOENCODERS
Studying compression means training the autoencoder at many compression points. The autoen-
coders of text-to-video generators release their weights but not their training data or code, so they
cannot be retrained. We instead design an open family of autoencoders for world models, one per
compression point (Figure 3). Its members share one recipe, differ only in their compression and
are each trained from scratch, so the world models trained on them differ by compression alone.
3.1
ARCHITECTURE
Frozen features and one trained layer encode the clip. As in MIRA (Hu et al., 2026), the encoder
is that of a representation autoencoder (Zheng et al., 2026). A frozen DINOv3 extractor (Sim´eoni
et al., 2026) encodes each frame into one feature per p×p patch, aggregating several layers as in
RAEv2 (Singh et al., 2026). One trained 3D convolution with kernel (τ, k, k) and stride (τ, s′, s′)
compresses τ frames of s′2 patches into a deterministic latent, with s = p s′ (Appendix B).
A causal diffusion decoder keeps textures sharp. A strongly compressed latent keeps a scene’s

ranges from 2 dimensions for driving to 102 for two hands. All videos are resampled to 20 frames

One world model recipe serves every autoencoder. The world model is a 1.2B-parameter causal
diffusion transformer conditioned on the τ actions of each latent frame and trained with diffusion
forcing (Chen et al., 2024a) (Appendix D). Every autoencoder, CADRE included, uses the same
recipe from MIRA (Hu et al., 2026), with its latents standardized by their own statistics. Follow-
ing SD3 (Esser et al., 2024) and RAE (Zheng et al., 2026), our tuning includes shifting the noise
schedule with each autoencoder’s latent size and token count.
Rollouts are scored on image and motion quality, and on action following. Each world model
receives 40 real frames (2 s) with their actions and generates the next 96 (4.8 s) under the recorded
actions, on 2048 held-out samples per environment shared across autoencoders. We score the last
16 decoded frames. FID (Heusel et al., 2017) measures image quality and FVD (Unterthiner et al.,
2019) also penalizes implausible motion and temporal seams. PSNR and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018)
measure closeness to the recorded future; PSNR should always be accompanied by distributional
metrics, as it rewards blur. Action following is measured by the action recoverability ratio (ARR),
which extends the inverse dynamics evaluation of MIRA (Hu et al., 2026): a frozen probe reads
the actions back from the rollout, and their coefficient of determination (R2) is normalized by the
probe’s R2 on real video, so an ARR of one means real-video recoverability (Appendix E).
We compare seven CADRE autoencoders with five recent ones. The seven differ only in their
compression τ×s×s, and we name each CADRE-τ×s×s (Figure 12, Appendix G). Of the others,
four are off the shelf and used at their native compression: Wan 2.2 (Wan et al., 2025) (4×16×16),
Cosmos (NVIDIA et al., 2025) (8×8×8), LTX-2 (HaCohen et al., 2026) (8×32×32) and the image
autoencoder DC-AE (Chen et al., 2025b) (1×32×32). The fifth retrains the MIRA recipe (Hu et al.,
2026) on our data at 2×32×32. The LTX-2 decoder reads future latents, so we decode each prefix
and keep its newest frames, as a streaming system would. The generators they come from train on
far more video, such as 20M curated hours for Cosmos, likely including many datasets used here.
4.1
SHOULD THE DECODER USE PERCEPTUAL REGRESSION OR DIFFUSION?
A decoder can regress one video from the latent or sample one by diffusion. MIRA (Hu et al., 2026)
takes the first route: it replaces the adversarial losses used since VQGAN (Esser et al., 2021) with
perceptual losses (LPIPS and DINO) and works well on Rocket League. It is also the only autoen-
coder besides CADRE that runs at 20 frames per second within the 230 ms lag humans notice (Fig-
ure 1). To test this route beyond games, we retrain the MIRA recipe on our mixture (Appendix C) at
2×32×32 with our extractor and budget (Figure 4a). Against the CADRE decoder, it gives higher
PSNR but worse FID and FVD in every environment. In one step, the CADRE decoder lowers FVD
by 1.4 to 2.5× with similar action following. A second step lowers FID by up to 23%.

Should the decoder use perceptual regression or diffusion?
Decode a compressed latent by diffusion. Such a latent fits many videos. Perceptual regression outputs
their blurry average, while diffusion outputs one sharp video. Self-distilled, it decodes in one step as fast
as regression with 1.4 to 2.5× lower FVD and similar action following; a second step improves it further.
4.2
HOW DO THE AUTOENCODERS TRADE QUALITY FOR LATENCY?
To compare the autoencoders on both axes at once, we plot rollout quality against action latency
(Figure 4b for robot manipulation, Figure 1 for driving, Figure 14 for all); an autoencoder lies on
the Pareto frontier when no other is both faster and better. CADRE autoencoders form most of this
frontier in every environment. In 42 of the 48 comparisons with MIRA, Wan 2.2, Cosmos and LTX-
2 (four environments; FID, FVD and ARR), a CADRE autoencoder is at least as fast and better by
more than 2%; DC-AE, faster than all, is compared below. With two decoding steps, the best FID
of every environment belongs to CADRE, most often to CADRE-4×16×16, and so does the best
FVD, except in robot manipulation, where CADRE and Cosmos tie. The gap widens with a moving
camera: in navigation, all CADRE autoencoders but one beat every other on FID and FVD, while
Wan 2.2’s FVD grows from 53 in robot manipulation to 600 or more in navigation and LTX-2’s ARR
falls to 0.04 indoors. CADRE also drifts least in 64 s robot manipulation rollouts (Appendix H).
At the fast end, CADRE-1×48×48 reacts about as fast as DC-AE and beats it in navigation; DC-AE
wins in manipulation. Few autoencoders are both fluid and responsive: streaming on one H100 with
eight denoising steps, only CADRE-2×32×32, CADRE-2×16×16 and MIRA run at 20 frames per
second under the 230 ms lag humans notice (Shimada et al., 2009) (Figure 1). The world models
of Figure 5 run fluidly, at 29 to 58 frames per second (Appendix D), and, from the same context,
follow actions never recorded with it, such as holding still or turning the other way (Figure 22).

HOW TO COMPRESS FOR INTERACTIVE WORLD MODELS
We now ask three questions: which component should compress temporally, how many frames one
latent should span, and what coarser spatial tokens trade for their speed. Comparisons follow the
protocol of Section 4, so arms differ only in how the video is compressed.
5.1
SHOULD THE AUTOENCODER OR THE WORLD MODEL COMPRESS TEMPORALLY?
Temporal compression can happen in the autoencoder or in the world model. The alternative to
CADRE is an image autoencoder that leaves frames temporally uncompressed, with the world model
patching its latent temporally. Both routes give the same token grid, but a different model learns the
compression. In robot manipulation, we reach each grid a second time by patching the latent of
CADRE-1×16×16 inside the world model (Figure 6). Only the compression changes. Actions and
noise levels stay per frame, and a patched token receives the actions of every frame it covers.

5.2
HOW MANY FRAMES SHOULD ONE LATENT SPAN?
To separate temporal compression from the cost of coarser tokens, we vary τ in two sweeps (Fig-
ure 7). The first holds s = 16 and isolates temporal compression. The second holds about 1100
tokens per 16-frame clip, so at a given τ its gap to the first measures the cost of coarser spatial
tokens. All world models are matched in training steps and total training frames.
Latents should span many redundant frames in manipulation and few in navigation. In manip-
ulation, the camera is mostly still, so consecutive frames are redundant and one latent can span many
of them at no cost (green curve in Figure 7). In navigation, the camera moves and the view shifts by
more than a token within one long latent (Appendix I), so long latents degrade motion, raising FVD
by up to 64% over two-frame latents. Action following changes less (Appendix G). Camera motion
is our leading explanation, though the domains also differ in actions and scenes.
In 64 s robot manipulation rollouts, long latents do not make errors accumulate: CADRE-8×16×16
is best from about 13 s on and ends with the lowest FVD (Appendix H). Long latents do leave
seams, visible jumps between chunks. A boundary excess compares the frame-to-frame change
across chunk boundaries with the change within chunks, relative to real video (Appendix K). It
grows with τ in all four environments, to 1.6–1.8 at τ = 8 (Table 10). With a moving view, the
seams also show in FVD, which grows 1.6 to 2.1× more at τ = 8 than at τ = 2.
At a fixed token budget, navigation favors few frames per latent and manipulation many.
Along the orange curve of Figure 7, autoencoders give about 1100 tokens per 16-frame clip. In
navigation, the low-latency CADRE-2×32×32 is best, with two frames and coarser tokens. In ma-
nipulation, CADRE-8×16×16 gives the most plausible motion, with eight frames and fine tokens.
How many frames should one latent span?
Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions. In manipulation the camera is still, so
each token sees temporally redundant content: temporal compression costs little, but spatial compression
loses the small objects being handled. In navigation the camera moves, so each token sees new content:
temporal compression shows seams, but spatial compression costs little because the whole view shifts.
5.3
WHAT DO COARSER SPATIAL TOKENS TRADE FOR THEIR SPEED?
At a given τ, coarser spatial tokens cut the world model’s cost, and the gap between the two sweeps
isolates what they lose in quality and action following.
8


Fine spatial tokens matter most when the action is small and stays in view. In robot manipula-
tion, the action shows in a small region that stays in view. At τ = 2, coarser spatial tokens multiply
FVD by 2.4 and lower action following, with almost no change in FID (orange against green in
Figure 7). In navigation, the action moves the whole frame, which coarse tokens already capture,
and the gap nearly vanishes (Appendix G). FID may miss errors confined to small regions, but ARR,
which reads the motion itself, drops too (Appendix G).
What do coarser spatial tokens trade for their speed?
Surprisingly, coarser tokens hurt motion and action following far more than image quality, above
all in manipulation. In manipulation, per-frame quality barely changes, but motion degrades, and so
does action following in robot manipulation. In navigation, where the whole view shifts, they cost little.

RELATED WORK
World models and their autoencoders. Video world models serve driving (Russell et al., 2025),
robotics (Yang et al., 2024) and open scenes (Bruce et al., 2024). Interactive ones stream in real
time (Valevski et al., 2025; Alonso et al., 2024), often by distilling a text-to-video generator whose
autoencoder they inherit (He et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Few study that autoencoder. SANA-
WM (Zhu et al., 2026a) swaps two of them offline, and MIRA (Hu et al., 2026) judges its own
through the world model, but only on a single game. We compare twelve autoencoders of varied
compression through world models in four environments.
Latent structure and compression. Generation quality depends on the structure of the latent (Rom-
bach et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2025). Representation autoencoders generate in the space of a frozen
self-supervised encoder (Zheng et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026), at one compression rate. Video au-
toencoders mostly compress 8× in space and 4× in time (Wan et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024), some
far more (HaCohen et al., 2024; 2026; Chen et al., 2025b). A generator can also compress through
its patch size (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Gupta et al., 2024). For images, DC-AE (Chen et al., 2025b)
finds that compressing in the autoencoder beats patching. We test this for video world models and
build representation autoencoders that span seven spatio-temporal compressions.
Few-step diffusion decoders. Diffusion decoders avoid adversarial losses (Preechakul et al., 2022;
Chen et al., 2025d) but need many steps, or a second stage to reach one (OpenAI, 2023; Vallaeys
et al., 2026). Shortcut models and flow maps learn few-step generation in one stage from their own
predictions (Frans et al., 2025; Boffi et al., 2025). Our causal video decoder learns one- or two-step
decoding this way, with no adversarial loss or second stage. Appendix L extends this section.
7
CONCLUSION
An interactive world model inherits its cost per frame, its action latency and much of its quality from
its autoencoder, so its compression is a design choice. To study this choice, we build CADRE, an
open family spanning seven compressions, and train the same world model on each in manipula-
tion and navigation. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving
camera, and our study points to four findings. At strong compression, a diffusion decoder is sharper
than perceptual regression, even in one or two steps. In robot manipulation, temporal compression
is more effective in the autoencoder than in the world model. Manipulation and navigation call
for opposite compressions: temporal compression costs little when the camera is still, and spatial
compression when it moves. Surprisingly, with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt motion far
more than image quality, and, in robot manipulation, action following too.
Our world models each cover one environment; whether these findings carry over to a generalist
one, and to policy training and planning, remains open. Our manipulation data use still or nearly
still cameras; wrist-mounted cameras move, so our account predicts they favor the compression of
navigation. We hope open autoencoders will let practitioners fit a world model’s compression to its
scenes and to the latency and throughput its users need.

A
OVERVIEW
The supplementary material details how CADRE and its world models are built and scored, reports
every autoencoder in every environment and metric, and adds the analyses behind the explanations
of Section 5: long rollouts, camera motion, counterfactual actions and seams. It also compares
rollouts of every autoencoder.
Supplementary findings at a glance
• CADRE world models run fluidly on one H100. With 10 denoising steps, every CADRE autoen-
coder with τ ≥4 sustains 20 frames per second, as does CADRE-2×32×32 with one decoding step,
at 185 ms of action latency. At τ = 1, this needs one decoding step and at most four denoising steps
(Appendix D).
• Rollouts follow the actions, not only the usual motion of the environment. Scored against com-
mands shifted by 48 frames, which the rollout never received, every CADRE autoencoder keeps a
clear gap in every environment. The gap of Wan 2.2, Cosmos and LTX-2 shrinks to 0.04 to 0.14 in
driving, where their rollouts reproduce forward travel (Appendix E).
• Reconstruction does not predict rollout quality. The rank correlation between reconstruction and
rollout FVD is +0.76 in robot manipulation but −0.39 in driving and −0.35 indoors. Wan 2.2
reconstructs best by far, yet ranks fifth of twelve on rollout FVD in robot manipulation and last
indoors (Appendix F).
• With two decoding steps, CADRE holds the best FID and FVD of every environment. Only
Cosmos ties it, on robot-manipulation FVD. At 2×32×32, the diffusion decoder beats perceptual
regression on FID and FVD in all four environments, and regression wins only PSNR, a sign of blur
(Appendix G).
• Manipulation tolerates temporal compression, navigation spatial compression. At s = 16, robot-
manipulation FVD stays between 39 and 46 from τ = 1 to 8, while from τ = 2 to 8 it rises by 31%
in driving and 64% indoors. At τ = 2, 32×32 tokens multiply robot-manipulation FVD by 2.4 and
lower ARR from 0.73 to 0.45 at nearly the same FID, but raise navigation FVD by only 11 to 12%
(Appendix G).
• Over 64 s, no metric collapses and CADRE drifts least. CADRE-8×16×16 is best from about
13 s on. FVD grows by 0.8 to 1.8 per second for CADRE, 2.0 to 2.7 for the others and 14.5 for
Wan 2.2, which starts best. Action recoverability barely decays, though single rollouts can still fail
late (Appendix H).
• The view moves far more in navigation than in manipulation. In manipulation, content moves by
about one pixel per frame or less, under one token over eight frames. In navigation, it moves by more
than two, and half to three quarters of the patches change per frame instead of a third (Appendix I).
• World models follow actions never recorded with their context. From the same real context, they
hold still or replay another clip’s actions in manipulation, and turn left or right in indoor navigation
(Appendix J).
• Long latents leave seams. At τ = 8, rollouts put 58 to 83% more change at chunk boundaries than
real video, against 8 to 47% at τ = 2 (Appendix K).

B
AUTOENCODER IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Encoder. DINOv3 ViT-B/16 (Sim´eoni et al., 2026) (p = 16) runs at 288×512, and its features
average layers 2 and 4 to 11 plus the last layer. The bottleneck convolution maps its 768 channels to
64. In time, its kernel equals its stride τ and it has no padding, so it is causal. In space, the 18×32
grid is zero-padded to a multiple of the stride and cropped back in the decoder. Kernel and stride are
3 for 1×48×48 (6×11 tokens), 2 for 2×32×32 and 1 for s = 16. CADRE-4×24×24 uses kernel
5, stride 3 and padding 1, and reshapes 4×64 output channels into a 2×2 block of latents (12×22
tokens).
Decoder. It has 24 space-time blocks of width 1024 with 16 heads on a 16×16 patch grid; the last 4
form the temporal tail at the native frame rate. Blocks use QK-normalization, gated attention, rotary
embeddings in space and time, SwiGLU and LayerScale, with 4 spatial and 1 temporal register
tokens. After the unpatchify, a refine head applies two residual blocks of dilated depthwise and
causal temporal convolutions. The decoder has about 480M parameters.
Spatial AdaLN. AdaLN in DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) modulates all tokens of a sample with one
vector. Like SPADE (Park et al., 2019), SEAN (Zhu et al., 2020), SCAM (Dufour et al., 2022) and
PixelDiT (Yu et al., 2026), spatial AdaLN modulates each token hi from the latent zi at its position.

AUTOENCODER TRAINING DATA
Every CADRE autoencoder and the MIRA-recipe baseline train on the same 23 video sources, about
76k hours (Figure 8, Table 2). A sample is a random 16-frame window from a video of a randomly
drawn source, resized to cover 288×512, center-cropped and resampled to 20 frames per second.
Videos shorter than 16 frames or below 256 pixels on the short side are dropped.
D
WORLD MODEL ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING
The world model follows MIRA (Hu et al., 2026), and Figure 9 is redrawn from theirs. Table 3 lists
its hyperparameters.
Blocks. Each block runs bidirectional space attention within a latent frame, then a SwiGLU MLP;
blocks 0, 4, 8, 12 and 15 add causal time attention in between. Attention uses 16 query and 4
key/value heads, QK-normalization, a gate and rotary embeddings (2D in space, 1D in time). Blocks
are pre-norm, without LayerScale or registers.
Conditioning. Each latent frame is conditioned on its flow time and on the τ actions that produced
it, action aj producing frame j + 1. Actions are normalized per environment; their per-frame em-
beddings are concatenated, projected and passed through an MLP. The flow time gets a sinusoidal
embedding and an MLP, and the sum of both embeddings drives AdaLN (Peebles & Xie, 2023) be-
fore space attention, time attention and the MLP of every block. In human manipulation, each hand
contributes the frame-to-frame change in wrist position (3), wrist orientation (3) and 15 finger-joint
rotations (45).
Objective and recipe. Under diffusion forcing (Chen et al., 2024a), each latent frame draws its
own flow time ti, and the model regresses the velocity z1 −z0 from zti = tiz1 + (1 −ti)z0
(Figure 9). PSD-U, the progressive self-distillation of Boffi et al. (2025) with a uniformly sampled
intermediate time, is added on a fixed quarter of every batch. The recipe (Table 3) is the same for
every autoencoder; only the action encoder follows the environment. The world model does not
patch the latent further, except in Section 5.1.
Inference. The context is encoded once. At each chunk boundary, the world model reads the
previous latent frame and the τ actions entered during the chunk and predicts the next latent frame in
10 denoising steps. The frame is decoded for display, re-noised to level 0.2 and cached (Figure 11).
Since at produces frame t + 1, an action first shows τ frames after it is entered. This timing comes
from supervision: the architecture only keeps an action from changing frames already shown, and
within a latent frame each decoded frame can depend on all τ actions, so frame 5 reflects a4 and
earlier actions such as a3.
Latency. Figures 4b and 14 plot the action latency of Equation (1) for the first action of a chunk, the
worst case, at f = 20 frames per second. We time tgen, one world model call with its 10 denoising
steps and cache pass, and tdec, the decode of one new latent frame, on one H100 at batch 1 in bf16
with torch.compile, averaged over 32 latent frames after warm-up on the robot-manipulation
world models (Table 4). Each decoder runs its most efficient streaming decode: causal decoders
decode only the new latent and reuse the context (exactly for Wan 2.2; for CADRE, MIRA and
Cosmos we time the decode of the new latent, the cost of a decoder cache), DC-AE decodes each
frame alone, and the non-causal LTX-2 re-decodes an 8-latent window. The rollouts of Table 7 are
decoded over each decoder’s trained window. The resulting maximum frame rate, τ/(tgen + tdec),
differs from the displayed rate, which is always 20 frames per second; it only tells whether the
world model runs fluidly. Every CADRE autoencoder with τ ≥4 does, as does CADRE-2×32×32
with one step; those with τ = 1 reach only 7 to 13 frames per second. Raising τ raises both the
action latency and the maximum frame rate (Figure 1). With fewer denoising steps, every CADRE
autoencoder with τ ≥2 runs fluidly, while at τ = 1 this needs one decoding step and at most four
denoising steps (Figure 10).

H
LONG-HORIZON ROLLOUTS
To test whether the rankings hold beyond 4.8 s, we roll out the world models of all twelve autoen-
coders on robot manipulation for 64 s (1280 frames, 256 clips, one-step decode; Figure 17), the
only environment with many episodes long enough for such rollouts. No metric collapses. CADRE-
8×16×16 is best from about 13 s on and ends with the lowest LPIPS and FVD and the highest
PSNR, while the s = 16 autoencoders lose their early lead in LPIPS and PSNR by then. Action
recoverability barely decays. The other autoencoders drift faster (Table 8): FVD grows by 0.8 to 1.8
per second for the CADRE autoencoders, 2.0 to 2.7 for the others and 14.5 for Wan 2.2, which starts
best but whose PSNR falls below every CADRE autoencoder by about 9 s. Drift rates are least-
squares slopes between 4.8 and 64 s; the upward bias of Fr´echet distances at 256 clips is roughly
constant over time, so slopes stay comparable. The non-causal LTX-2 decoder uses a 57-frame (8-
latent) history window here. Figure 16 shows one rollout per autoencoder; there, Wan 2.2 corrupts
its colors by 48 s.

is the ratio of the mean dt over boundary pairs (t a multiple of τ) to that over the other pairs, and the
boundary excess is E/Ereal, with Ereal from the real continuation at the same indices. One means no
seam. We compute it on 32 clips per autoencoder with the one-step decode; repeated draws differ
by about 0.15. At τ = 8, rollouts put 58 to 83% more change at chunk boundaries than real video
(Table 10).

EXTENDED RELATED WORK
We study how much the autoencoder of an interactive world model compresses space and time, and
whether that compression belongs in the autoencoder or in the world model.
World models, from offline to streaming. A world model predicts how an environment evolves
under actions. Early ones predict compact latents for control (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner
et al., 2020; Micheli et al., 2023); most recent ones generate the video itself (Bai et al., 2024), for
driving (Gao et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2025; Carri´on et al., 2025), robotics (Yang et al., 2024),
egocentric motion (Bai et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a) and open scenes (Bruce et al., 2024; Mao
et al., 2025; PAN Team et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026b). Most are offline, taking seconds to minutes
per clip. They compare autoencoders only in that regime: SANA-WM (Zhu et al., 2026a) swaps the
Wan 2.1 autoencoder for LTX-2’s. An interactive world model streams instead and must render each
frame before the next action arrives. Some reach real time by rendering directly from controls (Kim
et al., 2020; Menapace et al., 2021) or with dedicated models (Valevski et al., 2025; Alonso et al.,
2024; Bar et al., 2025; Ramanana Rahary et al., 2026a). Others adapt a large video generator by
streaming distillation (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025), as Matrix-Game 2.0 (He et al., 2025)
and Hunyuan-GameCraft (Li et al., 2025a) do. They inherit its autoencoder unchanged, at 8× in
space and 4× in time. Their blockwise generation also adds serial computation (Chao et al., 2026).
MineWorld (Guo et al., 2025) uses discrete tokens instead. Once generation is fast, the decoder
becomes the bottleneck, which Matrix-Game 3.0 (Wang et al., 2026b) and WorldPlay (Sun et al.,
2026) prune. MIRA (Hu et al., 2026) instead trains its own autoencoder and judges it by the world
model; we extend that principle along both compression axes.
Diffusion and flow-based generation. Both the world model and the decoder of CADRE are
diffusion models trained with flow matching. Diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho
et al., 2020; Song & Ermon, 2019; Song et al., 2021b) and their refinements of sampler, schedule,
loss weighting and preconditioning (Song et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2022; Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021;
Kingma et al., 2021; Kingma & Gao, 2023; Hang et al., 2023; Karras et al., 2022; 2024b) converge in
flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo et al., 2025), which regresses a velocity
along an interpolant between noise and data and scales with transformers (Bao et al., 2023; Peebles
& Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Esser et al., 2024). Three of its design choices interact with compres-
sion. The first is the prediction target. Noise and velocity prediction (Salimans & Ho, 2022) degrade
when each token is high-dimensional, and clean-sample prediction does not (Li & He, 2026). Our
decoder outputs large pixel patches, so it predicts the clean sample, and its wavelet loss, related
to the frequency balancing of Degeorge et al. (2026), emphasizes the fine texture it must restore.
The world model’s tokens keep 64 channels at every compression, so it keeps velocity prediction.
The second is the noise schedule, whose optimum shifts with the number of tokens (Chen, 2023;
Hoogeboom et al., 2025); RAE (Zheng et al., 2026) generalizes the shift of Esser et al. (2024) to
the number of tokens times their channels. Our compressions change the number of tokens by more
than an order of magnitude, so our tuning shifts the schedule with each autoencoder’s latent size
and token count. The third is the representation. Aligning a generator’s features to a self-supervised
encoder speeds up its training (Yu et al., 2025), as masking does (Gao et al., 2023); we place that
prior in the autoencoder instead, and the world model generates in the features of a frozen DINOv3.
Conditioning. The world model is conditioned on actions, and how faithfully it follows them is
one of our three axes. Image generators usually strengthen adherence to their condition with guid-
ance (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Ho & Salimans, 2022; Kynk¨a¨anniemi et al., 2024; Karras et al.,
2024a); we use none, so action following reflects the latent and the model alone. A condition can
also be weak, as with noisy web captions (Dai et al., 2023; Podell et al., 2024), and some action
dimensions barely change the image, such as the finger joints in human manipulation, which our
probe cannot read even on real video. We therefore measure action following directly, by how well
an inverse dynamics probe recovers the actions from the generated frames (Appendix E).
Evaluation. We judge each autoencoder by the world model it yields, with FID (Heusel et al.,
2017) and FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2019). These metrics are fragile. FID mixes fidelity and diver-
sity (Kynk¨a¨anniemi et al., 2019), depends on resizing and ImageNet features (Parmar et al., 2022;
Kynk¨a¨anniemi et al., 2023), can disagree with human judgment (Stein et al., 2023; Jayasumana
et al., 2024), and moves more when the model is retrained with another seed than when it is resam-
pled (Dufour et al., 2026).

Video and causal generation. Video diffusion moved from pixel space (Ho et al., 2022) to latents,
first by inflating image networks (Blattmann et al., 2023) and then with spatio-temporal transform-
ers (Yang et al., 2025b; Kong et al., 2024; Polyak et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025), which denoise a
whole clip at one noise level. Causal generation breaks that symmetry. Rolling diffusion (Ruhe et al.,
2024) gives later frames more noise, and diffusion forcing (Chen et al., 2024a) draws one noise level
per frame, so the next frame is denoised from a clean or partly noisy past. History guidance (Song
et al., 2025) builds on it, and MAGI-1 (Sand.ai et al., 2025) scales chunk-wise autoregression to
24B parameters. Noise on the context (Valevski et al., 2025) and training on the model’s own
rollouts (Huang et al., 2025) curb drift over long horizons, and FramePack (Zhang et al., 2025a)
compresses the history to bound its cost. Discrete alternatives predict quantized tokens autoregres-
sively (Yu et al., 2024; Bruce et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024) or by masked prediction (Chang et al.,
2022), and MAR (Li et al., 2024) keeps autoregression but replaces the quantizer with a per-token
diffusion loss. Our world model combines flow matching, diffusion forcing and context noise, and
stays fixed while the autoencoder varies.
The autoencoder as an object of study. In latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022; Esser et al.,
2021), generation quality depends on the structure of the latent. A line of work improves it at a
fixed generator (Section 4). Representation autoencoders go further and generate in the space of a
frozen self-supervised encoder (Zheng et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026), for text-to-image (Tong et al.,
2026), low-dimensional latents (Gao et al., 2026b; Shi et al., 2026; Bi et al., 2026) and video (Xie
et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026), on encoders such as DINO (Caron et al., 2021;
Oquab et al., 2024; Sim´eoni et al., 2026) and V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2025; Mur-Labadia et al.,
2026). Distilling such an encoder into the latent, as in GAIA (Hu et al., 2023; Russell et al., 2025)
and Mimi (D´efossez et al., 2024), follows the same idea and makes latents easier to diffuse. These
works use one compression rate; our representation autoencoders vary it in space and time, one
trained autoencoder per compression point.
Video autoencoders and strong compression. Video autoencoders use causal 3D convolutions (Yu
et al., 2024). Most settle at 8× in space and 4× in time (Yang et al., 2025b; Wan et al., 2025; Kong
et al., 2024). Others compress harder: LTX-Video (HaCohen et al., 2024), DC-AE (Chen et al.,
2025b), DC-VideoGen (Chen et al., 2025c), H3AE (Wu et al., 2025c), ProMAG (Mahapatra et al.,
2025) and DeltaTok (Kerssies et al., 2026), down to one token per frame. Comparisons across rates
use reconstruction (Tang et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025) or text-to-video generation (Hansen-
Estruch et al., 2025). The two rates usually move together. We vary them independently and judge
each by an action-conditioned world model.
Diffusion decoders and single-step generation. Pixel and perceptual losses alone blur, so autoen-
coders add an adversarial loss (Esser et al., 2021), at the price of a sensitive discriminator. Diffusion
decoders are the alternative (Preechakul et al., 2022; Pandey et al., 2022; Pern´ıas et al., 2024), and
scale as tokenizers with a single flow-matching loss (Chen et al., 2025d), but decode in many steps.
Single-step diffusion decoders so far need a second stage or an adversarial term (Vallaeys et al., 2026;
Sargent et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025); video diffusion decoders keep the adversarial loss (HaCo-
hen et al., 2024) or several steps (Yang et al., 2025a; Teng et al., 2026). Few-step generation itself
comes from distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022; Luo et al., 2023; Sauer et al., 2024) and consistency
models (Song et al., 2023; Lu & Song, 2025); shortcut models (Frans et al., 2025), flow maps (Boffi
et al., 2025) and MeanFlow (Geng et al., 2025) learn it in one stage from their own predictions. We
bring that one-stage self-distillation to a causal decoder with no discriminator.
Where compression lives. A generator can also compress through its patch size. DiT (Peebles &
Xie, 2023) improves as the patch shrinks, pixel-space transformers (Li & He, 2026; Hoogeboom
et al., 2025) replace the autoencoder with a large patch, W.A.L.T. (Gupta et al., 2024) and Latte (Ma
et al., 2025) find temporal patching costliest, and FlexiDiT (Anagnostidis et al., 2025) varies the
patch per step. For images, DC-AE (Chen et al., 2025b) finds that compressing in the autoencoder
beats patching at matched tokens. We make that comparison for a video world model.
