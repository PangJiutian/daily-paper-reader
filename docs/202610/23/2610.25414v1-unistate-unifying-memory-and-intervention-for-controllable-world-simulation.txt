ABSTRACT
World models offer increasingly rich environments for exploration, yet interven-
ing in a world while observing the consequences from freely chosen viewpoints re-
mains challenging. We tackle these challenges by introducing UniState, a frame-
work for controllable world simulation built on a unified world-state representa-
tion. This representation unifies spatial memory and user interventions. Users
specify object motion directly within spatial memory, while independently choos-
ing the camera trajectory from which the resulting world state is observed. This
unification makes the relationship between the scene and the requested changes
explicit, removing ambiguity between object motion and viewpoint change from
the control specification. Crucially, specifying an intervention does not require
prescribing every resulting movement: the video model can synthesize additional
motion within controlled objects and plausible interactions with their surround-
ings. We develop training and sampling strategies that help generated videos
follow the specified object motion and camera trajectory while preserving this
capacity to generate responses beyond the prescribed transformations. Experi-
ments demonstrate more accurate joint object and camera control than the existing
methods, alongside qualitative evidence that intervention-induced scene changes
persist across rollouts.

INTRODUCTION
World simulation models have been built from video models for their increasing performance of
producing high-fidelity future predictions given an initial frame and a description of the events oc-
curring in the scene (Team Wan et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2026). They can be used as explorable
environments for training agents, especially with camera controls (Wang et al., 2025a; Xing et al.,
2025) and object controls (Wang et al., 2024a; Geng et al., 2025).
However, using world models for learning agents is limited to viewpoint exploration (Bar et al.,
2025) even though recent world models cover complex real-world environments, and training agents
with physical intervention to the world is limited to restricted configurations such as grippers with a
fixed camera (Du et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2026). We attribute this gap to recent
world models that cover navigation and omit physical intervention.
We suggest that physical intervention beyond navigation is a crucial component for training phys-
ical agents, as well as other important requirements including spatio-temporal coherence and free-
viewpoint observation. Existing video models exploit persistent spatial memory to support spatio-
temporal coherence (Xiao et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026), camera control to support free-viewpoint
observation (He et al., 2025a;b), and dedicated conditions to support object motion (Wu et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025a). However, supporting physical intervention in a persistent world with free-
viewpoint observation remains challenging.

We introduce UniState, capable of above three requirements. Briefly, it contains initial spatial mem-
ory and user intervention in the same world coordinate system in order to work as a spatio-temporal
twin of the world and allow the viewpoint to be separately specified. It removes the ambiguity
that the same trajectories can be achieved by either object motion or viewpoint control in previous
methods. The initial spatial memory is the latents lifted into 3D from an initial image (Yu et al.,
2026). Then users specify motion of a region with a sequence of 6-DoF transformations, enabling
3D object translation and rotation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Finally, a camera trajectory determines the
viewpoints from which UniState is rendered. Last but not least, we introduce sampling and training
techniques to reflect all the above variables.
This unified state guides video generation while preserving the model’s capacity to synthesize plau-
sible responses to the specified interventions. For example, an intervention in the surrounding envi-
ronment can lead the model to generate an agent’s evasive maneuver, such as taking a detour to avoid
an approaching vehicle (Fig. 1(a)). Controlling a bulldozer can similarly induce motion in nearby
objects through contact, without explicitly prescribing their trajectories (Fig. 1(b)). This flexibility
also extends to motion within the controlled object: specifying only the translation of a cat’s torso
allows the model to generate the accompanying leg movements for walking (Fig. 1(f)).
To guarantee that the video model to faithfully translate the unified representation into observations
from the specified viewpoint, we design both sampling and training strategies for coherent joint
object and camera control. Our sampling strategy promotes consistent generation from the specified
viewpoint during object interventions, while our training strategy reduces the mismatch between
training and inference conditions, helping the video model follow the specified object motion and
camera trajectory while retaining its capacity to generate plausible responses.
Experiments show that UniState achieves more accurate joint object and camera control than the
evaluated baselines. Qualitative results demonstrate manipulation of whole objects and selected
parts with consistent depth ordering and occlusion, alongside motion and interactions beyond the
prescribed transformations. Ablation studies support the benefits of the proposed training and sam-
pling strategies for control accuracy.
Our key contributions are:
• With UniState, we show that unifying spatial memory and user interventions in a unified
world-state representation allows object motion and camera trajectory to be specified inde-
pendently.

RELATED WORK
Video world models as interactive simulators.
Video world models simulate the consequences
of actions for planning and policy learning, using generated videos as plans for control (Du et al.,
2023) or as interactive environments for policy training (Yang et al., 2024). Action-conditioned
video models further leverage pretrained video priors or learn from latent actions and recorded con-
trols for interactive simulation (Rigter et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Bruce et al., 2024; Alonso
et al., 2024; Valevski et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; He et al., 2026). Recent methods extend ex-
ploration across diverse scenes, using context management and accelerated generation to sustain
interaction (Mao et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026; Team et al., 2026; Ball et al., 2025). Complemen-
tary approaches use predicted observations for navigation planning (Bar et al., 2025), incorporate
geometric prediction (Zhu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), or ground exploration in geographically
indexed street views (Seo et al., 2026). Instruction-driven behaviors and object-directed actions fur-
ther broaden interaction beyond navigation (Tang et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). UniState extends
world exploration with time-varying 6-DoF interventions on selected object regions with the uni-
fied representation and world coordinate system as persistent memory, while allowing the camera
trajectory to be specified independently.
Object control and camera control.
Methods for controlling motion in video generation specify
motion through point trajectories (Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Geng et al., 2025; Wang
et al., 2025a) or bounding boxes (Wang et al., 2024a); joint object and camera control further re-
quires coordinating these motion signals with viewpoint conditions (Wang et al., 2024b; Xing et al.,
2025), often using depth, pose, or 3D guidance (Wang et al., 2025b; Ji et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2025;
Cao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026). Since viewpoint change and object mo-
tion both induce image-space displacement, recent methods disentangle them through cross-view
transfer (Liu et al., 2026) or dynamic reprojection of world-anchored trajectories (Gu et al., 2026).
Methods using explicit 3D representations condition video generation on Gaussians expressed in a
shared coordinate frame (Zheng et al., 2026) or editable foreground caches (Wu et al., 2026), while
appearance-aware methods transport latent features along point tracks (Chu et al., 2025; Gu et al.,
2026). As summarized in Table 1, the compared methods encode spatial memory and user interven-
tion in separate representations. This separation leaves generation susceptible to background drift
and ambiguity between object motion and viewpoint change. UniState grounds both in a shared
appearance-bearing 3D representation and world coordinate system, making their spatial relation-
ship explicit.
Persistent and editable spatial memory.
While camera conditioning controls the viewpoint (He
et al., 2025a;b), scene consistency can be maintained by retrieving spatially relevant observa-
tions (Xiao et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026) or compressing historical context (Zhang
et al., 2025a; Oshima et al., 2026; Hong et al., 2025). Retrieval and compression retain scene ev-
idence but do not themselves define geometric operations on entities. Geometry-based approaches

3
METHOD
UniState generates a video from an initial frame, user-specified interventions, and a camera tra-
jectory. We lift initial-frame latent features into 3D to construct spatial memory, then express user
interventions by transforming the geometry of selected regions within this memory. The transformed
entities and unchanged scene context thus remain in a single set of 3D latents, which we call a unified
world-state representation. Rendering this state along an independently specified camera trajectory
provides the conditions for video generation. Fig. 2 summarizes the inference and training pipelines.
We first describe how UniState constructs, manipulates, and renders this unified state (Section 3.1).
We then introduce Scene-Anchored Joint Guidance (Section 3.2) and a training strategy (Section 3.3)
that help the video model follow the resulting conditions.
3.1
UNIFIED WORLD-STATE REPRESENTATION
Constructing spatial memory.
We encode the initial image into VAE latent features and lift them
into 3D using the source camera and estimated depth (Wang et al., 2026b). Each resulting 3D latent
pair has geometry with its source feature; together, these latents form the initial state S0. Additional
views of the same initial scene can extend S0 by lifting and registering their latent features in the
same world coordinate system. Geometric construction details are provided in Appendix A.1.

Interaction and state updates.
User interventions specify the motion of the controlled entity,
while the video model synthesizes plausible responses in the surrounding scene. Conditioning scene
regions involved in an interaction on their previous configuration can constrain these responses. We
therefore temporarily exclude these regions from the memory condition while retaining the pre-
scribed control over the entity. This allows nearby objects to move in response to contact without
explicitly prescribing their trajectories.
After the interaction, we obtain updated geometry and latent features for the affected regions from
generated observations, register the geometry to the shared world coordinate system, and update the
corresponding state regions. The updated state serves as S0 for the next rollout, allowing subsequent
viewpoint changes and interventions to build on the resulting scene configuration.
3.2
SCENE-ANCHORED JOINT GUIDANCE
Our conditional video model fθ is trained with a flow-matching objective and predicts latent veloc-
ities during sampling.
Although the unified state specifies interventions independently of camera viewpoint, the generated
video may still entangle their effects, causing background motion to deviate from the prescribed
camera trajectory. Our key observation is that this ambiguity can be removed by constructing a
camera-only reference: if we render the initial state S0 under the same camera trajectory but without
applying any intervention, the resulting condition should contain scene changes induced by view-
point change alone. We therefore use the velocity conditioned on the intervention for the intervened
region and use the velocity conditioned on the initial state for the background. We spatially route be-
tween these two predictions and blend the result with the full joint prediction. We call this procedure
Scene-Anchored Joint Guidance (SAJG).

prediction alone, whereas α = 1 uses only the joint prediction. We use α = 0.25 unless stated
otherwise. At each denoising step, Vfinal is passed to the flow-matching solver.
3.3
TRAINING
Training conditions.
We train on videos paired with camera conditions C, latent conditions W,
and intervention masks M derived from per-frame object masks. We construct W by combining
initial-state renders with ground-truth entity latents. The latter preserve alignment with the target
video, avoiding misalignment and floating artifacts caused by transporting source features with im-
perfect geometry (see Fig. 10 in the appendix).
We render the initial state from each target camera and zero out the selected entities’ projected source
regions. We then insert ground-truth entity latents at their target locations, retaining the initial-state
render elsewhere. Vacated source regions remain zero unless covered by a target entity. The exact
compositing formulation is provided in Appendix A.3.
Transport-Aligned Augmentation.
The composited conditions introduce a training–inference
mismatch: training uses entity latents from each ground-truth target frame, whereas inference
transports source latents through the transformed geometry. To reduce this mismatch, we apply
Transport-Aligned Augmentation (TAA), which spatially coarsens future-frame entity latents while
preserving their target support. TAA modifies only the latent condition; the intervention masks and
ground-truth training targets remain unchanged. The augmentation procedure is detailed in Ap-
pendix A.3.
Curriculum training.
We train UniState in two stages using a flow-matching objective LFM,
keeping the pretrained video backbone, VAE, and text encoder frozen. (Ling et al., 2024). The first
stage trains the camera-conditioning module and UniState adapter on DL3DV (Ling et al., 2024).
Starting from these weights, the second stage adds the intervention-mask adapter and jointly trains
all three modules on HM-World(Chen et al., 2026) using the composited conditions and TAA de-
scribed above.

4.2
EVALUATION
We evaluate all methods on the same 277 scenarios spanning diverse camera trajectories and user
interventions. Each method generates two videos per scenario with independent random seeds (554
videos total), using an identical evaluation protocol and averaging metrics across the two seeds.
Joint control accuracy
We introduce World-Control Projection Error (WCPE) to evaluate object
control specified in world coordinates. Because video models produce 2D observations, an object’s
screen-space position alone does not establish whether it follows the specified motion in the world.
We therefore assess object motion relative to the surrounding scene, while checking that the scene
remains aligned with the specified camera trajectory. WCPE implements this assessment as a 2D
proxy, using target projections to evaluate object–background relative positions and background
alignment. Requiring both prevents correct screen-space object placement alone from yielding a low
error when the surrounding scene is misaligned. The metric formulation and evaluation protocol are
detailed in Section A.4.
Camera-control accuracy
We evaluate camera-control accuracy by comparing the camera tra-
jectory recovered from each generated video with its target trajectory. We report Cam-T, the mean
translation error between the recovered and target camera positions, and Cam-R, the mean geodesic
rotation error between their orientations, measured in degrees. Lower values for both metrics indi-
cate more faithful camera control.

ABLATION STUDY
Scene-Anchored Joint Guidance.
Fig. 4 shows that intermediate values of α outperform both
spatial routing alone (α = 0) and the joint prediction alone (α = 1). We select α = 0.25, which
achieves the lowest WCPE and Cam-R and nearly the lowest Cam-T. These results support combin-
ing a scene-anchor correction with a global joint contribution.
Transport-Aligned Augmentation.
We compare three ways of constructing entity latent con-
ditions during training: transporting initial-frame latents using estimated geometry, using aligned
ground-truth entity latents from each target frame, and applying TAA to those ground-truth latents.
All variants share training data, initialization, optimization budgets, inference conditions, and SAJG
settings. As shown in Table 2, replacing transported initial-frame latents with ground-truth latents
reduces WCPE and Cam-T, but slightly increases Cam-R. Applying TAA further reduces all three
errors, supporting the combination of spatially aligned training conditions and augmentation that
reduces their latent mismatch with inference-time transport.
Curriculum training.
Staged training outperforms interleaved training under matched data expo-
sure and computation; details are provided in Appendix B.1.

4.5
COMPARISONS
We compare our method with VerseCrafter (Zheng et al., 2026), WorldDirector (Wang et al., 2026a),
MotionCanvas (Xing et al., 2025), SymphoMotion (Zhang et al., 2026), and Real2SAM2Real (Wu
et al., 2026). We evaluate joint object and camera control using WCPE, Cam-T, and Cam-R. As
shown in Table 3, our method achieves the lowest WCPE, Cam-T, and Cam-R, demonstrating accu-
rate joint control of object motion and camera viewpoint. Qualitative comparisons in Fig. 5 further
show these failure modes. In this example, the user keeps the camera static while translating and ro-
tating the ball laterally behind the alphabet blocks. VerseCrafter and Real2SAM2Real often absorb
object motion into viewpoint change, resulting in undesired viewpoint change despite the specified
static camera condition. This makes the ball appear to move with the camera rather than indepen-
dently in the scene. WorldDirector and MotionCanvas preserve the static camera more reliably, but
fail to reproduce the correct depth ordering and rotation of the ball. In contrast, our method faithfully
follows the intended joint object and camera control, capturing object translation and rotation under
a static camera while handling occlusion. See Section C for additional qualitative comparisons. An
additional comparison with MosaicMem is provided in Table 7.

CONCLUSION
UniState unifies spatial memory and user interventions in a shared world coordinate system for con-
trollable world simulation. This unified world-state representation allows object motion and camera
trajectory to be specified independently. It provides spatial constraints for video generation while
preserving the video model’s capacity to synthesize plausible responses, so users can specify in-
tended changes without prescribing every resulting motion or interaction. Quantitative comparisons
and ablations support our training and sampling strategies for joint object and camera control across

diverse camera trajectories and user interventions. Revisitation results further show that generated
interaction outcomes can be incorporated into spatial memory and retained across rollouts. Together,
these findings support physical intervention in a persistent world with free-viewpoint observation.
Limitations
Interaction effects rely on the video model’s generative prior: although controlled ob-
ject states can be updated outside the camera view, their interactions with surrounding objects cannot
be explicitly simulated while out of view. Spatial resolution limits control over small objects, while
temporal resolution limits fast motion. The model may also fail to follow physically implausible
interventions, such as moving a solid object through a solid wall.

Scene-mask construction.
We construct Gs in latent-video coordinates. For each target camera,
we project the selected entities at their source and target poses to obtain the binary support volumes
Esrc and Etgt. The scene mask is
Gs = F1,1 [1 −D1 (Esrc ∪Etgt)] ,
(6)
where D1 applies one-cell spatial dilation and F1,1 applies one-cell spatial and temporal feathering.
The resulting values lie in [0, 1], and the mask is broadcast across latent channels. Unlike the inter-
vention mask M, which marks the visible target entity regions, Gs selects scene regions away from
both source and target entity supports, with soft transitions at their boundaries.
The mask is geometry-derived rather than learned. It may vary across video frames, but is computed
only once for each input and remains unchanged across denoising steps. All dilation and feathering
settings are fixed; only α is varied in the routing ablation.

The main-paper equations use predictions after classifier-free guidance. In implementation, we

predictions are not necessarily identical, we observed no noticeable differences in the generated

computation to three transformer evaluations per denoising step.

A.4
WCPE EVALUATION
Motivation and target projections.
An object’s image-space position alone does not establish
whether the prescribed world-space control is realized relative to the scene. WCPE therefore eval-
uates object–background relative positions together with background alignment. Target trajectories
are constructed from the input scene proxy: object points follow the prescribed object transforma-
tions, while background points remain fixed in world coordinates. Both are projected using the
target camera trajectory. These targets are shared across methods for each evaluation condition.

Interpretation.
WCPE measures projection-level consistency with the prescribed world-space ob-
ject and camera controls. It does not reconstruct or directly verify metric 3D trajectories, and its
accuracy depends on the scene proxy and point tracking.

ADDITIONAL ABLATIONS
B.1
CURRICULUM TRAINING
We compare the staged curriculum in Section 3.3 with an interleaved baseline that mixes the same
conditioning tasks throughout training. Both schedules use 30K total updates: 20K on DL3DV
with C + W conditioning and 10K on HM-World with C + W + M conditioning. All other train-
ing settings are identical, and we select the best checkpoint for each schedule based on validation
performance.
As shown in Table 4, staged training achieves lower WCPE, Cam-T, and Cam-R. Since both sched-
ules use the same data exposure and update counts, this comparison supports the benefit of training
order. We also continued the interleaved baseline beyond 30K steps and observed no further valida-
tion improvement.
