ABSTRACT
Text-to-video models often generate the visual concepts requested in natural language prompts but
fail to satisfy their intended ordering, timing, and persistence requirements. We propose representing
these requirements as specifications in a formal Temporal Logic, and introduce Temporal Specifica-
tion Guidance (TSG), a training-free inference-time framework that turns temporal specifications
into executable objectives for guiding video generation during sampling. TSG scores traces of
visual concept evidence using differentiable quantitative semantics and uses the score’s gradients to
construct a specification-guided denoising direction. We instantiate this framework with video–text
attention traces, efficient differentiable scores for Metric Temporal Logic, and gradient-derived
attention biases. This instantiation requires neither an external concept classifier nor backpropagation
through the video transformer. Its attention-steered forward pass yields a denoising direction that
augments classifier-free guidance (CFG). On a benchmark of 220 prompts spanning 11 categories of
temporal constraints, including ordered object entry and state transitions, this instantiation improves
specification satisfaction accuracy over CFG by 9.5–20 percentage points across five models, with
minimal memory overhead. These results demonstrate the potential of temporal specifications and
differentiable satisfaction objectives as a flexible interface for controlling video generation.

Introduction
Text-to-Video generation has seen tremendous progress in recent years, with models such as Gemini Omni (Google
DeepMind, 2026), Runway Gen (Esser et al., 2023; Runway, 2025), Wan (Wan et al., 2025), and CogVideoX (Yang
et al., 2025) producing videos with improved visual quality and semantic fidelity. Classifier-Free Guidance (Ho &
Salimans, 2022) was introduced to improve adherence to natural language prompts in image generation and is now
widely adopted by video generation models (Ho et al., 2022b,a). However, adherence to temporal constraints remains
challenging (Choi et al., 2025; Schiber et al., 2026). Figure 1 shows two examples of such failures with CogVideoX-5B.
While the model broadly incorporates the semantic concepts described in the text prompts (e.g., the presence of a traffic
light or a drone), it fails to follow the required ordering, persistence, and timing requirements. In these examples, the
model misses the intended timed color progression and the eventual appearance of a hiker or a flare signal, respectively.
In this work, we propose expressing temporal requirements as specifications in formal Temporal Logics. Formal
temporal specifications can precisely express requirements that are implicitly described in natural language prompts.
Frame-level visual concepts, such as the presence of a traffic light or the associated colors, can be represented as atomic
propositions. Temporal Logics offer standard Boolean operators (negation, conjunction, and disjunction) and temporal
operators such as Always (G), Until (U), and Eventually (F) to express persistence and ordering requirements. In this
work, we present a specification language adopted from Metric Temporal Logic (Koymans, 1990) to express temporal
requirements with different degrees of timing precision. Representations of temporal requirements from prior work,
including timed binary masks (Schiber et al., 2026) and untimed Temporal Logic formulae (Choi et al., 2025), can also
be written as specifications in this language.
We propose Temporal Specification Guidance (TSG), a training-free inference-time framework for improving
adherence to temporal specifications with transformer-based video generation models. Our key insight is that temporal
specifications provide an executable objective for guiding video generation during sampling. TSG makes this practical
through differentiable specification scores and attention interventions that avoid backpropagation through the video
transformer. Concretely, TSG computes traces of visual concept evidence using video-text attention from the video
transformer. For every attention head, TSG computes biases as gradients of a differentiable specification score, steers

We compute a differentiable specification score over attention traces using a quantitative semantics for Metric Temporal

a fixed specification. We establish monotonicity in independent literal-support inputs and predictable concept-score
gradient signs under consistent polarity.
We evaluate TSG on a benchmark of 220 prompts spanning temporal constraints from 11 categories, including
timed state transitions, entity appearance and disappearance, persistence, and choice. Across five models from the
CogVideoX (Yang et al., 2025) and Wan (Wan et al., 2025) families, TSG improves specification satisfaction accuracy
by 9.5–20 percentage points over CFG-only sampling. We also evaluate TSG with Wan 2.1 1.3B on strictly-timed
prompts from the TempoControl object appearance benchmark (Schiber et al., 2026), where it outperforms CFG
sampling by up to 22 percentage points in temporal accuracy and matches the state-of-the-art method on that benchmark
with approximately 62% lower peak GPU memory. Together, these findings support specification-guided attention
steering as an effective and memory efficient approach to temporal control in video generation.

Temporal constraints as formal specifications
Natural Language prompts to video generation models discuss the visual concepts that must be present in the video,
along with the order and optionally the timing of their appearance. In this section, we discuss a formal specification
language for these constraints.
Let M represent a Text-to-Video (T2V) generation model that takes a text prompt p as input and outputs a video
v = [v1, . . . , vT ] with T discrete frames. Let C denote the set of all frame-level visual concepts of interest. Examples

of such visual concepts include entity presence (e.g., traffic light), entity states (e.g., the color red), and settings with
properties (e.g., sky at dusk). We use y(vi, c) ∈{0, 1} to denote the presence or absence of the visual concept c in
frame vi.
Specification language for temporal constraints. We propose a fragment of Metric Temporal Logic over finite
traces (MTLf) as a suitable language for specifying temporal constraints over visual concepts (Koymans, 1990). A
specification φ in MTLf takes the following form:
φ := ⊤| ⊥| c | ¬c | φ1 ∧φ2 | φ1 ∨φ2 | XIφ | φ1UIφ2 | GIφ
where ⊤and ⊥represent True and False, respectively, and c denotes an atomic proposition corresponding to a visual
concept c. The Boolean operators ¬, ∧, and ∨denote negation, conjunction, and disjunction, respectively. The temporal
operators XI, UI and GI denote Next, Until, and Always, in interval I ⊆[0, 1], resp. The temporal operator Eventually
FI can be derived as FIφ = ⊤UIφ.
This language can be used to specify several interesting constraints on persistence, order, and timing of visual concepts.
Time intervals I = [t1, t2] allow us to express specifications with varying timing precision: untimed (t1 = 0, t2 = 1),
interval-timed (t1 ≥0, t2 ≤1), and strictly-timed (0 ≤t1 = t2 ≤1). Note that the intervals I = [t1, t2] are written
over time normalized in [0, 1] and the corresponding offsets can be computed as [⌈t1(T −1)⌉, ⌊t2(T −1)⌋]. The
constraint from Figure 1 that requires the drone to remain visible throughout the video and at least one of the two rescue
cues to appear at some point in the second half can be written in this language as:
φrescue = G[0,1]drone ∧F[0.5,1](flare_signal ∨stranded_hiker)
Representing temporal constraints from prior work. This specification language can be used to express temporal
constraints from recent works, including untimed Temporal Logic (TL) specifications (Choi et al., 2025) and binary
temporal masks that prescribe the presence or absence of a visual concept in each frame (Schiber et al., 2026). Untimed
TL specifications can be written with the trivial [0, 1] intervals. A temporal mask can be encoded as a conjunction of
Always constraints over intervals where the mask is constant, using c for presence and ¬c for absence. Additionally, we
can write specifications that correspond to a set of temporal masks, including the choice between visual concepts and
the disappearance of an object within an interval. In other words, a temporal mask corresponds to a given valid trace
while our specifications can admit several valid traces.
Deciding whether a video satisfies a specification. These specifications can be supplied directly with the text prompt,
or can be extracted from the prompt using a Large Language Model (LLM). Given the frame-level labels y(vi, c) for
visual concepts, the label y(v, φ, t) for a video v satisfying specification φ starting from frame 1 ≤t ≤T can be
computed deterministically using the standard Boolean semantics for MTLf. We present this semantics, along with
examples of other specifications, in Appendix A. The examples in Figure 1 thus score y(v, φ, 1) = 0 with CFG-only
sampling, which we discuss next, and y(v, φ, 1) = 1 with TSG, discussed in Section 4.

Video Generation and Classifier Free Guidance
We focus on latent text-to-video models that generate videos from text prompts through iterative sampling in a
compressed latent space, using a transformer to predict a denoising direction at each step. This formulation encompasses
model families such as CogVideoX (Yang et al., 2025) and Wan (Wan et al., 2025). The sampling process takes place in
a compressed spatio-temporal latent space. A video v ∈RT ×H×W ×3 corresponds to a latent z ∈RTz×Hz×Wz×Cz,
where Tz and (Hz, Wz) denote the latent temporal and spatial resolutions respectively, and Cz denotes the number of
latent channels. A 3D Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is trained to decode the video v = D(z). Given a text prompt p,
a text encoder produces a sequence of tokens ep. Sampling begins with a Gaussian latent zN ∼N(0, I) and proceeds
through N steps. At step k, the Transformer Tθ predicts the update direction dk as:
dk = Tθ(zk, ep, k),
zk−1 = Stepk(zk, dk).
We refer to dk as the update direction or denoising direction interchangeably. Stepk incorporates the sampling rule and
update schedule, and can vary across models. The latent generated from the final step (z0) is then de-compressed by the
VAE decoder D to the output video v = D(z0).
Classifier-free Guidance. Conditioning on the text prompt alone may not provide sufficient control over the content of
the generated video. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022) was proposed to strengthen this conditioning
during sampling, without requiring an external classifier. At each sampling step, CFG combines two update directions
using the transformer:
dk,u = Tθ(zk, eref, k),
dk,c = Tθ(zk, ep, k),
dk,cfg = dk,u + λcfg(dk,c −dk,u).

computed using the predictions from the unconditional or reference branch dk,u and the prompt conditional branch dk,c.
The strength of the extrapolation is controlled by the CFG guidance scale λcfg ∈R, with different recommendations
across models.

TSG: Specification Guidance for Text-to-Video Generation
We propose Temporal Specification Guidance (TSG), a training-free sampling-time framework that augments CFG with
an update direction dk,tsg guided by the temporal specification φ. This framework has three components: temporal
traces that provide evidence of visual concepts, a differentiable scoring function that evaluates these traces against the
specification φ, and an update mechanism that uses gradients of this score to steer generation. In this work, we compute
traces of visual concept evidence using video-text attention within the transformer Tθ, score them against φ with a
differentiable quantitative semantics for MTLf, and use the gradients as attention logit biases. This attention-steered
forward pass produces the specification-guided direction dk,φ. We present the overall process in Figure 2 and discuss
each step in detail now.
Temporal Specification-guided sampling. Let dk,u and dk,c denote the reference and prompt-conditional direction
predictions from Section 3. Similarly, let dk,cfg and λk,cfg denote the CFG-predicted direction and the CFG guidance
scale, respectively. At sampling step k, TSG computes a specification-guided direction dk,φ using φ and yields the
update direction dk,tsg as:
dk,tsg = dk,cfg + λk,tsg(dk,φ −dk,c),
zk−1 = Stepk(zk, dk,tsg).
As with CFG, the strength of TSG is controlled by the TSG guidance scale λk,tsg ≥0. As the CFG guidance scale
λk,cfg usually takes values recommended by model providers and can dynamically vary across sampling steps, we
compute λtsg using the ratio between the TSG contribution and the λcfg-scaled CFG contribution as:

5.2
RQ1. Does TSG improve adherence to temporal specifications?
Result 1: TSG consistently outperforms CFG-only on interval-timed and strictly-timed temporal constraints.
Table 1 presents the results comparing TSG against CFG-only sampling on the TSG benchmark. TSG improves
satisfaction accuracy by 9.5–20 percentage points across models, with Wan 2.2 A14B reporting the highest satisfaction
accuracy of 46.8% with TSG. Interestingly, Wan2.1 1.3B reports a 31.4% accuracy with TSG compared to Wan2.2
5B’s 25% with CFG-only sampling, despite having fewer parameters. We see this pattern more broadly: smaller models
with TSG report higher or comparable satisfaction accuracies to larger models with CFG only. On the strictly-timed
prompts from the TempoControl benchmark, TSG improves temporal accuracy over CFG-only by 17.6–22.0 percentage
points across splits, and exceeds full TempoControl by 2.97 and 0.58 percentage points on the one-object and two-object
splits, respectively (Table 2).
5.3
RQ2. What are the computational and quality costs of TSG?
Result 2: TSG incurs little to no memory overhead but increases time taken for generation. TSG matches the
CFG-only sampling in terms of the peak GPU memory allocated to the model. This is expected as attention steering
detaches the computational graph and hence does not need to store gradients with respect to model weights. TSG uses
30.52 GiB versus 79.43 GiB for full TempoControl, a reduction of approximately 62%, as TempoControl optimizes
video latents requiring a gradient computation graph through the entire video transformer (Table 2).
The additional specification-guided forward pass through the transformer incurs a latency cost with TSG. This scales
with the size of the model as expected, with TSG taking around 1 extra minute with Wan2.1 1.3B to 11 extra minutes
with Wan2.2 A14B (Table 1). Full TempoControl takes approximately 4.9× and 3.6× as long as TSG on the one-object
and two-object splits, respectively (Table 2). Limiting TempoControl to one optimization round brings its latency close
to TSG’s but retains approximately 79.4 GiB peak memory; TSG improves temporal accuracy over this variant by
20.1% and 27.5% relative to its one-object and two-object scores.
Result 3: TSG modestly impacts the visual quality of the generated videos. TSG lowers imaging quality by up to

Analysis and Discussion
Category-wise results and qualitative examples. Figure 4 (left) shows category-wise satisfaction accuracies, averaged
across five models, with whiskers indicating the minimum and maximum. TSG improves mean accuracy across all
11 categories by approximately 2–43 percentage points. The largest gains occur for alternative final states (23% to
66%), two-state transitions (35% to 71%), and reversible state returns (21% to 43%). Performance nevertheless varies
substantially across models: with TSG, accuracy ranges from 20% to 75% for reversible state returns and from 10%
to 80% for transitions with a persistent entity. Handover with overlap, triggered responses, and three ordered entries
remain challenging, with mean accuracies of approximately 9%, 17%, and 12%, respectively, and gains of only about
2 percentage points. These results show that TSG is particularly effective for state-transition requirements, while
coordinating multiple entity appearances, disappearances, and dependencies remains challenging.

Related work
Test-time control for video generation. Methods for controllable video generation differ primarily in terms of the
control surface and the time of control (during or post sampling). Control surfaces include spatiotemporal scene
layouts (Lian et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2026), and motion transfer using reference videos (Ling et al., 2024), which
are complementary to TSG’s focus on temporal constraints over frame-level visual concepts. Closest to our work,
TempoControl (Schiber et al., 2026) aligns concept attention traces with prescribed binary temporal masks by repeatedly
optimizing video latents during sampling. TSG offers control over more expressive temporal constraints and outperforms
TempoControl on strict-timed traces with lower computational overhead (Table 2). Post-sampling temporal control
methods include NeuS-E (Choi et al., 2025) that uses Vision Language Models (VLM) to detect visual concepts and
feedback from a Temporal Logic Model Checker to edit faulty video frames. TSG and NeuS-E offer tradeoffs as
TSG does not require access to a VLM or Image Editing model, while NeuS-E can be applied to black-box models.
MinT (Wu et al., 2025) learns temporal control by fine-tuning a video diffusion transformer on temporally grounded
data. TSG operates at test-time with frozen parameters and no additional training data.
Attention steering for text, image, and video generation. Attention provides both a signal of concept grounding and
a mechanism for controlling generation of text (Zhang et al., 2024; Guardieiro et al., 2025), and images (Hertz et al.,
2022; Chefer et al., 2023). VideoTetris (Tian et al., 2024) composes attention maps across space and time to control
spatial positions of concepts. TSG supports richer temporal compositions beyond transitions but does not directly
support control of spatial positions.

Benchmarks for video generation. VBench (Huang et al., 2024) assesses visual quality, temporal consistency, and

8
Conclusion and Future Work
We present Temporal Specification Guidance (TSG), a training-free framework that turns temporal specifications into
executable objectives for guiding video generation during sampling. We instantiate TSG with attention-derived concept
traces, differentiable Metric Temporal Logic scores, and attention interventions that avoid backpropagation through the
video transformer. This instantiation improves specification adherence across five models on a 220-prompt benchmark
spanning 11 categories and achieves comparable temporal accuracy to TempoControl at lower computational cost.
Future work should explore traces of concept evidence beyond video-text attention maps, along with other scoring
functions. TSG is limited to the latent temporal resolution of models and extending it to finer-grained temporal
constraints is an interesting direction, along with identifying richer specification languages to express temporal
requirements.

starting from frame 1 ≤t ≤T can be computed deterministically using the standard Boolean semantics for MTLf as

• y(v, ⊤, t) = 1 and y(v, ⊥, t) = 0.
• y(v, c, t) = y(vt, c).
• y(v, ¬c, t) = 1 iff y(v, c, t) = 0.
• y(v, φ1 ∧φ2, t) = 1 iff y(v, φ1, t) = 1 and y(v, φ2, t) = 1.

Sliding-window aggregation. Algorithm 2 computes all window aggregates for an associative operation with a
constant-size summary. Its queue is implemented with a front stack and a back stack. Every stack entry stores its value
and a cached aggregate. The front aggregate GF runs from the top to the bottom of the front stack; the back aggregate
GB runs from the bottom to the top of the back stack. An empty stack has aggregate e. The operations are:
• PushBack(x): push (x, GB ◦x) onto the back stack.
• PopFront(): if the front stack is empty, transfer all values from the back stack, popping each value x and
pushing (x, x ◦GF ) onto the front stack. Then pop the front stack.
• Aggregate(): return GF ◦GB.
The order of combination is essential because the Until operation is not commutative. Each value enters the back stack
once, transfers to the front stack at most once, and leaves the queue at most once. Push and aggregate operations have
constant cost; removal has amortized constant cost. No inverse operation is needed, so zero child scores and empty
windows are handled directly.

Numerical evaluation. The mathematical definition admits exact zeros and ones. For numerical gradients, literal inputs
can be clipped to [ϵLS, 1 −ϵLS], separately from the calibration in Equation 2. Composite scores are not clipped to
one, and negation is applied only to atoms. Products are computed as sums of logarithms, and sums as log-sum-exp
reductions. Empty products are one and empty sums are zero: Always on an empty window has log score zero, while
Eventually and Until have log score −∞. On the domain where Qφ[1] > 0, the log score is differentiable for literal
inputs strictly between zero and one. Structurally zero branches, including log-add-exp with two constant −∞inputs,
should be handled explicitly when differentiating. If the entire score is identically zero, for example because a required
witness window is empty, its logarithm provides no finite gradient; the specification must be checked for feasibility on
the chosen grid, and that objective should not produce a steering update.

The TSG algorithm
Algorithm 3 presents TSG with online attention steering. The guided steps are the first Ntsg sampling updates, i.e.,
K = {N −Ntsg + 1, . . . , N}. The loop over selected attention layers takes place within a single additional forward
pass: normal transformer operations proceed between interventions, and later layers use the representations produced
by earlier steered layers. Differentiation is enabled only for the detached traces, their calibration, and the specification
score.

The predicate p jointly encodes the required presence and absence of visual concepts. Explicit persistence requirements
are evaluated over their stated intervals rather than reduced to a 0.5-second witness. We use the same duration threshold
across the evaluated 5–6-second videos to require sustained evidence beyond an isolated frame while allowing flexibility
in transition timing.
Interpretation of the criterion. We evaluate whether each required phase occurs within its designated interval and persists
for at least 0.5 seconds. We intentionally leave the intervening transitions unconstrained: intermediate configurations
may not map unambiguously to the discrete propositions. For example, a partially open door may be labeled either open
or closed while it moves between states. Requiring a sustained witness for each phase establishes that the requested
states occur in the specified temporal order without prescribing the duration or frame-by-frame labeling of the transition
between them. This criterion does not require each phase to occupy its entire interval or restrict behavior outside the
witness runs, except where the specification imposes explicit persistence or absence constraints.

E.3
TempoControl comparison
We use all 80 one-object and 82 two-object prompts from the official TempoControl benchmark repository. One-object
prompts request an initially absent object that appears during the second, third, fourth, or last second and remains
visible, with 20 prompts for each onset. Two-object prompts require a static object throughout, with another object
appearing in the second half. Binary masks are derived from the requested timing and the actual video frame rate and
duration, rather than reusing the original 21-bin masks at a different duration. For our five-second Wan clips, the four
one-object onsets occur at sampled indices 4, 8, 12, and 16, respectively; the object must be absent before its onset and
present from that index through index 20. Two-object masks require absence of the entering object at indices 0–9 and
presence at indices 10–20, together with the static object throughout.
To obtain TSG specifications, we convert each maximal constant segment of a binary mask into an always requirement
for the proposition or its negation and conjoin the segment requirements. The static-object requirement is a global
always formula. The runtime steering grid with Wan2.1 1.3B has 21 latent frames. All four comparison methods, i.e.,
CFG-only, TempoControl, one-round TempoControl, and TSG, use Wan 2.1 at 480 × 832, 81 frames, 16 FPS, 50 steps,
CFG 6, and flow shift 3. We use seed 42 and replay the initial latent tensor saved by the full TempoControl run for each
prompt.
Our TempoControl port preserves the released optimization order using the Diffusers backbone: latent optimization
follows the scheduler update and queries attention at the preceding timestep. It optimizes the latent with AdamW (weight
decay 0.01) at steps 0–10, with learning rate 5 × 10−4 for one-object and 10−3 for two-object prompts. The objective
uses energy weight 0.3 and entropy weight 10, with query chunks of 512 positions and activation checkpointing for
the attention forward pass. The full variant allows up to ten optimization updates per guided step, following official
recommendations in the repository. Early stopping checks whether the first controlled concept’s Pearson correlation
with its target mask exceeds 0.9, before applying the next update. The one-round variant changes only the maximum
update count to one; it retains this early-stopping check and the same 11 guided steps.
E.4
Ablation protocol and parameter selection
The guidance-strength ablations in Section 6 compare Wan 2.1 and Wan 2.2 TI2V on the same 25 samples. Every
configuration replays its model’s saved base latent tensor for each example. We retain the model-specific settings in
Table 6, all attention layers and heads, the first 15 guided steps, step size 1, and the calibration and clipping settings
above. The additional strengths are ρ ∈{2, 3, 4, 5}, compared with the existing base and ρ = 1 runs. Only ρ changes
within this sweep.
The CFG interaction ablations also use the same base settings but modify the sampling as required. For the double
strength CFG runs, we double the CFG strengths in Table 6 for Wan2.1 and Wan2.2 5B. For TSG-only, we run TSG
for all 50 steps and replace the conditional directions in CFG’s update with the TSG predicted specification-guided
direction (CFG’s guidance scale is used).
E.5
Compute and measurement protocol
Wan 2.1, Wan 2.2 TI2V, both CogVideoX models, and the TempoControl comparison are run on a cluster with NVIDIA
B200 GPUs. The Wan 2.2 A14B model is run on a cluster with NVIDIA H200s. The models use bfloat16 except
CogVideoX 2B, which uses float16; the VAE uses float32. The recorded runtime is PyTorch 2.9.1 with CUDA 12.8,
Diffusers 0.36.0, and Transformers 4.57.3. CogVideoX enables VAE tiling and slicing. The attention implementation
uses PyTorch scaled-dot-product attention for ordinary computation and explicit attention-score computation where
needed for capture and steering; the exact kernel selected by the runtime is not recorded in the per-video profiles.
Timing uses a monotonic clock with CUDA synchronization at measurement boundaries. Before each measured video,
the profiler empties the CUDA allocator cache and resets peak-memory statistics after model loading. There is no
separate discarded warm-up video in this measurement procedure. Generation latency includes initial-noise preparation
or replay, prompt encoding, denoising and guidance, and VAE decoding. It excludes model loading, video export,
evaluation, and separately timed diagnostic artifacts. Diagnostic memory allocations can still contribute to the recorded
peak. When reported as mean ± standard deviation, the standard deviation is the sample standard deviation across
videos, not a confidence interval or variation over repeated seeds. Peak memory is the maximum of the per-video

E.6
Evaluation metrics
Frame sampling and specification accuracy. We evaluate 21 uniformly spaced decoded frames for every model,
including the first and last frame. For a video with F frames, sampled index i is ⌊i(F −1)/20⌋, for i = 0, . . . , 20. We
extract binary concept traces using Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. Specification satisfaction accuracy (Sat
Acc.) evaluates the prompt-derived specification under the Boolean semantics from Appendix A.
Visual quality. We use the MUSIQ-SPAQ model underlying VBench’s imaging-quality dimension (Huang et al., 2024).
It scores the same sampled frames used for specification evaluation, resizing the longer side to at most 512 pixels
according to the VBench preprocessing. We average over frames within each video and then over videos. The table
reports raw MUSIQ units, with larger values indicating better perceptual frame quality. Latency and peak GPU memory
follow the measurement definitions above.
Evaluator configuration. The Qwen evaluator makes one request per sampled frame, asking for all relevant propositions
together. The frame is converted to a uint8 PIL image and passed through the model’s image processor and chat
template. The application sends one user message containing the image and the verification prompt. Decoding is greedy,
with at most 256 new tokens and one frame per request. The evaluator sees proposition definitions but not the requested
temporal schedule or neighboring frames.
The fixed user-prompt preamble is reproduced below; the schema-specific field definitions are then appended from each
example’s proposition contexts.
Inspect only the supplied frame. Report what is visibly supported, not what ought to happen. The scene may be
stylized or synthetic. Do not infer missing objects or a desired temporal phase. Return only one JSON object
with exactly the keys described below, without explanations.
Object-presence fields: “present” means any recognizable visible part, even at the edge, small, or partially
occluded. “absent” means confidently not visible. “unclear” means the evidence cannot establish presence or
absence; do not equate uncertainty with absence.
State fields: return a JSON list of the exact state names visibly supported. Normally one state applies. If multiple
states visibly coexist, list all of them; do not force a valid phase. Return [] only when you can confidently
establish that none applies. Return [“unclear”] if the state cannot be determined, the transition is ambiguous, or
the relevant entity is not recognizable. Do not combine “unclear” with other labels. Use the same visible entity
when distinguishing its states. A closed door is shut; an open or ajar door is not closed. If the opening cannot be
seen clearly, use unclear. Standing and sitting refer to the actual pose; an ambiguous intermediate pose is unclear.
For traffic lights, judge the illuminated signal, not an unlit colored lens.
For example, a pose-transition request appends a state field listing the allowed compound state labels and their defi-
nitions. Entity-presence fields instead allow present, absent, or unclear. City-lighting alternatives additionally
receive explicit daytime, sunset, and nighttime definitions.
Parsing accepts a JSON object, optionally within a JSON code fence, and normalizes case, whitespace, underscores,
and Unicode dash separators in labels. Multiple supported states may be returned simultaneously; we do not force the
answer to match a valid temporal phase. Explicit unclear labels and omitted fields map to false in the binary trace.
Unknown fields are ignored; invalid known fields, unknown state labels, and inconsistent aliases raise protocol errors.
The current evaluator allows two schema-correction retries on the same frame and preserves the invalid responses;
exhausted retries leave an error record rather than a fabricated verdict. Earlier saved evaluations without retries remain
identified by their raw-response records and source snapshots.
Across all five models, Qwen returned “unclear” labels for at least one proposition in 433 of 46,200 individual frames
(0.94%). We assessed sensitivity to these labels using conservative and optimistic treatments across all five models.
Conservatively, unclear labels support neither positive nor negative literals; no currently passing video fails under this
treatment, leaving reported accuracies unchanged. Optimistically, unclear labels may take Boolean values that satisfy
the specification. With models weighted equally, this increases CFG accuracy by 0.73 percentage points and TSG
accuracy by 1.36 percentage points.
TempoControl object and action metrics. Object evaluation uses the YOLOv10x detector with Ultralytics 8.4.152 on
21 sampled frames. The detector is invoked with its prediction defaults. Detected class names and requested labels are
compared literally after lowercasing.
