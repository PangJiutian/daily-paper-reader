# Generative Adversarial Learning from Deterministic Processes 

# Joris C. Kühl ∗1 and Hanno Gottschalk †11Institute of Mathematics, Technical University of Berlin, Germany 

# May 19, 2026 

Abstract. Physical AI is being successfully applied to data which does not follow the traditional paradigm of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples. In fact, physical AI is often trained on data which is not random at all, and is instead derived from chaotic dynamical systems like turbulence. We aim to explain the empirical success of these methods using the example of generative adversarial networks (GANs), whose statistical learning theory under the i.i.d. assumption is generally well understood. We prove that it is possible, using an infinite-dimensional model of generative adversarial learning (GAL), to learn the invariant distribution of a sufficiently chaotic dynamical system from a single deterministically evolving time series of its states or measurements thereof, and give explicit rates for the convergence to the solution in terms of the Jensen-Shannon divergence. 

Keywords: Ergodic Dynamical Systems • Generative Adversarial Learning • Statistical Learning Theory • Concentration Inequalities 

# 1 Introduction 

Physical artificial intelligence is a new major trend in machine learning. In particular, many authors propose to replace traditional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solvers by neural-network-based learning methods. Prominent examples are physics informed neural networks (PINN) [39], operator learning methods like DeepONet [32], and Fourier neural operators (FNO) [30]. Another line of research, starting with [7, 8, 16, 19, 25, 26, 27, 46], suggests the use of generative learning for the fast generation of solutions to turbulent fluid dynamical systems [1, 14, 28, 37, 40, 42]. This line of work provides extensive numerical evidence for successful learning of chaotic dynamical systems, both in terms of visual similarity and physics-based statistical evaluation. However, there is little to no explanation given as to why these approaches achieve the observed level of performance. While there exists a substantial body of work dealing with the statistical learning theory of generative models, we cannot directly apply these works to the generative learning of chaotic dynamical systems. In particular, the predominant paradigm of statistical learning theory is learning from independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data samples [41]. But learn-ing from the observation of a single trajectory of a deterministic system, as is often done in physical AI approaches, is certainly far from the i.i.d. assumption. One could resort to learn-ing from multiple trajectories with i.i.d. initial conditions, but this is generally avoided due to considerable burn-in times until CFD solutions develop a statistically stationary turbulent state. In [16], the authors propose replacing the i.i.d. assumption by ergodicity , which captures an important statistical property of chaotic dynamical systems. The authors also sketch a mathematical argument to support this approach to learning from physical systems. But no detailed mathematical argument is provided and, in particular, no rates of convergence in the observation time are given. The goal of this article is to establish a framework in which statistical learning from chaotic but deterministic data is mathematically justified, and to provide rates of convergence in analogy to standard results of statistical learning theory. Though it is not specific to them, we demonstrate our approach on the example of generative adversarial networks (GANs). GANs were first introduced in [22]. They have since shown remarkable potential, particularly for image generation. Several variants have been developed, including Conditional GAN [36], Wasserstein GAN [2], StyleGAN [24] and more. However, we focus on the original setting of [22], sometimes referred to as “vanilla GAN”. The statistical learning theory of GANs was studied, for instance, in [6], which links them to the Jensen-Shannon divergence and provides a first proof of consistency. Later papers on the topic include [4, 31, 38]. These papers succeed in proving quantitative rates of convergence in various finite- and infinite-dimensional settings, always under the i.i.d. assumption, which enables the use of the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem and other classical results of probability theory. One possible weaker assumption is the aforementioned ergodicity, which is also used in [16]. A measure-preserving dynamical system is ergodic if and only if any integrable observable of its states satisfies the strong law of large numbers in the following sense: Let (M, A) be a measurable space and (Xn)n∈N the M -valued sequence of states of the system, initialized according to its invariant distribution μ = P ◦ X−11 . # 2 Generative Adversarial Learning 

Given a sequence of training data samples (Yi)i∈N following some distribution μ ∈ M +1 (Rd), the goal of generative learning is to be able to generate new samples (Xi)i∈N following the same or a similar distribution. In other words, to sample from a learned representation of μ.In generative adversarial learning (GAL), we are trying to learn a transport map ϕ (the gen-erator ) from a noise measure ν to an approximation ϕ∗ν = ν ◦ ϕ−1 of μ. The key feature that makes the learning adversarial is that, instead of scoring the generator’s output using a difficult to compute negative log-likelihood loss, one introduces a new map ξ (the discriminator ), which is trained along with the generator in a minimax game, see Figure 1.

# 4 A Chaotic Deterministic Data Model 

## 4.1 Ergodic Dynamical Systems 

The motivation for this paper is learning the invariant distribution of a chaotic dynamical system from a single time series, as in [16]. This requires said dynamical system to satisfy conditions that can substitute for the law of large numbers and related results of the classical i.i.d. setting. A simple class of such systems turns out to be that of ergodic dynamics. The definitions below follow [18, Chapters 1 & 5]. 

Definition 4.1 (dynamical system; measure-preserving system) .

A dynamical system (M, A, T ) consists of a measurable space (M, A) together with a measurable map T : M → M called the dynamics .A measure-preserving system (M, A, μ, T ) is a dynamical system (M, A, T ) endowed with a probability measure μ ∈ M +1 (M, A) such that μ = μ ◦ T −1. We call μ an invariant measure 

for (M, A, T ).

Definition 4.2 (invariant σ-algebra; ergodicity) .

Let (M, A, μ, T ) be a measure-preserving system. The invariant σ-algebra is 

IT = {A ∈ A | A = T −1(A)}.

We call the system ergodic if IT is μ-trivial, i.e., μ(A) ∈ { 0, 1} for all A ∈ I T .In this case we also call T an ergodic transformation on (M, A, μ ).We now fix a probability space (Ω , F, P). Let any random variable or stochastic process whose domain is not explicitly specified be defined on this space. If X = ( Xn)n∈N is a stochastic process taking values in a measurable space (M, A), we refer to 

P ◦ X−1 ∈ M +1 (M N, A⊗N) as the (joint) distribution of the process X.9Definition 4.3 (ergodic process) .

Let (M, A) be a measurable space and X = ( Xn)n∈N an M -valued stochastic process. X is called an ergodic process if the left-shift 

τ : M N → M N, (xn)n∈N 7 → (xn+1 )n∈N

is an ergodic transformation on (M N, A⊗N, P ◦ X−1).

Remark 4.4 (ergodic processes of observables) .

Ergodic processes can be defined in a very natural manner on ergodic systems. Let (M, A, μ, T )

be an ergodic system, ( fM , eA) a measurable space and g : M → fM a measurable observable of the system. We define the fM -valued process X = ( Xn)n∈N of values of the observable g under repeated application of T by Xn = g ◦ T n−1. Then X is an ergodic process. As a special case of the above, we obtain what we refer to as the canonical process of the system (M, A, μ, T ). Let g = Id M , then we have X1(x) = x and Xn+1 (x) = T (Xn), i.e., the (deterministic) process of states of the system under repeated application of T . ⋄

In [4], the authors use the uniform law of large numbers to prove that εgen (n) → 0 almost surely as n → ∞ . At this point, if we simply required (Yi)i∈N to be an ergodic process, we could already prove consistency along a similar line, using Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem together with the Lipschitz continuity of Proposition 3.5. See [16] for a sketch of such an argument. To prove not just consistency but quantitative rates of convergence, however, we require stronger results than the ergodic theorem. The proof in [4] utilizes McDiarmid’s inequality (and its special case, Hoeffding’s inequality), but there is no analogous statement for general ergodic systems. While there are several narrower classes of dynamical systems that satisfy such ex-ponential concentration inequalities – some others, we mention in Section 6 – we focus here on one such class to exemplify our approach. 

## 4.2 Young Towers 

For this section, let (M, g ) be a smooth Riemannian manifold, d the distance on it, A =

B(M ) the Borel-σ-algebra and μ the Riemannian volume on M . Such Riemannian manifolds include Rn, the (n − 1) -dimensional unit sphere Sn−1 in Rn, and the n-torus Tn = Rn/Zn

[29]. We consider dynamical systems (M, A, T ), where T is either a Ck-diffeomorphism with 

k ≥ 2 or a C1,α -diffeomorphism for some α ∈ (0 , 1] . We collectively refer to either as a C1+ ε-diffeomorphism . Since the σ-algebra is always the Borel-σ-algebra, we usually omit A and simply speak of dynamical systems (M, T ) or measure-preserving systems (M, μ, T ).

Young towers can be seen as a broad generalization of Axiom A systems and their symbolic dynamics (see e.g. [9, Chapter 3] for an introduction to Axiom A and Anosov systems) and were first introduced by Lai-Sang Young in [48]. To be precise, a uniform Young tower is a dynamical system (∆ , bT ) that extends the underlying system (M, T ) and which can be more easily studied using methods of symbolic dynamics. Properties like mixing or ergodicity are proven for (∆ , bT ), and from there transfer to (M, T ).10 The first question, then, must be under what conditions it is possible to construct a uniform Young tower over the system (M, T ). The exact conditions are listed in Appendix A and only briefly summarized in this section. 

The Underlying System: Hyperbolic Product Structure and Return Times 

We assume there exists Λ ⊆ M with a so-called hyperbolic product structure . This means that, though Λ is not necessarily a hyperbolic set and not even T -invariant, T exhibits hyperbolic behavior on Λ with clearly defined stable and unstable directions in every point. For a more detailed account, see Definition A.3. Let (Λ i)i∈I be a partition of Λ, where either I = {1, . . . , n } for some n ∈ N or I = N. Moreover, for each i ∈ I let Ri ∈ N be the return time of Λi, that is, the first time n ∈ N where T n(Λ i) ⊆ Λ.More precisely, T Ri (Λ i) must be a u-subset of Λ, i.e., it spans Λ fully in the unstable direction, as defined by the hyperbolic product structure. See Definition A.4 for more details. Crucially, Ri is not necessarily the first return time for each individual x ∈ Λi, but rather the first simultaneous return time for the entirety of Λi.For any individual x ∈ Λi, its orbit up to time Ri − 1 may or may not be in Λ. By time Ri,it will land in Λ, though possibly in a different partition element, i.e., x′ = T Ri (x) ∈ Λj . From there, the cycle begins anew only with Rj instead of Ri.

Remark 5.1 (on the applicability of Chazottes-Gouëzel) .

Theorem B.9 is stated for separately Lipschitz observables of the canonical process ( eYi)i∈N.However, any separately Lipschitz observable K of the observable process (Yi)i∈N can be re-stated as the separately Lipschitz observable eK = K ◦ (g, . . . , g ) of the canonical process, with Lipschitz constants eLi = Li · L. 24 6 Conclusions and Outlook 

In this work, we have proven that the traditional assumption of independence is not critical to the convergence of generative adversarial learning, explaining – in part – the empirical success of GANs trained on time series of chaotic dynamical systems, and opening the way for a better theoretical understanding of recent developments in the field of physical AI. Most importantly, we were able to replace Hoeffding’s / McDiarmid’s inequality with Chazottes and Gouëzel’s concentration inequality B.9 to obtain the same O(n− 12 ) (up to a polylogarithm) rate of almost sure convergence. To adapt the Dudley estimates in [4], we also proved to our knowledge novel metric entropy bounds for the embedding of spaces of Hölder differentiable functions into C1

(as opposed to C). The results in this paper concern generative adversarial learning for systems that admit an aperiodic uniform Young tower with exponential tails, but the methodology is largely not specific to this setting. GAL can easily be replaced by traditional maximum likelihood-based ERM learning, so long as McDiarmid’s inequality is used to prove convergence in the i.i.d. case. Likewise, any ergodic system that satisfies an appropriate exponential concentration inequality can be considered in place of Young towers. Geometrically ergodic Markov chains, for example, satisfy a type of McDiarmid’s inequality [13, 15, 23]. One may also consider continuous time dynamical systems as in [16]. In this case, it might be possible to access a continuous time analog of McDiarmid’s inequality through the exponential decay of correlations property discussed, for example, in [20]. Another interesting direction for future research is replacing the infinite-dimensional hypothesis spaces of [4] by spaces of neural networks. [5] proves that ReQU networks approximate Hölder differentiable functions in Ck norm. In this case, the model error is once again in consideration and must be balanced with the generalization error. To preserve the diffeomorphism of the generators, NeuralODE models, where the actual generator is the flow endpoint associated to a vector field modeled by the neural network, seem promising. Their use for the highly related (and aforementioned) problem of maximum likelihood-based ERM learning has been studied, e.g., in [17, 34]. Finally, on the topic of Young towers, it is possible to sidestep the observables of Remark 5.2 fully by learning directly on the torus. This requires generators that are diffeomorphisms on the torus – the Rosenblatt transformation is not, due to its lack of periodicity. Implementing such functions on the torus itself via neural networks can also lead to learning the dynamics directly.
